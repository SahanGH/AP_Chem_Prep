  1. Work all four before checking anything.
  2. Check against the gray check: line. All four right on the first try earns the tick on the tracker (last page).
  3. Any misses: re-read the worked example, then redo the missed ones from a blank page.
The one habit that decides this chapter: track the sign. Every quantity here is signed, and the sign always answers the same question — did the system gain energy or lose it? Gained is positive, lost is negative. A correct magnitude with the wrong sign earns no marks and describes the opposite of what happened.

Ladder 1 • System, surroundings, and the first law

ZUM §6.1

Draw a boundary. Inside is the system (the reaction); everything outside is the surroundings. Energy crosses that boundary two ways: \[ \boxed{\;\Delta E = q + w\;} \qquad w = -P\,\Delta V \]

Signs, from the system's point of view — always:

QuantityPositive whenNegative when
\(q\)heat flows in (endothermic)heat flows out (exothermic)
\(w\)surroundings compress the systemthe system expands and does work

The minus sign in \(w = -P\Delta V\) exists so that an expanding gas (\(\Delta V > 0\)) reports negative work: it spent energy pushing the atmosphere back.

I do: a gas that absorbs heat and expands

A gas absorbs 575 J of heat and expands from 1.20 L to 3.40 L against a constant external pressure of 1.00 atm. Find \(w\) and \(\Delta E\).

Work — expansion, so expect a negative value: \[ w = -P\,\Delta V = -(1.00)(3.40 - 1.20) = -2.20\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm} \] Convert, because L·atm is not a joule: \[ -2.20 \times 101.3 = -223\,\mathrm{J} \] First law: \[ \Delta E = q + w = 575 + (-223) = \mathbf{+352~J} \] Read the answer back: the gas took in 575 J, spent 223 J of it pushing the atmosphere aside, and kept the remaining 352 J as internal energy. The bookkeeping is the physics.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. A system releases 125 J of heat and has 75 J of work done on it. Find \(\Delta E\).
  2. A gas is compressed from 5.00 L to 2.00 L at 2.00 atm. Find \(w\) in L·atm and in joules.
    \(+6.00\) L atm \(=\) +608 J
  3. A reaction absorbs 40. kJ of heat while doing 12 kJ of work on the surroundings. Find \(\Delta E\).
  4. For a reaction run in a rigid sealed vessel, what is \(w\), and what does \(\Delta E\) then equal?
    \(w = 0\), so \(\Delta E = q\)

(a) -50. J   (b) \(+6.00\) L atm \(=\) +608 J   (c) +28 kJ   (d) \(w = 0\), so \(\Delta E = q\)

(a) \(q = -125\) (out), \(w = +75\) (done on the system): \(\Delta E = -125 + 75 = \mathbf{-50.}\) J. (b) Compression means \(\Delta V = -3.00\) L, so \(w = -(2.00)(-3.00) = \mathbf{+6.00}\) L atm \(\times\, 101.3 = \mathbf{+608}\) J — positive, because work was done on the gas. (c) \(q = +40.\), \(w = -12\): \(\Delta E = \mathbf{+28}\) kJ. (d) Rigid means \(\Delta V = 0\), so \(w = -P(0) = \mathbf{0}\) and \(\Delta E = q\). This is exactly what a bomb calorimeter measures.

Ladder 2 • Enthalpy, and why chemists use it

ZUM §6.2

At constant pressure — a beaker open to the room, which is nearly every reaction you will run — the heat is given its own name: \[ \boxed{\;\Delta H = q_P\;} \] That is the whole reason \(H\) exists: it is the energy change you can measure with a thermometer, without tracking the work of expansion separately.

\(\Delta H < 0\)\(\Delta H > 0\)
Nameexothermicendothermic
Heatreleased to surroundingsabsorbed from surroundings
Surroundings feelwarmercolder
Products' enthalpylower than reactantshigher than reactants
\(\Delta H\) is an extensive quantity: double the amounts and you double the enthalpy change. So a thermochemical equation's \(\Delta H\) belongs to exactly the coefficients written.
I do: scaling and reversing a thermochemical equation

Given \[ \ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) -> 2SO3(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -198\,\mathrm{kJ} \]

(a) Heat released when 1.00 mol of \(\ce{SO2}\) reacts. The \(-198\) kJ belongs to two moles of \(\ce{SO2}\): \[ \frac{-198}{2} = \mathbf{-99.0~kJ} \] (b) \(\Delta H\) for the reverse reaction. Reversing flips the sign: \[ \ce{2SO3(g) -> 2SO2(g) + O2(g)} \qquad \Delta H = \mathbf{+198~kJ} \] (c) \(\Delta H\) when 32.0 g of \(\ce{SO2}\) (\(M = 64.07\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)) reacts. \[ n = \frac{32.0}{64.07} = 0.4995\,\mathrm{mol} \;\Rightarrow\; 0.4995 \times (-99.0) = \mathbf{-49.4~kJ} \] Carry the unrounded moles into the multiplication: rounding to 0.500 first would give \(-49.5\), and that last digit is the one being asked for.

Every one of these is the same idea: \(\Delta H\) scales with amount and flips with direction.

Ladder 3 • Specific heat

ZUM §6.2 \[ \boxed{\;q = m\,c\,\Delta T\;} \qquad \Delta T = T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}} \] \(c\) is the specific heat capacity — the energy needed to raise one gram by one degree. Water's is unusually large, 4.18 J/g/°C, which is why lakes moderate climate and why water is the coolant of choice.

Because \(\Delta T\) is a difference, a change of 1 °C equals a change of 1 K — so specific-heat problems are the one place in this course where you may leave temperatures in °C.

I do: heating a metal, then finding its identity
(a) How much heat raises 125 g of copper (\(c = 0.385\,\mathrm{J/g/{}^\circ C}\)) from 22.0 °C to 88.5 °C? \[ q = (125)(0.385)(88.5 - 22.0) = (125)(0.385)(66.5) = \mathbf{3200~J} = 3.20\,\mathrm{kJ} \] (b) A 45.0 g sample of an unknown metal absorbs 326 J while warming from 25.0 °C to 58.0 °C. Find \(c\), and identify the metal. \[ c = \frac{q}{m\,\Delta T} = \frac{326}{(45.0)(33.0)} = \mathbf{0.220~J/g\cdot^\circ C} \] Compare: \(\ce{Ag}\) 0.235, \(\ce{Cu}\) 0.385, \(\ce{Fe}\) 0.449. The value points to silver — 0.220 against 0.235 is ordinary experimental error, while the alternatives are far off.

  3. Specific heat of a 80.0 g metal that loses 1250 J while cooling from 95.0 °C to 35.0 °C:
  4. Equal masses of water and copper absorb the same heat. Which warms more, and why?
    copper — its specific heat is far smaller

(a) 57.5 kJ   (b) 22.0 °C   (c) 0.260 J/g/°C   (d) copper — its specific heat is far smaller

(a) \(q = (250.)(4.18)(55.0) = 57{,}475\) J \(= \mathbf{57.5}\) kJ. (b) \(\Delta T = 2500/[(150.)(4.18)] = 3.99\); \(T_f = 18.0 + 4.0 = \mathbf{22.0}\) °C. (c) \(c = 1250/[(80.0)(60.0)] = \mathbf{0.260}\) J/g °C. (d) Copper: with \(\Delta T = q/mc\) and the same \(q\) and \(m\), the smaller \(c\) gives the bigger \(\Delta T\) — copper's 0.385 against water's 4.18, so copper warms about eleven times as much.

Ladder 4 • Calorimetry

ZUM §6.2

A calorimeter measures the surroundings and infers the system: \[ q_{\text{reaction}} = -q_{\text{solution}} = -m\,c\,\Delta T \] That minus sign is the entire technique. The thermometer sits in the water, so a temperature rise means the water gained heat, which means the reaction lost it.

AP trap

Two habits that protect the answer. Use the mass of the solution, not of the solute — in a coffee-cup calorimeter the water is what warms. And convert to kJ per mole at the end: \(q\) is the heat for the sample you used, while \(\Delta H\) is quoted per mole.

I do: a coffee-cup neutralization

50.0 mL of 1.00 M \(\ce{HCl}\) is mixed with 50.0 mL of 1.00 M \(\ce{NaOH}\), both at 21.0 °C. The temperature rises to 27.7 °C. Take the solution's density as 1.00 g/mL and \(c = 4.18\). Find \(\Delta H\) per mole of water formed.

Heat gained by the solution — total mass 100.0 g: \[ q_{\text{soln}} = (100.0)(4.18)(6.7) = 2801\,\mathrm{J} \] Flip the sign for the reaction: \(q_{\text{rxn}} = -2801\,\mathrm{J} = -2.80\,\mathrm{kJ}\) Moles of water formed: \((0.0500)(1.00) = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}\) — 1:1, and neither reagent is in excess. Per mole: \[ \Delta H = \frac{-2.80}{0.0500} = \mathbf{-56.0~kJ/mol} \] The accepted value for strong acid \(+\) strong base is about \(-57.3\) kJ/mol, so the experiment is good to about 2%.

  2. Is that process endothermic or exothermic?
    endothermic
  3. A 100.0 g water sample in a calorimeter rises 4.50 °C when 0.0250 mol of a reagent reacts. Find \(\Delta H\) in kJ/mol.
  4. Why is the mass in these calculations the mass of the solution rather than of the reacting solute?
    the solution is the surroundings the thermometer reads

(a) +2.30 kJ   (b) endothermic   (c) -75.2 kJ/mol   (d) the solution is the surroundings the thermometer reads

(a) Solution mass \(= 100.0\) g; \(q_{\text{soln}} = (100.0)(4.18)(-5.5) = -2299\) J, so the dissolving absorbed \(q = \mathbf{+2.30}\) kJ. (b) Endothermic — the temperature fell, so heat came out of the water and into the process. (c) \(q_{\text{soln}} = (100.0)(4.18)(4.50) = 1881\) J; \(q_{\text{rxn}} = -1.881\) kJ; \(\Delta H = -1.881/0.0250 = \mathbf{-75.2}\) kJ/mol. (d) The calorimeter infers the reaction's heat from the temperature of its surroundings, and the whole solution is what warms or cools and what the thermometer reads.

Ladder 5 • Hess's law

ZUM §6.3

Enthalpy is a state function: it depends only on the initial and final states, never on the route. So known reactions can be added like algebra to reach an unknown one.

  1. Reverse a step \(\Rightarrow\) flip the sign of \(\Delta H\).
  2. Multiply a step \(\Rightarrow\) multiply \(\Delta H\) by the same factor.
  3. Add the manipulated steps; species on opposite sides cancel.

Work backward from the target: put each target species on the side where it belongs, and the manipulations reveal themselves.

I do: forming nitrogen dioxide in two steps

Target: \(\ce{N2(g) + 2O2(g) -> 2NO2(g)}\), \(\Delta H = ?\)

Given: \begin{align*} \text{(i)}\quad & \ce{N2(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO(g)} & \Delta H_1 &= +180\,\mathrm{kJ}\\ \text{(ii)}\quad & \ce{2NO2(g) -> 2NO(g) + O2(g)} & \Delta H_2 &= +112\,\mathrm{kJ} \end{align*}

Step (i): \(\ce{N2}\) is already a reactant in the target — use as written, \(+180\) kJ. Step (ii): the target needs \(\ce{2NO2}\) as a product, but (ii) has it as a reactant — reverse it: \[ \ce{2NO(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO2(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -112\,\mathrm{kJ} \] Add, and watch \(\ce{2NO}\) cancel (product of the first, reactant of the second): \[ \ce{N2 + O2 + O2 -> 2NO2} \quad\text{i.e.}\quad \ce{N2 + 2O2 -> 2NO2} \] \[ \Delta H = 180 + (-112) = \mathbf{+68~kJ} \]

The \(\ce{2NO}\) cancelling is the check that the manipulations were right: if an intermediate survives, something is wrong.

(a) reverse it (\(\Delta H = +566.0\,\mathrm{kJ}\))   (b) -221.0 kJ   (c) -110.5 kJ   (d) \(\ce{CO2}\) cancels

(a) Reverse the second equation: \(\ce{2CO2 -> 2CO + O2}\), \(\Delta H = \mathbf{+566.0}\) kJ. (b) Double the first (\(2 \times -393.5 = -787.0\)) and add the reversed second (\(+566.0\)): \(\ce{2C + 2O2 -> 2CO2}\) plus \(\ce{2CO2 -> 2CO + O2}\) gives \(\ce{2C + O2 -> 2CO}\), \(\Delta H = -787.0 + 566.0 = \mathbf{-221.0}\) kJ. (c) Halve it: \(\mathbf{-110.5}\) kJ — which is \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) of \(\ce{CO}\). (d) \(\ce{CO2}\) — 2 mol appear as a product of the doubled first equation and as a reactant of the reversed second. An intermediate that fails to cancel means a step was manipulated wrongly.

Ladder 6 • Standard enthalpies of formation

ZUM §6.4 \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) is the enthalpy change forming one mole of a compound from its elements in their standard states. Two consequences do all the work:
  • An element in its standard state has \(\Delta H_f^\circ = 0\) — \(\ce{O2(g)}\), \(\ce{C(graphite)}\), \(\ce{Br2(l)}\), \(\ce{Na(s)}\). Nothing needs to happen to make it.
  • \[ \boxed{\;\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})\;} \]

Products minus reactants — in that order. Reversing it flips the sign of the answer, which is the commonest error on this calculation.

I do: burning ethanol
\[ \ce{C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l)} \] \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) (kJ/mol): \(\ce{C2H5OH(l)}\) \(-277.7\), \(\ce{CO2(g)}\) \(-393.5\), \(\ce{H2O(l)}\) \(-285.8\), \(\ce{O2(g)}\) \(0\). Products: \[ 2(-393.5) + 3(-285.8) = -787.0 + (-857.4) = -1644.4\,\mathrm{kJ} \] Reactants — and note the oxygen contributes nothing: \[ (-277.7) + 3(0) = -277.7\,\mathrm{kJ} \] Subtract: \[ \Delta H^\circ = -1644.4 - (-277.7) = \mathbf{-1366.7~kJ} \] Sense check: a combustion must be strongly exothermic, and it is. A positive answer here would mean the arithmetic ran reactants-minus-products.

Ladder 7 • Energy sources and fuel values

ZUM §6.5–6.6

Sections 6.5–6.6 are descriptive, and the CED does not assess them — read for context. The one quantitative idea worth carrying is fuel value: energy released per gram, which is what matters when a fuel must be carried.

I do: comparing two fuels by mass

Methane releases 890. kJ/mol (\(M = 16.04\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)); octane releases 5470 kJ/mol (\(M = 114.2\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)). Which is the better fuel per gram?

\[ \ce{CH4}: \frac{890.}{16.04} = \mathbf{55.5~kJ/g} \qquad \ce{C8H18}: \frac{5470}{114.2} = \mathbf{47.9~kJ/g} \] Methane wins per gram — it is the most hydrogen-rich hydrocarbon, and hydrogen carries more energy per gram than carbon. Yet cars burn octane, because methane is a gas: per litre of tank, the liquid wins easily. Which figure of merit you use depends on what is scarce — mass or volume.
