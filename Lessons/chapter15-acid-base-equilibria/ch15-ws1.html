Chapter 15 • Acid–Base Equilibria

Worksheet 1 • Common Ion and Henderson–Hasselbalch

Zumdahl §15.1–15.2 • the ratio sets the pH, not the concentration

Directions & data
p\(K_a\): \(\ce{HF}\) 3.14 • \(\ce{HNO2}\) 3.40 •\ \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) 4.74 • \(\ce{HOCl}\) 7.46 • \(\ce{HCN}\) 9.21 • \(\ce{NH4+}\) 9.25. \(K_a(\ce{HF}) = 7.2\times10^{-4}\).
1. Predict the effect of each addition on the pH of the solution (rises, falls, or no change), and name the ion responsible:
  1. \(\ce{NaF}\) added to \(\ce{HF}\): rises — \(\ce{F-}\)
  2. \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) added to \(\ce{NH3}\): falls — \(\ce{NH4+}\)
  3. \(\ce{KNO3}\) added to \(\ce{HNO2}\): no change — neither ion participates
  4. \(\ce{NaCN}\) added to \(\ce{HCN}\): rises — \(\ce{CN-}\)
  5. \(\ce{HCl}\) added to \(\ce{NH3}\): falls — \(\ce{H+}\) reacts with \(\ce{NH3}\)
2. Explain the common ion effect in terms of Le Ch\^atelier's principle, using \(\ce{HF}\) and \(\ce{NaF}\) as your example.
3. Zumdahl's comparison. For 1.0 M \(\ce{HF}\) alone, \([\ce{H+}] = 2.7\times10^{-2}\) and the acid is 2.7% ionized.
  1. Calculate \([\ce{H+}]\) in a solution 1.0 M in \(\ce{HF}\) and 1.0 M in \(\ce{NaF}\).
    \([\ce{H+}] = K_a[\ce{HF}]/[\ce{F-}] = (7.2\times10^{-4})(1.0/1.0) = 7.2\times10^{-4}\); pH \(= 3.14\)
  2. Calculate the percent ionization in that solution. 0.072%
  3. State in one sentence what the comparison demonstrates.
4. Buffer or not? Circle one and give a one-phrase reason.
  1. \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\): buffer — weak acid and its conjugate base
  2. \(\ce{HCl}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NaCl}\): not — \(\ce{HCl}\) is strong, \(\ce{Cl-}\) inert
  3. \(\ce{NH3}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NH4NO3}\): buffer — weak base and its conjugate acid
  4. \(\ce{NaOH}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\): not — no weak conjugate pair present
  5. \(\ce{HNO2}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NaNO2}\): buffer — weak acid and its conjugate base
5. Derive the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation from the \(K_a\) expression. Show each step.
6. Calculate the pH of each buffer:
  1. 0.20 M \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) \(+\) 0.20 M \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\): 4.74
  2. 0.10 M \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) \(+\) 0.30 M \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\):
    pH \(= 4.74 + \log(0.30/0.10) = 4.74 + 0.48 = \mathbf{5.22}\)
  3. 0.50 M \(\ce{HF}\) \(+\) 0.25 M \(\ce{NaF}\):
    pH \(= 3.14 + \log(0.25/0.50) = 3.14 - 0.30 = \mathbf{2.84}\)
  4. 0.20 M \(\ce{HCN}\) \(+\) 0.40 M \(\ce{NaCN}\): 9.51
  5. 0.50 M \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) \(+\) 0.25 M \(\ce{NH3}\):
    pH \(= 9.25 + \log(0.25/0.50) = 9.25 - 0.30 = \mathbf{8.95}\)
7. For each buffer in problem 6, state without further calculation whether its pH is above, below, or equal to p\(K_a\), and why.
8. Working backwards.
  1. What ratio \([\ce{C2H3O2^-}]/[\ce{HC2H3O2}]\) gives pH 5.00?
    \(5.00 = 4.74 + \log r\); \(\log r = 0.26\); \(r = \mathbf{1.8}\)
  2. What ratio \([\ce{NH3}]/[\ce{NH4+}]\) gives pH 9.00? 0.56
  3. A \(\ce{HF}\)/\(\ce{NaF}\) buffer has pH 3.00. Is there more \(\ce{HF}\) or more \(\ce{F-}\)? Justify without calculating.
9. A student prepares two acetate buffers: X is 1.0 M in each component, Y is 0.010 M in each.
  1. Which has the higher pH? neither — both 4.74
  2. Which is the better buffer, and why?
10. Explain why diluting a buffer barely changes its pH, while diluting a solution of the weak acid alone raises the pH noticeably.
