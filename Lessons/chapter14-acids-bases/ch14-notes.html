Arrhenius and Brønsted–Lowry ZUM §14.1

SP 1
  • Arrhenius: an acid produces in water; a base produces . Historically important, but limited — it applies only to solutions and allows only one kind of base.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: an acid is a proton ; a base is a proton . More general, and the model AP uses.
Note

Why water can act as a base: its oxygen carries two , either of which can form a bond to \(\ce{H+}\). When \(\ce{HCl}\) gas dissolves, a proton transfers from \(\ce{HCl}\) to water: \[ \ce{HCl(g) + H2O(l) -> H3O+(aq) + Cl^-(aq)} \] So \(\ce{H+}\) in solution is really hydronium, \(\ce{H3O+}\). Writing \(\ce{H+}\) is shorthand — convenient and universally accepted, but remember what it stands for.

Conjugate acid–base pairs

Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction has two pairs, differing by one proton: \[ \underbrace{\ce{HC2H3O2}}_{\text{acid}} + \underbrace{\ce{H2O}}_{\text{base}} \rightleftharpoons \underbrace{\ce{H3O+}}_{\text{conj. acid}} + \underbrace{\ce{C2H3O2^-}}_{\text{conj. base}} \]

The conjugate base of an acid is what remains after it .

SP 5 For a weak acid, ionization is an equilibrium with its own constant:

The inverse relationship

AP trap
The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. A strong acid gives up its proton readily, so the resulting anion has almost no affinity to take it back. \(\ce{Cl-}\) is such a poor base that it is effectively inert in water.

Conversely, a very weak acid like \(\ce{HCN}\) has a conjugate base (\(\ce{CN-}\)) strong enough to matter. Zumdahl's ordering makes the point: \[ \ce{Cl-} < \ce{H2O} < \ce{F-} < \ce{NO2-} < \ce{CN-} \] weakest base to strongest — exactly the reverse of their conjugate acids' strengths.

pK\(_a\)

\(\mathrm{p}K_a =\)
A lower pK\(_a\) means a acid. Acetic acid's \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\) gives pK\(_a = 4.74\).

\(K_w\) and the pH scale ZUM §14.3

SP 5 Water is amphoteric — it acts as both acid and base with itself: \[ \ce{2 H2O(l) <=> H3O+(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \]
\(K_w = [\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}] =\) \(\quad\text{at 25\,\mathrm{{}^\circ C}}\)

In any aqueous solution at 25 °C, the product of \([\ce{H+}]\) and \([\ce{OH-}]\) equals \(K_w\) — so knowing one gives the other.

AP trap

“Neutral means pH 7” is true only at 25 °C. \(K_w\) increases with temperature, so at 50 °C pure water has a pH below 7 — and is still perfectly neutral, because \([\ce{H+}]\) still equals \([\ce{OH-}]\). Neutrality is defined by that equality, not by the number 7.

The scale is logarithmic

A change of one pH unit is a factor of in \([\ce{H+}]\). pH 3 is times more acidic than pH 5 — not 40% more.

INSTRUCTION B • Strong acids and bases 20 min

When ionization is complete ZUM §14.4, 14.6

SP 5

For a strong acid, ionization is complete, so \([\ce{H+}]\) simply equals the . No equilibrium calculation is needed.

There are only six strong acids, and they are worth memorizing outright — every other acid you meet this year is weak: \[ \ce{HCl} \quad \ce{HBr} \quad \ce{HI} \quad \ce{HNO3} \quad \ce{H2SO4} \quad \ce{HClO4} \] Note that \(\ce{HF}\) is not on the list. The strong bases are the hydroxides of Group 1 and the heavier Group 2 metals — \(\ce{LiOH}\), \(\ce{NaOH}\), \(\ce{KOH}\), and \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\), \(\ce{Sr(OH)2}\), \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\).

Worked example 1: strong acid and strong base

0.010 M \(\ce{HCl}\): \([\ce{H+}] = 0.010\), so \(\mathrm{pH} = -\log(0.010) = \mathbf{2.00}\).

0.010 M \(\ce{NaOH}\): \([\ce{OH-}] = 0.010\), so \(\mathrm{pOH} = 2.00\) and \(\mathrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.00 = \mathbf{12.00}\).

Worked example 2: the factor everyone forgets

0.010 M \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\) produces two hydroxide ions per formula unit: \[ [\ce{OH-}] = 2(0.010) = 0.020\,\mathrm{M} \] \(\mathrm{pOH} = -\log(0.020) = 1.70\), so \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{12.30}\) — not 12.00. Always check the formula for how many ions are released.

\(K_a = \frac{x^2}{C_0 - x} \approx \frac{x^2}{C_0} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad x = [\ce{H+}] \approx\)
Worked example: acetic acid

Find the pH of 0.100 M acetic acid, \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\). \begin{align*} \frac{x^2}{0.100 - x} &= 1.8\times10^{-5}\\ x^2 &\approx (1.8\times10^{-5})(0.100) = 1.8\times10^{-6}\\ x &= 1.34\times10^{-3} = [\ce{H+}]\\ \mathrm{pH} &= -\log(1.34\times10^{-3}) = \mathbf{2.87} \end{align*} 5% check: \(1.34\times10^{-3}/0.100 = 1.3\%\) — well under 5%, so the approximation holds.

AP trap

Compare that pH of 2.87 with 0.100 M \(\ce{HCl}\), which has pH 1.00. Same concentration, but the weak acid's \([\ce{H+}]\) is roughly 75 times smaller. This is the difference between strong and concentrated, made numerical.

How much actually ionized ZUM §14.5

SP 5

Note

A result worth understanding rather than memorizing: for a given weak acid, percent dissociation increases as the solution is . Since \(x \approx \sqrt{K_aC_0}\), the dissociation \(x\) falls only as the square root of concentration while \(C_0\) falls linearly — so their ratio grows.

Diluting a weak acid therefore raises the fraction ionized even though it lowers \([\ce{H+}]\) and raises the pH. Both statements are true at once, and exam questions exploit the apparent tension.

\(K_b\) and the parallel method ZUM §14.6

SP 5
\(\ce{B(aq) + H2O(l) <=> BH+(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \qquad K_b =\)

The ICE method is identical to the weak acid case — but it produces \([\ce{OH-}]\), so you must convert through pOH at the end.

Worked example: ammonia

Find the pH of 0.100 M \(\ce{NH3}\), \(K_b = 1.8\times10^{-5}\). \begin{align*} x^2/0.100 &\approx 1.8\times10^{-5}\\ x &= 1.34\times10^{-3} = [\ce{OH-}]\\ \mathrm{pOH} &= 2.87 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.87 = \mathbf{11.13} \end{align*} Note the symmetry with acetic acid: identical \(K\) value, identical \(x\) — but the answers sit on opposite sides of 7.

AP trap

The single most common error here is stopping at \(x\) and calling it the pH. For a base, \(x\) is \([\ce{OH-}]\), giving pOH first. Forgetting the conversion turns pH 11.13 into 2.87 — a basic solution reported as strongly acidic.

The conjugate relationship

For any conjugate acid–base pair:
\(K_a \times K_b =\) \(= 1.0\times10^{-14}\)
Worked example: acetate

Acetic acid has \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\). Its conjugate base, acetate: \[ K_b = \frac{K_w}{K_a} = \frac{1.0\times10^{-14}}{1.8\times10^{-5}} = 5.6\times10^{-10} \] Small, but not zero — which is exactly why sodium acetate solutions are basic.

INSTRUCTION B • Polyprotic acids 20 min

One proton at a time ZUM §14.7

SP 5

A polyprotic acid ionizes in successive steps, each with its own constant: \[ \ce{H3PO4} \xrightarrow{K_{a1}} \ce{H2PO4^-} \xrightarrow{K_{a2}} \ce{HPO4^2-} \xrightarrow{K_{a3}} \ce{PO4^3-} \] \[ K_{a1} = 7.5\times10^{-3} \gg K_{a2} = 6.2\times10^{-8} \gg K_{a3} = 4.8\times10^{-13} \]

Each successive constant is roughly times smaller. The reason is electrostatic: pulling a positive proton away from an increasingly anion gets progressively harder.

Note

The practical consequence: for pH purposes, only the first ionization matters. The second contributes so little \(\ce{H+}\) that ignoring it changes the pH by far less than experimental error. Solve \(K_{a1}\) alone and stop.

\(\ce{H2SO4}\) is the exception worth knowing — its first ionization is strong, and its second (\(K_{a2} = 1.2\times10^{-2}\)) is large enough to matter in concentrated solutions.

Deciding from the parent acid and base ZUM §14.8

SP 6

Dissolve a salt and you get its ions. Whether the solution is acidic or basic depends on whether either ion reacts with water.

Cation fromAnion fromSolutionExample
strong basestrong acid\(\ce{NaCl}\)
strong baseweak acid\(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\)
weak basestrong acid\(\ce{NH4Cl}\)
weak baseweak acidcompare \(K_a\) and \(K_b\)\(\ce{NH4C2H3O2}\)

The logic is entirely conjugate-pair reasoning: an anion derived from a weak acid is a meaningful base, while one from a strong acid is not.

Worked example: Zumdahl's sodium fluoride

Find the pH of 0.30 M \(\ce{NaF}\), given \(K_a(\ce{HF}) = 7.2\times10^{-4}\).

Major species: \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{F-}\), \(\ce{H2O}\). Sodium is the cation of a strong base and does nothing. Fluoride is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it hydrolyses: \[ \ce{F^-(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HF(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \] \begin{align*} K_b &= \frac{K_w}{K_a} = \frac{1.0\times10^{-14}}{7.2\times10^{-4}} = 1.4\times10^{-11}\\ x^2/0.30 &\approx 1.4\times10^{-11}\\ x &= 2.0\times10^{-6} = [\ce{OH-}]\\ \mathrm{pOH} &= 5.69 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{8.31} \end{align*} Basic, as predicted — and only mildly so, because \(K_b\) is tiny.

INSTRUCTION B • Why some acids are stronger 20 min

Structure and acid strength ZUM §14.9

SP 6

Binary acids: bond strength dominates

Down a group, \(\ce{HF}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HCl}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HBr}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HI}\) in strength. The H–X bond gets as the halogen grows, so the proton is released more easily — and that outweighs the falling electronegativity.

AP trap
\(\ce{HF}\) is the weakest of the hydrohalic acids despite fluorine being the most electronegative element. Bond strength, not electronegativity, controls this trend. Students who reason only from electronegativity get it exactly backwards.

Oxyacids: count the oxygens

For the same central atom, more oxygens means a acid: \[ \ce{HClO} < \ce{HClO2} < \ce{HClO3} < \ce{HClO4} \] Additional electronegative oxygens pull electron density away from the O–H bond, weakening it, and they also the resulting anion by spreading its negative charge.

For the same number of oxygens, a more electronegative central atom gives a stronger acid: \(\ce{HOI}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HOBr}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HOCl}\).
