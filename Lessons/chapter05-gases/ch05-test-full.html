Sessions 2–5 (Parts C–F) are the full end-of-chapter exercise set, grouped by topic. Session 6 (Part G) is the Challenge Problem set – the hardest material in the chapter. Calculator, periodic table, and equations sheet are permitted throughout. Show every step. A bare final answer earns the answer mark only; the setup marks are for work you can point to. Carry units through each line and round only at the end. Use \(R = 0.08206\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm/mol/K}\) unless a part specifically calls for SI units.

Scope — read before Session 5

Three tools this chapter uses are not on the AP equation sheet and not part of CED Topics 3.4–3.6: the root-mean-square speed formula (\(u_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3RT/M}\)), Graham's law of effusion (\(\text{rate}_1/\text{rate}_2 = \sqrt{M_2/M_1}\)), and the van der Waals equation itself as a numerical tool. The CED gives you \(KE = \tfrac12 mv^2\) and the qualitative idea that Kelvin temperature tracks average kinetic energy (3.5.A.2–3.5.A.3), and it gives you the qualitative reasoning that interparticle attraction and particle volume explain non-ideal behavior (3.6.A.1) – but neither an rms-speed equation, an effusion-rate equation, nor van der Waals constants are anywhere in the official equation sheet. Every item using one of these three tools is flagged [beyond CED]. It is here because this is standard introductory chemistry and because reading a molecular-speed or non-ideal-gas argument qualitatively is core CED content even when solving for a number is not.

Free-response 2 • short • 3 points

A 1.53 L sample of gaseous \(\ce{SO2}\) at 5.6e3 Pa has its pressure changed to 1.5e4 Pa at constant temperature.

Calculate the new volume.
Boyle's law: \(P_1V_1 = P_2V_2\) \[V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{(5.6\times10^3)(1.53)}{1.5\times10^4} = \mathbf{0.57}\ \text{L}\]

Free-response 10 • short • 5 points

0.35 mol argon gas is held at 13 °C and 568 torr. Conditions change to 56 °C and 897 torr.

Find the change in volume, \(V_2-V_1\).
Convert everything first. \(P_1 = 568/760=0.7474\,\mathrm{atm}\), \(P_2=897/760=1.1803\,\mathrm{atm}\), \(T_1=286\,\mathrm{K}\), \(T_2=329\,\mathrm{K}\). Solve each state separately (moles are fixed, but pressure and temperature both change, so there is no single-ratio shortcut for \(\Delta V\)): \[V_1 = \frac{nRT_1}{P_1} = \frac{(0.35)(0.08206)(286)}{0.7474} = 11.0\ \text{L}\] \[V_2 = \frac{nRT_2}{P_2} = \frac{(0.35)(0.08206)(329)}{1.1803} = 8.01\ \text{L}\] \[\Delta V = V_2-V_1 = 8.01-11.0 = \mathbf{-3.0}\ \text{L}\]

The volume decreases by about 3.0 L — the pressure rose by a larger factor than the temperature did, so compression wins out over thermal expansion.

Free-response 15 • long • 5 points

For a deep dive, a scuba tank is filled by pumping 46 L \(\ce{He}\) gas (at 25 °C, 1.0 atm) and 12 L \(\ce{O2}\) gas (at 25 °C, 1.0 atm) into a previously evacuated 5.0 L tank.

Free-response 20 • short • 3 points

Calculate the ratio of effusion rates of \(\ce{H2}\) gas to \(\ce{UF6}\) gas, under identical conditions.
\[\frac{\text{rate}(\ce{H2})} {\text{rate}(\ce{UF6})} = \sqrt{\frac{M(\ce{UF6})}{M(\ce{H2})}} = \sqrt{\frac{352.03}{2.016}} = \mathbf{13.2}\]

A gas sample is connected to an open-tube mercury manometer; atmospheric pressure is 760. torr. In two separate trials, the gas pressure exceeds atmospheric pressure, and the mercury column shows height differences of 118 mm and 215 mm.

Free-response 102 • long • 4 points

\[\left[P+a\left(\frac{n}{V}\right)^2\right](V-nb) = nRT\] Show algebraically that this reduces to \(PV=nRT\) at low pressure (large \(V\), fixed \(n\)) and explain why the same is true at high temperature.
Low pressure / large volume. As \(V\) grows without bound at fixed \(n\): the attraction term \(a(n/V)^2\to0\), since \(n/V\to0\); and \(nb\) becomes negligible relative to \(V\), so \((V-nb)\to V\). Substituting both limits: \[[P+0](V-0) \ \longrightarrow\ PV=nRT\]

Physically: at low pressure molecules are far apart on average, so (i) their own volume \(nb\) is a negligible fraction of the container, and (ii) intermolecular attractions — which fall off steeply with distance — rarely have a chance to act.

High temperature. The attraction term \(a(n/V)^2\) does not depend on \(T\) at all, but \(nRT\) grows without bound as \(T\) rises, so the attraction term's contribution to the measured pressure becomes comparatively negligible: kinetic energy increasingly dominates over the potential energy of intermolecular attraction. (The \(nb\) term still requires \(V\) to stay reasonably large — i.e., pressure not also pushed to an extreme — for it to vanish too.) Both limits describe the same underlying idea: the two correction terms exist specifically to capture molecular size and intermolecular attraction, and both become unimportant whenever the gas is dilute or energetic enough that molecules spend most of their time far apart and moving fast relative to any attraction between them.
