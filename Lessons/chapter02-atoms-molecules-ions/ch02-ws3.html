How to use this sheet

Naming is not a reasoning skill, it is a recall skill sitting on top of one decision. The decision is always the same:

TypeWhat it isHow you know
Iionic, fixed-charge metalmetal in group 1A, 2A, 3A, or \(\ce{Ag}\), \(\ce{Zn}\), \(\ce{Cd}\) — no Roman numeral
IIionic, variable-charge metaltransition metal (or \(\ce{Sn}\), \(\ce{Pb}\)) — Roman numeral required
IIIbinary covalenttwo nonmetals — prefixes, and only here
Aacidformula begins with \(\ce{H}\) and is aqueous

Almost every naming error is a type error, not a memory error — prefixes used on an ionic compound, or a Roman numeral left off a transition metal.

The oxyanion pattern — learn this instead of memorizing

Where a series of anions differ only in oxygen count, the names follow one pattern. Two members: fewer oxygens \(\to\) -ite, more \(\to\) -ate. More than two: the extremes take hypo- (fewest) and per- (most).

The chlorine series is the one to hold in memory, because every other halogen copies it exactly: \[ \underbrace{\ce{ClO-}}_{\text{hypochlorite}} \quad \underbrace{\ce{ClO2-}}_{\text{chlorite}} \quad \underbrace{\ce{ClO3-}}_{\text{chlorate}} \quad \underbrace{\ce{ClO4-}}_{\text{perchlorate}} \] Know \(\ce{ClO3-}\) is chlorate and \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) is sulfate, and the rest of each series follows without memorizing anything else.

AP trap
“I ate it, so it was ic.” The -ate anion gives the -ic acid. The commonest slip on this topic is naming \(\ce{H2SO3}\) sulfuric acid — it contains sulfite, so it is sulfurous acid.

Part 3 • Worked examples

Worked example 1: \(\ce{Fe(ClO3)2}\), one step at a time
1. Type? \(\ce{Fe}\) is a transition metal \(\Rightarrow\) Type II, so a Roman numeral is required and no prefixes are used. 2. Anion? \(\ce{ClO3-}\) is chlorate, charge \(1-\). There are two of them, so the negative charge totals \(2-\). 3. Cation charge, by balance? \(\ce{Fe}\) must be \(2+\). 4. Assemble. Iron(II) chlorate. Note what supplied the Roman numeral — not the formula's subscript on iron, but the total charge on the anions. The subscript 2 belongs to \(\ce{ClO3-}\), not to \(\ce{Fe}\).
Worked example 2: reverse — ammonium sulfate
1. Ions. Ammonium is \(\ce{NH4+}\); sulfate is \(\ce{SO4^2-}\). 2. Balance. One \(2-\) needs two \(1+\), so two ammonium ions. 3. Assemble. A polyatomic ion taken more than once needs parentheses: \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\).

Writing \(\ce{NH42SO4}\) would read as forty-two hydrogens. Parentheses are not decoration.

Worked example 3: three lookalikes
\[ \ce{Na2SO3} \qquad \ce{Na2SO4} \qquad \ce{Na2S2O3} \] Sodium is fixed-charge in all three, so the whole answer sits in the anion: sulfite, sulfate, and thiosulfate respectively — sodium sulfite, sodium sulfate, sodium thiosulfate.

The prefix thio- means an oxygen has been replaced by a sulfur: compare \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) with \(\ce{S2O3^2-}\).
