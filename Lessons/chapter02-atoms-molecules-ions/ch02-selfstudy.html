Ladder 1 • The laws that demanded atoms

ZUM §2.2–2.3

Three quantitative laws came first; Dalton's theory was invented to explain them.

LawWhat it states
Conservation of massmass is neither created nor destroyed in a reaction
Definite proportiona given compound always has the same proportions by mass
Multiple proportionswhen two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one combining with a fixed mass of the other are in small whole-number ratios

The third is the decisive one: small whole numbers are what you expect if matter comes in indivisible units, and are hard to explain otherwise.

I do: testing the law of multiple proportions

Two oxides of sulfur are analysed. Oxide A is 50.05% S by mass; oxide B is 40.05% S. Show that these obey the law of multiple proportions, and identify the ratio.

Fix the sulfur, compare the oxygen. Take 100 g of each: \[ \text{A}: 50.05~\text{g S} + 49.95~\text{g O} \qquad \text{B}: 40.05~\text{g S} + 59.95~\text{g O} \] Oxygen per gram of sulfur: \[ \text{A}: \frac{49.95}{50.05} = 0.998 \qquad \text{B}: \frac{59.95}{40.05} = 1.497 \] Ratio of those two figures: \[ \frac{1.497}{0.998} = 1.50 = \frac{3}{2} \]

A small whole-number ratio, exactly as the law requires. The compounds are \(\ce{SO2}\) and \(\ce{SO3}\): for a fixed amount of sulfur, B holds half again as much oxygen.

The move to remember: hold one element constant, then divide. The ratio only looks simple after that normalization.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. Two carbon oxides give 1.33 g and 2.66 g of oxygen per gram of carbon. State the ratio and name both compounds.
  2. A 10.0 g sample of a compound decomposes into 4.0 g of A and 6.0 g of B. What mass of B is in a 25.0 g sample, and which law did you use?
  3. 12.0 g of carbon burns in 32.0 g of oxygen to give carbon dioxide. What mass of \(\ce{CO2}\) forms, and by which law?
  4. Which of the three laws is the strongest evidence for atoms, and why?

check: (a) \(1{:}2\) — \(\ce{CO}\) and \(\ce{CO2}\)   (b) 15.0 g of B, by definite proportion   (c) 44.0 g, by conservation of mass   (d) multiple proportions

Ladder 2 • Three experiments, three conclusions

ZUM §2.4

Every one of these is examinable as experiment \(\to\) observation \(\to\) conclusion. Learn the chain, not the anecdote.

ExperimentKey observationConclusion
Thomson, cathode raythe beam bends toward the positive plate, identically for every cathode metala negative particle common to all atoms; gave \(e/m\), not \(e\)
Millikan, oil dropdroplet charges are all multiples of one valuethe charge on the electron, so with Thomson's ratio, its mass
Rutherford, gold foila few alpha particles deflect sharply backwarda tiny, dense, positive nucleus in a mostly empty atom
I do: reasoning from the observation
Why did Rutherford's result overturn the plum-pudding model? What the old model predicted. If positive charge were spread thinly through the whole atom, an alpha particle would meet only weak, diffuse repulsion everywhere. Every alpha should pass nearly straight through, with slight deflection at most. What was observed. The vast majority did pass straight through — but roughly 1 in 20 000 bounced back at more than 90 degree. Why that is decisive. Reversing a fast, massive alpha particle requires a concentrated positive charge that is also massive enough not to simply recoil away. Spread-out charge cannot do it at any strength. So the positive charge and nearly all the mass must sit in a minute volume — a nucleus — while the rest of the atom is essentially empty. Note the shape of the argument: the rare event, not the common one, carries the information. Most alphas passing through told Rutherford the atom is mostly empty; the few that came back told him what sits at the centre.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Thomson used several different cathode metals and got the same particle each time. What does that show?
  2. Millikan measured charge. Why was Thomson's experiment alone not enough to give the electron's mass?
  3. In the gold foil experiment, what did the alphas that passed straight through establish?
  4. Which subatomic particle was discovered last, and why was it the hardest to detect?

check: (a) electrons are in all atoms   (b) it gave only the charge-to-mass ratio   (c) the atom is mostly empty space   (d) the neutron — it carries no charge

Ladder 3 • Reading isotope symbols

ZUM §2.5 \[ \ce{^{A}_{Z}X} \qquad Z = \text{atomic number} = \text{protons} \qquad A = \text{mass number} = \text{protons} + \text{neutrons} \] Three counts, three rules:
  • Protons \(= Z\) — and \(Z\) is the element's identity. Change it and you have a different element.
  • Neutrons \(= A - Z\). Change it and you have an isotope of the same element.
  • Electrons \(= Z - \text{charge}\). Change it and you have an ion.
I do: three species, one procedure
(a) \(\ce{^{31}_{15}P}\): protons \(= 15\); neutrons \(= 31 - 15 = \mathbf{16}\); neutral, so electrons \(= \mathbf{15}\). (b) \(\ce{^{80}_{35}Br^-}\): protons \(= 35\); neutrons \(= 80 - 35 = \mathbf{45}\); the \(1-\) charge means one extra electron, so electrons \(= 35 - (-1) = \mathbf{36}\). (c) \(\ce{^{27}_{13}Al^{3+}}\): protons \(= 13\); neutrons \(= 27 - 13 = \mathbf{14}\); the \(3+\) charge means three electrons missing, so electrons \(= 13 - 3 = \mathbf{10}\). The sign trap, stated once: a negative ion has more electrons, a positive ion has fewer. The formula electrons \(= Z - \text{charge}\) handles both automatically, provided you carry the charge with its sign. Worth noticing: the bromide ion in (b) has 36 electrons, the same as krypton, and the aluminium ion in (c) has 10, the same as neon. Ions form by reaching a noble-gas electron count.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. \(\ce{^{56}_{26}Fe}\): protons, neutrons, electrons.
  2. \(\ce{^{32}_{16}S^{2-}}\): protons, neutrons, electrons.
  3. An ion has 20 protons, 20 neutrons, and 18 electrons. Identify it and write its full symbol.
  4. \(\ce{^{14}C}\) and \(\ce{^{14}N}\) share a mass number. Are they isotopes of each other? Explain.

check: (a) 26, 30, 26   (b) 16, 16, 18   (c) \(\ce{^{40}_{20}Ca^{2+}}\)   (d) no — different \(Z\), so different elements

Ladder 4 • The periodic table as a map

ZUM §2.7

The table is arranged so that position predicts behaviour.

  • Groups (columns) share chemical behaviour: 1A alkali metals, 2A alkaline earth metals, 7A halogens, 8A noble gases.
  • Periods (rows) are filled left to right.
  • Metals sit left of the staircase, nonmetals right, metalloids along it (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te).
  • Metals lose electrons to form cations; nonmetals gain them to form anions.
I do: predicting from position alone
Predict the ion formed by strontium, by selenium, and by argon — using only their positions. Strontium is in group 2A, so it has two outer electrons to lose and forms \(\ce{Sr^2+}\). Every group 2A metal does the same, which is why the group has a shared name. Selenium is in group 6A, two short of eight, so it gains two: \(\ce{Se^2-}\). The pattern across the nonmetals is \(8 - \text{group number}\) electrons gained. Argon is in group 8A, already at eight outer electrons, so it forms no ion — there is nothing to gain or lose cheaply. That is exactly why the noble gases are unreactive, and why every other ion above lands on a noble-gas count. The single rule underneath all three: elements form the ion that reaches the nearest noble-gas electron count.
YOUR TURN 4 — four questions
  1. Predict the ions formed by potassium, by nitrogen, and by barium.
  2. Which group do \(\ce{Cl}\), \(\ce{Br}\), and \(\ce{I}\) belong to, and what charge do their ions carry?
  3. Name the metalloid in period 3, and say what “metalloid” means.
  4. Predict the formula of the compound formed between magnesium and nitrogen, showing the charge balance.

check: (a) \(\ce{K+}\), \(\ce{N^3-}\), \(\ce{Ba^2+}\)   (b) group 7A, the halogens; \(1-\)   (c) silicon — properties between metal and nonmetal   (d) \(\ce{Mg3N2}\)

Ladder 5 • Molecules, ions, and formulas

ZUM §2.6

An ionic compound is a lattice of ions held by electrostatic attraction — its formula is the simplest whole-number ratio, not a discrete particle. A molecular compound is made of discrete molecules, and its formula counts the atoms in one of them.

Charge balance writes every ionic formula: total positive \(=\) total negative, with no charges shown in the finished formula.

I do: building formulas from charges
(a) Aluminium and oxygen. \(\ce{Al^3+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\). The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so two \(\ce{Al^3+}\) (\(+6\)) balance three \(\ce{O^2-}\) (\(-6\)): \(\ce{Al2O3}\). (b) Calcium and phosphate. \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and \(\ce{PO4^3-}\). Again LCM 6: three \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) (\(+6\)) with two \(\ce{PO4^3-}\) (\(-6\)). The polyatomic ion is taken more than once, so it needs parentheses: \(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2}\). (c) Ammonium and sulfate. \(\ce{NH4+}\) and \(\ce{SO4^2-}\): two ammonium per sulfate, \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\). Why parentheses are not optional: \(\ce{NH42SO4}\) would read as forty-two hydrogens. The parentheses say “two of the whole group”. And the conceptual point: \(\ce{Al2O3}\) does not describe a molecule. There is no \(\ce{Al2O3}\) particle — only a vast lattice in which aluminium and oxide ions occur in a 2:3 ratio.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. Write the formula from lithium and sulfide ions.
  2. Write the formula from iron(III) and hydroxide ions.
  3. Write the formula from ammonium and phosphate ions.
  4. Is \(\ce{P4O10}\) ionic or molecular? How can you tell from the elements alone?

check: (a) \(\ce{Li2S}\)   (b) \(\ce{Fe(OH)3}\)   (c) \(\ce{(NH4)3PO4}\)   (d) molecular — two nonmetals

Ladder 6 • Averaging isotopes

ZUM §2.5

The mass on the periodic table is a weighted average over the naturally occurring isotopes: \[ \bar m = \sum(\text{fractional abundance} \times \text{isotope mass}) \] No single atom has that mass. The average always lies between the extremes and leans toward the abundant isotope — two checks that cost nothing and catch most errors.

I do: the atomic mass of chlorine

Chlorine is 75.78% \(\ce{^{35}Cl}\) (34.969 u) and 24.22% \(\ce{^{37}Cl}\) (36.966 u).

\[ \bar m = (0.7578)(34.969) + (0.2422)(36.966) \] \[ \phantom{\bar m} = 26.500 + 8.953 = \mathbf{35.45~u} \] Both checks: 35.45 lies between 34.969 and 36.966 — good; and it sits much closer to 34.969, which is right because \(\ce{^{35}Cl}\) is roughly three times as abundant. What the value means physically: there is no chlorine atom weighing 35.45 u. Every one weighs about 35 or about 37; 35.45 is what a mole of natural chlorine averages to, which is exactly the number needed for weighing bulk samples.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. Copper is 69.17% \(\ce{^{63}Cu}\) (62.930 u) and 30.83% \(\ce{^{65}Cu}\) (64.928 u). Find the atomic mass.
  2. Without calculating: is boron's atomic mass nearer 10 or 11, given that \(\ce{^{11}B}\) is about 80% abundant?
  3. Lithium's atomic mass is 6.94 u, with isotopes of 6.015 and 7.016 u. Which is more abundant, and how do you know?
  4. Explain in one sentence why no individual atom has the mass printed on the periodic table.

check: (a) 63.55 u   (b) nearer 11   (c) \(\ce{^{7}Li}\) — the average sits close to 7.016   (d) it is an average over isotopes

Ladder 7 • Choosing the naming rule

ZUM §2.8

Nomenclature is drilled at length in Worksheet 3. What matters here is the decision that comes first — almost every naming error is a mis-classification, not a forgotten name.

TypeHow you recognize itHow it is named
Imetal of fixed charge (1A, 2A, 3A, \(\ce{Ag}\), \(\ce{Zn}\), \(\ce{Cd}\))metal \(+\) anion; no Roman numeral
IItransition metal, or \(\ce{Sn}\) / \(\ce{Pb}\)Roman numeral gives the charge, deduced from the anions
IIItwo nonmetalsprefixes on both elements (no mono- on the first)
Aformula starts with \(\ce{H}\), aqueous-ide to hydro… ic; -ite to -ous; -ate to -ic
I do: classify first, then name

Name \(\ce{TiO2}\), \(\ce{SO3}\), and \(\ce{HBrO3(aq)}\) — classifying each before writing anything.

\(\ce{TiO2}\). Titanium is a transition metal \(\Rightarrow\) Type II, so a Roman numeral is required and no prefixes are used. Two \(\ce{O^2-}\) give \(-4\), so titanium is \(+4\): titanium(IV) oxide. The numeral came from the anions, never from the subscript on titanium. \(\ce{SO3}\). Sulfur and oxygen are both nonmetals \(\Rightarrow\) Type III, so prefixes and no numeral: sulfur trioxide. Writing “sulfur(VI) oxide” applies the wrong rule set entirely. \(\ce{HBrO3(aq)}\). Starts with \(\ce{H}\) and is aqueous \(\Rightarrow\) acid. The anion \(\ce{BrO3-}\) is bromate, an -ate, which gives an -ic acid: bromic acid. The pattern: each name followed from the classification, and each classification took one glance at what kind of elements were present.
