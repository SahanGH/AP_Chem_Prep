Unit 6 • Thermochemistry

Comprehensive Test

CED 6.1–6.9 • Session 1: Worked Examples, 14 questions, 80 points, 105 minutes • Session 2: Challenge Problems, 10 questions, 72 points, 90 minutes

How this paper runs
Two sessions. Session 1 works through every worked example in Zumdahl Chapter 6 and §8.8, in the book's own order — the techniques build on each other. Session 2 is the chapter's own Challenge Problem set in full.

Calculator, periodic table, and equations sheet are permitted throughout. Show every step. A bare final answer earns the answer mark only; the setup marks are for work you can point to. Carry units through each line and round only at the end.

Scope — read before you start

Two CED exclusions shape this paper. Under CED 6.6: the technical distinction between enthalpy and internal energy is not assessed. Most AP reactions run at constant pressure, where \(q_p = \Delta H\) — that equivalence is all the CED asks for. Under CED 6.9: the formal concept of a state function is not assessed — Hess's law itself is fully examinable, the thermodynamic formalism behind why it works is not.

Chapter 6 itself is a general-chemistry chapter, and several of its early worked examples are built entirely around \(\Delta E = q + w\) and \(w = -P\Delta V\) — squarely the excluded material. Those parts are flagged [beyond CED] throughout. They are here because the calculation is short, standard, and worth knowing — not because the AP exam will ask for it.

Section A • Worked Examples — Energy, Work, and Enthalpy 7 questions • 32 points

Free-response 1 • short • 3 points

A system absorbs 15.6 kJ of heat from its surroundings, and 1.4 kJ of work is done on the system.

Find \(\Delta E\) for the system.
Step 1 — assign signs. Heat flows into the system: \(q = +15.6\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Work is done on the system: \(w = +1.4\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Step 2 — apply the first law. \[\Delta E = q + w = 15.6 + 1.4 = \mathbf{+17.0}\ \text{kJ}\]

The system's internal energy increases by 17.0 kJ — both terms added energy, so both carry a positive sign.

Free-response 2 • short • 3 points

A gas expands from 46 L to 64 L at a constant external pressure of 15 atm.

Calculate the work associated with this expansion.
\[\Delta V = 64 - 46 = 18\,\mathrm{L}\] \[w = -P\Delta V = -(15)(18) = \mathbf{-270}\ \text{L\,atm}\]

The negative sign is not optional bookkeeping: an expanding gas does work on the surroundings, so the system's own energy account records a loss.

Free-response 3 • long • 5 points

A weather balloon expands from 4.00e6 L to 4.50e6 L while 1.3e8 J of heat is added, against a constant external pressure of 1.0 atm. (\(1\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm} = 101.3\,\mathrm{J}\).)

Find \(\Delta E\) for the gas inside the balloon.
Step 1 — work, in L atm. \[\Delta V = 4.50\times10^6 - 4.00\times10^6 = 5.0\times10^5\ \text{L}\] \[w = -P\Delta V = -(1.0)(5.0\times10^5) = -5.0\times10^5\ \text{L\,atm}\] Step 2 — convert to joules. \[w = (-5.0\times10^5\ \text{L\,atm}) \times 101.3\ \frac{\text{J}} {\text{L\,atm}} = \mathbf{-5.1\times10^7}\ \text{J}\] Step 3 — apply the first law. \[\Delta E = q + w = 1.3\times10^8 + (-5.1\times10^7) = \mathbf{8\times10^7}\ \text{J}\]

More heat was added than the balloon spent expanding, so the net internal energy rises — to 1 significant figure, since \(q\) was only given to two.

Free-response 4 • short • 4 points

Burning 1 mol of \(\ce{CH4(g)}\) at constant pressure releases 890 kJ.

Find \(\Delta H\) for burning 5.8 g of \(\ce{CH4}\).
\[\mathcal{M}(\ce{CH4}) = 12.01 + 4(1.008) = 16.04\,\mathrm{g/mol}\] \[n = 5.8/16.04 = 0.362\ \text{mol}\] \[\Delta H = 0.362 \times (-890) = \mathbf{-320}\ \text{kJ}\] \(\Delta H\) is an extensive property — it scales directly with the amount reacting, unlike an intensive property such as temperature.

Free-response 5 • long • 6 points

1.00 L of 1.00 M \(\ce{Ba(NO3)2}\) and 1.00 L of 1.00 M \(\ce{Na2SO4}\), both at 25.0 °C, are mixed in a coffee-cup calorimeter. The temperature rises to 28.1 °C. Take the specific heat of the mixed solution as 4.18 J/g/°C and its density as 1.0 g/mL.

  1. Write the net ionic equation.
    \(\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}\)
  2. Find \(\Delta H\) per mole of \(\ce{BaSO4}\) formed.
    Step 1 — heat released to the solution.

    mass of solution \(= 2.00\ \text{L} \times 1000\ \text{mL/L} \times 1.0\ \text{g/mL} = 2.0\times10^3\) g

    \(\Delta T = 28.1 - 25.0 = 3.1\,\mathrm{{}^\circ C}\) \[q_{\text{soln}} = (4.18)(2.0\times10^3)(3.1) = 2.6e4\ \text{J} = 26\ \text{kJ}\] Step 2 — sign flip. The reaction released this heat to the solution, so the reaction's own \(q_p = \Delta H = -26\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Step 3 — moles of \(\ce{BaSO4}\). Both solutions supply exactly \(1.00\ \text{L} \times 1.00\ \text{M} = 1.00\) mol of their ion, a clean 1:1 match — 1.00 mol \(\ce{BaSO4}\) forms. \[\Delta H = \frac{-26\ \text{kJ}}{1.00\ \text{mol}} = \mathbf{-26}\ \text{kJ/mol}\]

Free-response 6 • long • 6 points

A bomb calorimeter has heat capacity 11.3 kJ/°C. Burning 1.50 g of \(\ce{CH4}\) raises the temperature 7.3 °C; burning 1.15 g of \(\ce{H2}\) raises it 14.3 °C.

Which fuel releases more energy per gram, and by what factor?
Step 1 — total energy released in each trial. \(\ce{CH4}\): \(E = (11.3)(7.3) = 82.5\,\mathrm{kJ}\) \(\ce{H2}\): \(E = (11.3)(14.3) = 162\,\mathrm{kJ}\) Step 2 — divide by sample mass. \(\ce{CH4}\): \(82.5/1.50 = \mathbf{55.0}\) kJ/g \(\ce{H2}\): \(162/1.15 = \mathbf{141}\) kJ/g Step 3 — compare. \[\frac{141}{55.0} = \mathbf{2.56}\] Hydrogen releases about 2.5 times as much energy per gram as methane — part of why it is attractive as a fuel despite being hard to store (Example 6.13 returns to exactly this trade-off).

Free-response 7 • short • 5 points

Enthalpies of combustion: graphite, -394 kJ/mol; diamond, -396 kJ/mol.

Find \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{C_{graphite}(s) -> C_{diamond}(s)}\).
Step 1 — write both combustion equations. \(\ce{C_{graphite}(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\)   \(\Delta H = -394\) kJ \(\ce{C_{diamond}(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\)   \(\Delta H = -396\) kJ Step 2 — reverse the second (diamond must end up as a product), flipping its sign, and add: \(\ce{C_{graphite}(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\)   \(-394\) kJ \(\ce{CO2(g) -> C_{diamond}(s) + O2(g)}\)   \(+396\) kJ \(\ce{O2}\) and \(\ce{CO2}\) cancel, leaving the target equation. \[\Delta H = -394 + 396 = \mathbf{+2}\ \text{kJ/mol}\]

Slightly endothermic — consistent with diamond being the higher-energy, less stable form of carbon at ordinary conditions.

Section B • Worked Examples — Hess's Law and Formation Enthalpies 7 questions • 48 points

Free-response 8 • long • 7 points

Diborane, \(\ce{B2H6(g)}\), was once studied as a rocket fuel. Find \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{2B(s) + 3H2(g) -> B2H6(g)}\) using:

(a) \(\ce{2B(s) + 3/2O2(g) -> B2O3(s)}\)   \(\Delta H = -1273\) kJ
(b) \(\ce{B2H6(g) + 3O2(g) -> B2O3(s) + 3H2O(g)}\)   \(\Delta H = -2035\) kJ
(c) \(\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(l)}\)   \(\Delta H = -286\) kJ
(d) \(\ce{H2O(l) -> H2O(g)}\)   \(\Delta H = +44\) kJ
Step 1 — match reactants/products to the target. The target needs \(\ce{B(s)}\) and \(\ce{H2(g)}\) as reactants, \(\ce{B2H6(g)}\) as the only product. Keep (a) as written — \(\ce{B(s)}\) is already a reactant, matching the target's \(2\ce{B(s)}\). Reverse (b) — the target needs \(\ce{B2H6}\) as a product, but (b) has it as a reactant: \[\ce{B2O3(s) + 3H2O(g) -> B2H6(g) + 3O2(g)}\quad \Delta H = +2035\ \text{kJ}\] Combine (c)+(d), then triple — the target needs \(3\ce{H2}\) as a reactant with no water anywhere in the final equation. Adding (c) and (d) gives \(\ce{H2(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> H2O(g)}\), \(\Delta H = -286+44 = -242\) kJ; tripling for 3 mol \(\ce{H2}\): \[\ce{3H2(g) + 3/2O2(g) -> 3H2O(g)}\quad \Delta H = 3(-242) = -726\ \text{kJ}\] Step 2 — add all three and check cancellation. \(\ce{B2O3(s)}\) cancels (appears both sides); \(3\ce{H2O(g)}\) cancels; \(3/2+3/2=3\) mol \(\ce{O2}\) on the left cancels the 3 mol \(\ce{O2}\) on the right. What remains is exactly \(\ce{2B(s) + 3H2(g) -> B2H6(g)}\). \[\Delta H = -1273 + 2035 + (-726) = \mathbf{+36}\ \text{kJ}\]

Free-response 9 • long • 6 points

\(\ce{4NH3(g) + 7O2(g) -> 4NO2(g) + 6H2O(l)}\). Given \(\Delta H_f^\circ\): \(\ce{NH3(g)}\) \(=-46\); \(\ce{NO2(g)}\) \(=+34\); \(\ce{H2O(l)}\) \(=-286\) (all kJ/mol; \(\ce{O2}\) is an element). Find \(\Delta H^\circ\) for the reaction.
Step 1 — decompose the reactants to elements, the reverse of formation: \(4\ce{NH3}\): \(4\times(-(-46)) = +184\) kJ \(7\ce{O2}\): already elemental, \(0\) kJ Step 2 — build the products from elements, the formation reactions themselves: \(4\ce{NO2}\): \(4\times(34) = +136\) kJ \(6\ce{H2O(l)}\): \(6\times(-286) = -1716\) kJ Step 3 — sum. \[\Delta H^\circ = 184 + 0 + 136 - 1716 = \mathbf{-1396}\ \text{kJ}\]

The general rule this whole method reduces to: \(\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum n\,\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})\).

Free-response 10 • short • 4 points

\(\ce{2Al(s) + Fe2O3(s) -> Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(s)}\). Given \(\Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{Fe2O3}) = -826\), \(\Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{Al2O3}) = -1676\) kJ/mol. Find \(\Delta H^\circ\) for the reaction.
\(\ce{Al(s)}\) and \(\ce{Fe(s)}\) are elements, so \(\Delta H_f^\circ = 0\) for both. \[\Delta H^\circ = \Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{Al2O3}) - \Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{Fe2O3}) = -1676 - (-826) = \mathbf{-850.}\ \text{kJ}\]

The famously violent thermite reaction is this exothermic — enough to melt the iron product on the spot.

Free-response 11 • long • 8 points

Given \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) (kJ/mol): \(\ce{CO2(g)}\) \(=-394\); \(\ce{H2O(l)}\) \(=-286\); \(\ce{CH3OH(l)}\) \(=-239\); \(\ce{C8H18(l)}\) \(=-269\).

  1. Find the enthalpy of combustion of methanol per gram. \(\ce{2CH3OH(l) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 4H2O(l)}\).
    \[\Delta H = 2(-394) + 4(-286) - 2(-239) = \mathbf{-1454}\ \text{kJ (per 2 mol)}\] \[\mathcal{M}(\ce{CH3OH}) = 32.04\ \text{g/mol} \Rightarrow 2\ \text{mol} = 64.08\ \text{g}\] \[\frac{-1454}{64.08} = \mathbf{-22.7}\ \text{kJ/g}\]
  2. Find the enthalpy of combustion of octane per gram. \(\ce{2C8H18(l) + 25O2(g) -> 16CO2(g) + 18H2O(l)}\).
    \[\Delta H = 16(-394) + 18(-286) - 2(-269) = -10{,}914\ \text{kJ (per 2 mol)} \approx \mathbf{-1.09\times10^4}\ \text{kJ}\] \[\mathcal{M}(\ce{C8H18}) = 114.22\ \text{g/mol} \Rightarrow 2\ \text{mol} = 228.4\ \text{g}\]

    Dividing the unrounded enthalpy by mass (rounding only at the end, per the front-page rule):

    \[\frac{-10{,}914}{228.4} = \mathbf{-47.8}\ \text{kJ/g}\]
  3. Octane releases about twice the energy per gram that methanol does. Methanol was nonetheless once preferred in race-car engines. Suggest why.
    Methanol burns more smoothly and completely, and is harder to ignite accidentally — safety and combustion-quality advantages that outweighed needing more fuel mass on board for the same energy.

Free-response 12 • short • 5 points

Using Example 6.6's results (55.0 kJ/g for \(\ce{CH4}\), 141 kJ/g for \(\ce{H2}\)): equal volumes of ideal gases at the same temperature and pressure hold equal numbers of moles. Which gas releases more energy per equal volume, and by what factor?
Step 1 — convert each to a per-mole basis, since equal volume means equal moles. \(\ce{H2}\): \(141 \times 2.02 = \mathbf{285}\) kJ/mol \(\ce{CH4}\): \(55.0 \times 16.04 = \mathbf{882}\) kJ/mol Step 2 — compare. \[\frac{285}{882} = \mathbf{0.32}\]

Mole for mole (hence volume for volume), \(\ce{CH4}\) releases about 3 times as much energy as \(\ce{H2}\) — the opposite ranking from the per-gram comparison in Example 6.6. \(\ce{H2}\) wins per gram because it is so light; it loses per volume because a mole of gas is a mole of gas regardless of what it weighs.

Free-response 13 • long • 9 points

\(\ce{H2}\) provides about 3 times the energy per gram that gasoline does (from Examples 6.6 and 6.11). Densities: liquid \(\ce{H2}\), 0.0710 g/mL; gasoline, 0.740 g/mL. A full tank holds 80.0 L of gasoline.
  1. Find the mass of liquid \(\ce{H2}\) needed to match the tank's energy.
    \[m(\text{gasoline}) = 80.0\times1000 \times 0.740 = 5.92\times10^4\ \text{g}\] \[m(\ce{H2}) = \frac{5.92\times10^4}{3} = \mathbf{1.97\times10^4}\ \text{g}\]
  2. Find the volume that mass of liquid \(\ce{H2}\) occupies.
    \[V = \frac{1.97\times10^4}{0.0710} = 2.78\times10^5\ \text{mL} = \mathbf{278}\ \text{L}\]

    Already nearly 3.5 times the gasoline tank's own volume, and that is the easy case — liquid.

  3. Find the volume the same mass of \(\ce{H2}\) would occupy as a gas at 25 °C, 1.00 atm.
    \[n = \frac{1.97\times10^4}{2.016} = 9.79\times10^3\ \text{mol}\] \[V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{(9.79\times10^3)(0.08206)(298)} {1.00} = \mathbf{2.39\times10^5}\ \text{L}\]

    That is almost 3000 times the volume of the 80.0 L gasoline tank it replaces (and about 860 times the volume of part (b)'s liquid \(\ce{H2}\)) — the real reason hydrogen cars store the gas compressed or liquefied rather than at room pressure.

Free-response 14 • long • 9 points

Average bond energies (kJ/mol): \(\ce{H-H}\) 432, \(\ce{F-F}\) 154, \(\ce{H-F}\) 565, \(\ce{C-H}\) 413, \(\ce{Cl-Cl}\) 239, \(\ce{C-F}\) 485, \(\ce{C-Cl}\) 339, \(\ce{H-Cl}\) 427.

  1. As a warm-up, find \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{H2(g) + F2(g) -> 2HF(g)}\) from these bond energies, then check it against \(\Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{HF}) = -271\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\).
    \[\Delta H = D(\ce{H-H}) + D(\ce{F-F}) - 2D(\ce{H-F}) = 432 + 154 - 2(565) = \mathbf{-544}\ \text{kJ}\]

    Check: \(2\Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{HF}) = 2(-271) = \mathbf{-542}\) kJ — the two methods agree to within rounding, confirming bond energies are a legitimate shortcut for estimating \(\Delta H\).

  2. Find \(\Delta H\) for the synthesis of Freon-12: \(\ce{CH4(g) + 2Cl2(g) + 2F2(g) -> CF2Cl2(g) + 2HF(g) + 2HCl(g)}\).
    Bonds broken (reactants — take everything apart to atoms): \(4\ \ce{C-H}: 4(413) = 1652\) kJ \(2\ \ce{Cl-Cl}: 2(239) = 478\) kJ \(2\ \ce{F-F}: 2(154) = 308\) kJ Total required \(= 1652+478+308 = 2438\) kJ Bonds formed (products — reassemble the atoms): \(2\ \ce{C-F}: 2(485) = 970\) kJ \(2\ \ce{C-Cl}: 2(339) = 678\) kJ \(2\ \ce{H-F}: 2(565) = 1130\) kJ \(2\ \ce{H-Cl}: 2(427) = 854\) kJ Total released \(= 970+678+1130+854 = 3632\) kJ \[\Delta H = \text{required} - \text{released} = 2438 - 3632 = \mathbf{-1194}\ \text{kJ per mol \ce{CF2Cl2}}\]

Free-response 15 • long • 8 points

2.00 mol of an ideal gas moves from state A (\(P=2.00\,\mathrm{atm}\), \(V=10.0\,\mathrm{L}\)) to state B (\(P=1.00\,\mathrm{atm}\), \(V=30.0\,\mathrm{L}\)) by two different routes.

Pathway 1 (via point C, \(P=2.00\,\mathrm{atm}\), \(V=30.0\,\mathrm{L}\)): A\(\to\)C at constant pressure, then C\(\to\)B at constant volume. Pathway 2 (via point D, \(P=1.00\,\mathrm{atm}\), \(V=10.0\,\mathrm{L}\)): A\(\to\)D at constant volume, then D\(\to\)B at constant pressure.
  1. Find the total work for each pathway, in joules.
    Work is done only on a constant-pressure leg; a constant-volume leg has \(\Delta V=0\), so \(w=0\) on that leg regardless of how much the pressure changes. Pathway 1: A\(\to\)C at \(P=2.00\) atm: \(w = -(2.00)(30.0-10.0) = -40.0\) L atm. C\(\to\)B: \(w=0\). \[w_{\text{total}} = -40.0\ \text{L\,atm} \times 101.3\ \text{J/L\,atm} = \mathbf{-4.05\times10^3}\ \text{J}\] Pathway 2: A\(\to\)D: \(w=0\). D\(\to\)B at \(P=1.00\) atm: \(w = -(1.00)(30.0-10.0) = -20.0\) L atm. \[w_{\text{total}} = -20.0 \times 101.3 = \mathbf{-2.03\times10^3}\ \text{J}\]
  2. Is work a state function? Explain using your answer above.
    No. A state function depends only on the initial and final states, but here the same states A and B give different total work (\(-4.05\times10^3\) J versus \(-2.03\times10^3\) J) depending on the path taken between them — the defining behaviour of a path function, not a state function.

Free-response 16 • short • 6 points

1 mol of a liquid vaporizes at its boiling point (80.0 °C), 1.00 atm. \(\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 30.7\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\) at that temperature.

Find \(w\) and \(\Delta E\) for the vaporization.
Step 1 — work. The liquid's own volume is negligible next to the gas it becomes, so \(\Delta V \approx V_{\text{gas}} = nRT/P\), and \[w = -P\Delta V \approx -nRT = -(1)(8.314)(353.15) = \mathbf{-2.94}\ \text{kJ}\] Step 2 — first law, using \(q_p = \Delta H_{\text{vap}}\) at constant pressure: \[\Delta E = q + w = 30.7 + (-2.94) = \mathbf{27.8}\ \text{kJ}\]

Most of the heat absorbed goes into internal energy, not into pushing back the atmosphere — consistent with a liquid's own volume being almost nothing compared to the vapor it turns into.

Free-response 17 • long • 7 points

The sun delivers about 1.0 kW/m². A field produces 20. kg of sucrose (\(\ce{C12H22O11}\)) per hour per hectare (1 hectare \(= 10000\,\mathrm{m^2}\)):

\(\ce{12CO2(g) + 11H2O(l) -> C12H22O11(s) + 12O2(g)}\)   \(\Delta H = 5640\,\mathrm{kJ}\)
Find the percentage of incident sunlight energy that ends up stored in the sucrose.
Step 1 — energy incident on 1 hectare in 1 hour: \[E_{\text{in}} = (1.0\ \text{kJ/s per m}^2)(10{,}000\ \text{m}^2) (3600\ \text{s}) = 3.6\times10^7\ \text{kJ}\] Step 2 — energy stored as sucrose. \(20.\) kg \(=2.0\times10^4\) g; \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{C12H22O11})=342.3\) g/mol: \[n = \frac{2.0\times10^4}{342.3} = 58.43\ \text{mol}\] \[E_{\text{stored}} = 58.43 \times 5640 = 3.30\times10^5\ \text{kJ}\] Step 3 — ratio. \[\text{efficiency} = \frac{3.30\times10^5}{3.6\times10^7} \times 100 = \mathbf{0.92\%}\]

A useful number to know: photosynthesis, the process every food chain on Earth ultimately runs on, captures under 1% of the sunlight that hits a leaf.

Free-response 18 • short • 5 points

Solar panels convert sunlight to electricity at 19 % efficiency. A home uses 40. kW·h of electricity per day and receives 8.0 h of useful sunlight (at the 1.0 kW/m² flux from the previous question).

Find the minimum panel area needed to supply the whole home.
Step 1 — incident energy per square metre per day. \[1.0\ \text{kW/m}^2 \times 8.0\ \text{h} = 8.0\ \text{kWh/m}^2\] Step 2 — usable energy, at 19% efficiency: \[8.0 \times 0.19 = 1.52\ \text{kWh/m}^2\] Step 3 — divide the daily need by the usable rate. \[\text{area} = \frac{40.}{1.52} = \mathbf{26}\ \text{m}^2\]

Free-response 19 • long • 10 points

\(\ce{2HNO3(aq) + Na2CO3(s) -> 2NaNO3(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\). Given \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) (kJ/mol): \(\ce{HNO3(aq)}\) \(=-207\); \(\ce{Na2CO3(s)}\) \(=-1131\); \(\ce{NaNO3(aq)}\) \(=-467\); \(\ce{H2O(l)}\) \(=-286\); \(\ce{CO2(g)}\) \(=-393.5\).

About \(2.0\times10^4\) gallons of a 70.0%-by-mass \(\ce{HNO3}\) solution (density 1.42 g/mL) spilled and required neutralization with \(\ce{Na2CO3(s)}\).

  1. Find \(\Delta H^\circ\) for the neutralization, per the equation as written.
    \[\Delta H^\circ = [2(-467) + (-286) + (-393.5)] - [2(-207) + (-1131)]\] \[= -1613.5 - (-1545) = \mathbf{-68.5}\ \text{kJ}\]
  2. Find the mass of \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) required, and the total heat evolved.
    Step 1 — moles of \(\ce{HNO3}\) spilled. \(V = 2.0\times10^4\ \text{gal} \times 3785\ \text{mL/gal} = 7.57\times10^7\) mL \(m(\text{soln}) = 7.57\times10^7 \times 1.42 = 1.0749\times10^8\) g \(m(\ce{HNO3}) = 1.0749\times10^8 \times 0.700 = 7.52\times10^7\) g \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{HNO3}) = 63.02\) g/mol \(\Rightarrow n = \mathbf{1.19\times10^6}\) mol Step 2 — stoichiometry, 2 \(\ce{HNO3}\) : 1 \(\ce{Na2CO3}\): \(n(\ce{Na2CO3}) = 1.19\times10^6/2 = 5.97\times10^5\) mol \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{Na2CO3}) = 106.0\) g/mol \(\Rightarrow m = \mathbf{6.33\times10^7}\) g \(\approx 6.3\times10^4\) kg (about 63 metric tons) Step 3 — heat, using \(\Delta H^\circ\) per mole of \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) (coefficient 1): \[q = (5.97\times10^5)(-68.5) = \mathbf{-4.1\times10^7}\ \text{kJ}\]
  3. Given the size of \(\Delta H^\circ\), what was officials' major concern about air pollution during the cleanup?
    The reaction is strongly exothermic and produces gaseous \(\ce{CO2}\) — adding the base quickly could drive the reaction fast enough to release a large burst of \(\ce{CO2}\) (potentially carrying acid mist with it) into the air rather than a slow, controlled fizz.

Free-response 20 • short • 5 points

A slice of cake contains about 400 (nutritional) Calories (\(1\ \text{Cal} = 1000\ \text{cal} = 4.184\,\mathrm{kJ}\)). A 180-lb person climbs 8-inch steps; potential energy per step is \(PE = mgh\).

How many steps must they climb to expend the 400 Cal?
Step 1 — energy available. \[400\ \text{Cal} \times 4.184\ \text{kJ/Cal} = 1674\ \text{kJ}\] Step 2 — convert mass and height to SI. \(180\ \text{lb} \times 0.4536\ \text{kg/lb} = 81.6\) kg \(8\ \text{in} \times 0.0254\ \text{m/in} = 0.203\) m Step 3 — energy per step. \[PE = mgh = (81.6)(9.81)(0.203) = 163\ \text{J}\] Step 4 — divide. \[\frac{1{,}674{,}000\ \text{J}}{163\ \text{J/step}} \approx \mathbf{1.0\times10^4}\ \text{steps}\]

Roughly ten thousand steps — a reminder of just how much chemical energy a small amount of food actually carries.

Free-response 21 • short • 7 points

\(\Delta H_f^\circ(\ce{H2O(l)}) = -285.8\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\) at 298 K. Find \(\Delta E\) for \(\ce{H2O(l) -> H2(g) + 1/2O2(g)}\) at 298 K, 1 atm.
Step 1 — \(\Delta H\). This is the reverse of the formation reaction: \[\Delta H = -\Delta H_f^\circ = \mathbf{+285.8}\ \text{kJ}\] Step 2 — work. No gas exists on the reactant side; 1.5 mol of gas appears on the product side. For an ideal gas at constant \(T,P\), \(PV=nRT \Rightarrow P\Delta V = \Delta n_{\text{gas}}RT\): \[w = -\Delta n_{\text{gas}}RT = -(1.5)(8.314)(298) = \mathbf{-3.72}\ \text{kJ}\] Step 3 — first law. \[\Delta E = \Delta H + w = 285.8 + (-3.72) = \mathbf{282}\ \text{kJ}\]

Free-response 22 • long • 9 points

1.00 mol of water starts as ice at -30.0 °C and ends as steam at 140. °C. Specific heats: ice 2.03 J/g/°C; water 4.18 J/g/°C; steam 2.02 J/g/°C. \(\Delta H_{\text{fus}} = 6.02\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\) at 0 °C; \(\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 40.7\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\) at 100. °C.

Find \(q\) for the entire process.
Five legs, in order. \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{H2O}) = 18.02\) g, so \(1.00\) mol \(=18.02\) g throughout. 1. Warm ice, \(-30.0\to0\,^\circ\)C: \[q_1 = (18.02)(2.03)(30.0) = 1.10\ \text{kJ}\] 2. Melt at 0 °C: \[q_2 = (1.00)(6.02) = 6.02\ \text{kJ}\] 3. Warm liquid water, \(0\to100.\,^\circ\)C: \[q_3 = (18.02)(4.18)(100.) = 7.53\ \text{kJ}\] 4. Vaporize at 100. °C: \[q_4 = (1.00)(40.7) = 40.7\ \text{kJ}\] 5. Warm steam, \(100.\to140.\,^\circ\)C: \[q_5 = (18.02)(2.02)(40.0) = 1.46\ \text{kJ}\] Total: \[q = 1.10 + 6.02 + 7.53 + 40.7 + 1.46 = \mathbf{56.8}\ \text{kJ}\]

Notice how lopsided this is — the single vaporization step (40.7 kJ) costs almost as much as everything else combined. That is the entire reason steam burns are so much worse than boiling-water burns: steam carries all that extra energy, released the instant it condenses on your skin.

Free-response 23 • long • 6 points

A 500.0 g sample of an unknown element at 195 °C is dropped into an ice–water mixture; 109.5 g of ice melts, and an ice–water mixture remains (so the final temperature is 0 °C). \(\Delta H_{\text{fus}}(\ce{H2O}) = 6.02\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}\).
Step 1 — heat absorbed by the melting ice equals the heat released by the cooling element (an ice–water mixture staying put at 0 °C is what pins the final temperature). \[n(\text{ice melted}) = 109.5/18.02 = 6.077\ \text{mol}\] \[q = (6.077)(6.02\ \text{kJ/mol}) = 36.6\ \text{kJ} = 3.66\times10^4\ \text{J}\] Step 2 — specific heat of the element, cooling from 195 °C to 0 °C: \[q = mc\Delta T \Rightarrow c = \frac{3.66\times10^4}{(500.0)(195)} = \mathbf{0.375}\ \text{J/(°C\,g)}\]

This is close to copper's specific heat (0.385 J/g/°C) — consistent with the sample being copper.

Free-response 24 • long • 9 points

88.0 g of \(\ce{N2O(g)}\) cools from 165 °C to 55 °C at a constant pressure of 5.00 atm. The molar heat capacity of \(\ce{N2O(g)}\) is 38.7 J/°C/mol.

Find \(q\), \(w\), \(\Delta E\), and \(\Delta H\) for this process.
Step 1 — moles. \[\mathcal{M}(\ce{N2O}) = 2(14.01)+16.00 = 44.02\ \text{g/mol} \Rightarrow n = \frac{88.0}{44.02} = 2.00\ \text{mol}\] Step 2 — \(q\) and \(\Delta H\), at constant pressure \(q_p=\Delta H\): \[q = \Delta H = nC_p\Delta T = (2.00)(38.7)(55-165) = \mathbf{-8.51\times10^3}\ \text{J} = -8.51\ \text{kJ}\]

Negative, as expected for a gas being cooled.

Step 3 — \(w\) [beyond CED]. At constant \(P,n\): \(\Delta V = nR\Delta T/P\), so \[w = -P\Delta V = -nR\Delta T = -(2.00)(8.314)(-110) = \mathbf{+1.83\times10^3}\ \text{J}\]

Positive: as the gas cools it contracts, so the surroundings do work on it.

Step 4 — \(\Delta E\) [beyond CED]. \[\Delta E = q + w = -8.51\times10^3 + 1.83\times10^3 = \mathbf{-6.68\times10^3}\ \text{J} = -6.68\ \text{kJ}\]
