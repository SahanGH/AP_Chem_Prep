Unit 6 • Thermochemistry

Self-Study • CED 6.1–6.9, I do / You do

Ten ladders • four YOUR TURN questions each • work alone, check after all four

How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a worked example, then four YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four before checking anything.

The habit that decides this unit: track the sign, every time. Energy leaving the system is negative; energy entering is positive. Most lost marks here are not arithmetic — they are a correct number with the wrong sign, or a \(\Delta T\) taken for the wrong substance. Two constants used throughout. \(c(\ce{H2O}) = 4.18\,\mathrm{J/g/{}^\circ C}\), \(\Delta H_{\text{fus}} = 334\,\mathrm{J/g}\) and \(\Delta H_{\text{vap}} = 2260\,\mathrm{J/g}\). One scope note. Topics CED 6.6 and CED 6.9 carry a CED exclusion covering the formal enthalpy-versus-internal-energy distinction and state-function theory. Hess's law itself is fully examinable; the thermodynamic formalism behind it is not.

Ladder 1 • Endothermic and exothermic

CED 6.1

Define the system (the reaction) and the surroundings (everything else, including the beaker and the thermometer). Then ask which way energy flowed.

I do: reading a temperature change backwards

A beaker becomes cold when two solids are mixed. State the sign of \(\Delta H\) and explain the flow.

The surroundings got colder, so the surroundings lost energy. That energy went into the system. The system absorbed energy, so the process is endothermic and \(\Delta H\) is positive. The reversal that trips people: what you feel is the surroundings, but the sign refers to the system. A cold beaker means a positive \(\Delta H\); a hot beaker means a negative one. What never to write: “the reaction gives off cold”. Cold is not a substance. Energy flowed one way only — into the system.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. Combustion warms its surroundings. Sign of \(\Delta H\)?
    negative
  2. \(\ce{NH4NO3}\) dissolving makes the beaker cold. Sign?
    positive
  3. In an exothermic reaction, do the surroundings gain or lose energy?
    gain
  4. Melting ice is endothermic. Explain in terms of the system.
    the ice absorbs energy to overcome attractions

(a) negative   (b) positive   (c) gain   (d) the ice absorbs energy to overcome attractions

(a) Negative — the system released energy to the surroundings. (b) Positive — the system absorbed energy from the surroundings, which therefore cooled. (c) They gain energy, which is why they warm up. (d) The ice (the system) must absorb energy to break the hydrogen bonds holding its lattice together. That energy comes from the surroundings, which is why your hand feels cold holding it: your hand is the surroundings, losing energy.

Ladder 2 • Energy diagrams

CED 6.2

The vertical axis is energy. Where the products sit relative to the reactants gives the sign of \(\Delta H\); the hump between them is the activation energy, which says nothing about \(\Delta H\).

I do: reading both quantities off one picture

A diagram shows reactants at 60 kJ, a peak at 150 kJ and products at 25 kJ. Find \(E_a\) and \(\Delta H\), and classify the reaction.

\(E_a\) (forward): \(150 - 60 = \mathbf{90~kJ}\). \(\Delta H\): products minus reactants, \(25 - 60 = \mathbf{-35~kJ}\). Negative, so exothermic. The visual signature: products below reactants means exothermic; above means endothermic. Read the two ends, never the peak, for \(\Delta H\). Why the peak is irrelevant to \(\Delta H\): a catalyst lowers the peak without moving either end, so it changes \(E_a\) and leaves \(\Delta H\) untouched. The two quantities are independent.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Reactants 40 kJ, peak 130 kJ, products 95 kJ. \(E_a\)?
  2. \(\Delta H\) for that reaction, and its classification?
    \(+55\) kJ, endothermic
  3. On an exothermic diagram, where do the products sit?
    below the reactants
  4. Does a large \(E_a\) mean a large \(\Delta H\)? Explain.
    no — they are independent

(a) 90 kJ   (b) \(+55\) kJ, endothermic   (c) below the reactants   (d) no — they are independent

(a) \(130 - 40 = \mathbf{90}\) kJ. (b) \(95 - 40 = \mathbf{+55}\) kJ, so endothermic. (c) Below the reactants — the system ended at lower energy, having released the difference. (d) No. \(E_a\) is the height of the barrier between reactants and products; \(\Delta H\) is the difference between the two ends. A reaction can have an enormous barrier and a tiny \(\Delta H\), or a small barrier and a huge \(\Delta H\). Adding a catalyst proves the point: it changes \(E_a\) and leaves \(\Delta H\) exactly as it was.

Ladder 3 • Heat transfer and thermal equilibrium

CED 6.3

Energy flows from hotter to cooler until both reach the same temperature. Same temperature does not mean same energy.

I do: what equalizes and what does not

Two blocks at different temperatures are placed in contact and isolated. Describe what happens at the particle level, and state what is equal at equilibrium.

At the boundary: particles of the hotter block have greater average kinetic energy. They collide with particles of the cooler block and transfer energy to them. Over time: the hotter block's average kinetic energy falls, the cooler block's rises, until the two averages match. Collisions continue, but with no further net transfer — the flow is now equal in both directions. What is equal at equilibrium: the temperature, meaning the average kinetic energy per particle. What is not equal: the total thermal energy. That depends on mass and specific heat capacity too. A large block of water at 25 °C holds far more energy than a small block of copper at the same temperature.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. Which direction does energy flow between objects at different temperatures?
    hotter to cooler
  2. At thermal equilibrium, what quantity is equal?
    temperature
  3. Do the two objects then hold equal thermal energy? Explain.
    no — mass and \(c\) differ
  4. Do collisions stop at thermal equilibrium?
    no — net transfer stops, not the collisions

(a) hotter to cooler   (b) temperature   (c) no — mass and \(c\) differ   (d) no — net transfer stops, not the collisions

(a) From hotter to cooler, always. (b) The temperature — the average kinetic energy per particle. (c) No. Total thermal energy depends on mass and specific heat capacity as well as temperature. Two objects at the same temperature can hold vastly different amounts. (d) No. The particles keep colliding and keep exchanging energy. What stops is the net flow: transfer in each direction becomes equal, so nothing changes on the macroscopic scale.

Ladder 4 • Specific heat

CED 6.4 \(q = mc\,\Delta T\). Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise one gram by one degree — a property of the substance, not of the sample.
I do: energy to warm water

How much energy warms 125 g of water from 20.0 °C to 45.0 °C?

\[ q = mc\,\Delta T = (125)(4.18)(25.0) = \mathbf{1.31\times10^{4}~J} = 13.1\,\mathrm{kJ} \] \(\Delta T\) is final minus initial, and its size in °C equals its size in kelvin — so either unit works here, as long as you use a difference. Why water's value is so large. At 4.18 J/g/°C, water takes far more energy per degree than most substances (copper is 0.385). Its hydrogen-bond network absorbs energy without the molecules speeding up much — which is why water moderates climate and makes a good coolant.
YOUR TURN 4 — four questions
  1. Energy to warm 50.0 g of water by 30.0 °C:
  2. 2500 J into 100.0 g of water. Temperature rise?
  3. 1200 J raises a metal (\(c = 0.900\,\mathrm{J/g/{}^\circ C}\)) by 15.0 °C. Mass?
  4. Equal masses of water and copper absorb the same energy. Which warms more, and why?
    copper — much smaller \(c\)

(a) 6270 J   (b) 5.98 °C   (c) 88.9 g   (d) copper — much smaller \(c\)

(a) \((50.0)(4.18)(30.0) = \mathbf{6270}\) J. (b) \(\Delta T = q/(mc) = 2500/[(100.0)(4.18)] = \mathbf{5.98}\) °C. (c) \(m = q/(c\,\Delta T) = 1200/[(0.900)(15.0)] = \mathbf{88.9}\) g. (d) Copper, by roughly a factor of eleven. Its specific heat capacity (0.385) is far smaller than water's (4.18), so the same energy per gram produces a much larger temperature change.

Ladder 5 • Calorimetry

CED 6.4 CED 6.6

In a coffee-cup calorimeter the solution absorbs the heat, so \(q\) is computed for the solution — and the reaction's \(\Delta H\) is the opposite sign, per mole.

I do: a neutralization

50.0 mL of 1.00 M \(\ce{HCl}\) and 50.0 mL of 1.00 M \(\ce{NaOH}\), both at 21.0 °C, are mixed. The temperature peaks at 28.2 °C. Find \(\Delta H\) per mole of water formed.

Mass of solution: \(50.0 + 50.0 = 100.0\,\mathrm{g}\) (assuming the density of water). Total volume — not one of them. Heat absorbed by the solution: \[ q = (100.0)(4.18)(7.2) = 3.0e3\,\mathrm{J} = 3.01\,\mathrm{kJ} \] Moles of water formed: \((0.0500)(1.00) = 0.0500\) mol — both reactants supply exactly this, so neither is in excess. Per mole, with the sign flipped: \[ \Delta H = -\frac{3.01}{0.0500} = \mathbf{-60.2~kJ/mol} \] Negative because the solution gained what the reaction released. Two traps: using 50.0 g instead of 100.0 g, and forgetting to flip the sign.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. Why is the total mass of solution used, not one reactant's?
    the whole solution absorbs the heat
  2. If the same experiment used 100 mL of each, would \(\Delta T\) change? Would \(\Delta H\) per mole?
    \(\Delta T\) about the same; \(\Delta H\) per mole unchanged
  3. Why is \(\Delta H\) negative when the temperature rose?
    the system released it
  4. Give one reason the measured magnitude is usually too small.
    heat lost to the surroundings

(a) the whole solution absorbs the heat   (b) \(\Delta T\) about the same; \(\Delta H\) per mole unchanged   (c) the system released it   (d) heat lost to the surroundings

(a) Because the heat released spreads through all the liquid present. The whole mixed solution is what warms up, so its whole mass appears in \(q = mc\,\Delta T\). (b) \(\Delta T\) would be essentially unchanged: twice the heat is released, but it warms twice as much solution. \(\Delta H\) per mole is also unchanged — it is an intensive property of the reaction, not of how much was run. (c) Because the sign convention refers to the system. The solution (surroundings) gained energy, so the reaction released it, making \(\Delta H\) negative. (d) Heat is lost to the surroundings — the cup, the thermometer, the air — so the measured temperature rise, and hence the computed \(q\), is smaller than the true value. (Accept also: the heat capacity of the calorimeter itself is neglected.)

Ladder 6 • Energy of phase changes

CED 6.5

A phase change uses \(q = m\,\Delta H_{\text{phase}}\) — mass only, no \(\Delta T\), because the temperature does not change.

I do: ice to steam in three steps

Find the energy to convert 40.0 g of ice at 0 °C into steam at 100 °C.

Step 1 — melt (no \(\Delta T\)): \(q = (40.0)(334) = 13.4\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Step 2 — warm the liquid (this one has a \(\Delta T\)): \(q = (40.0)(4.18)(100) = 16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Step 3 — vaporize (no \(\Delta T\)): \(q = (40.0)(2260) = 90.4\,\mathrm{kJ}\). \[ \text{Total} = 13.4 + 16.7 + 90.4 = \mathbf{120~kJ} \] Notice which step dominates. Vaporizing takes more than the other two combined, because melting only loosens the lattice while vaporizing must separate the molecules completely — overcoming essentially all the hydrogen bonding. The structural error to avoid: putting a \(\Delta T\) into a phase-change step. During melting or boiling the temperature is constant, so there is no \(\Delta T\) to use.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. Energy to melt 60.0 g of ice at 0 °C:
  2. Energy to vaporize 10.0 g of water at 100 °C:
  3. Why does a phase-change calculation use no \(\Delta T\)?
    temperature is constant during the change
  4. Why is \(\Delta H_{\text{vap}}\) so much larger than \(\Delta H_{\text{fus}}\)?
    vaporizing separates molecules completely

(a) 20.0 kJ   (b) 22.6 kJ   (c) temperature is constant during the change   (d) vaporizing separates molecules completely

(a) \((60.0)(334) = 20040\,\mathrm{J} = \mathbf{20.0}\) kJ. (b) \((10.0)(2260) = 22600\,\mathrm{J} = \mathbf{22.6}\) kJ. (c) Because the temperature does not change during a phase transition — all the supplied energy goes into overcoming intermolecular attractions (potential energy) rather than into speeding the particles up. (d) Melting only loosens the structure: molecules leave fixed positions but remain in contact, and most attractions survive. Vaporizing must separate them completely, overcoming essentially all of the remaining attractions and moving the molecules far apart. For water that is nearly seven times as much energy per gram.

Ladder 7 • Hess's law

CED 6.9

Enthalpy changes add. Reverse a reaction and the sign flips; multiply a reaction and \(\Delta H\) multiplies too.

I do: manipulating to reach a target

Given \(\ce{C(s) + O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\), \(\Delta H = -393.5\) kJ, and \(\ce{CO(g) + 1/2O2(g) -> CO2(g)}\), \(\Delta H = -283.0\) kJ, find \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{C(s) + 1/2O2(g) -> CO(g)}\).

Target check: \(\ce{CO}\) must end on the right, but in the second equation it is on the left. So reverse the second and flip its sign: \[ \ce{CO2(g) -> CO(g) + 1/2O2(g)} \qquad \Delta H = +283.0~\text{kJ} \] Add to the first: \(\ce{C + O2 + CO2 -> CO2 + CO + 1/2O2}\). The \(\ce{CO2}\) cancels and the oxygens net to \(\ce{1/2 O2}\) on the left. \[ \Delta H = (-393.5) + (+283.0) = \mathbf{-110.5~kJ} \] Why it works: enthalpy depends only on the initial and final states, not the route. Any sequence of steps connecting the same start and finish must sum to the same total — which is what lets us obtain a value for a reaction too difficult to measure directly, as this one is.
YOUR TURN 7 — four questions
  1. If \(\ce{A -> B}\) has \(\Delta H = -50\) kJ, what is it for \(\ce{B -> A}\)?
  2. If \(\ce{A -> B}\) has \(\Delta H = -50\) kJ, what is it for \(\ce{2A -> 2B}\)?
  3. Why can enthalpy changes be added at all?
    enthalpy depends only on the states
  4. Why is Hess's law useful in practice?
    it gives values that cannot be measured directly

(a) \(+50\) kJ   (b) \(-100\) kJ   (c) enthalpy depends only on the states   (d) it gives values that cannot be measured directly

(a) \(\mathbf{+50}\) kJ — reversing flips the sign. (b) \(\mathbf{-100}\) kJ — doubling the amounts doubles the energy. (c) Because enthalpy depends only on the current state of the system, not on how it got there. The difference between reactants and products is therefore fixed, so any path between them sums to the same total. (d) It supplies \(\Delta H\) for reactions that cannot be run cleanly in a calorimeter. The example above is exactly such a case: burning carbon to give only \(\ce{CO}\) is not achievable in practice — some \(\ce{CO2}\) always forms — so the value must be obtained indirectly.

Ladder 8 • Bond enthalpies

CED 6.7

Breaking bonds costs energy; forming them releases it. \[ \Delta H \approx \sum(\text{bonds broken}) - \sum(\text{bonds formed}) \]

I do: a simple gas-phase reaction

Estimate \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}\) from \(\ce{H-H}\) 436, \(\ce{Cl-Cl}\) 243, \(\ce{H-Cl}\) 431 kJ/mol.

Broken (energy in): \(436 + 243 = 679\) kJ. Formed (energy out): \(2 \times 431 = 862\) kJ. \[ \Delta H = 679 - 862 = \mathbf{-183~kJ} \]

Negative, so exothermic — more energy is released making the new bonds than was needed to break the old ones. The accepted value is -185 kJ, so the estimate is good to about 1 %.

Why only an estimate: tabulated bond enthalpies are averages across many different compounds. A particular \(\ce{H-Cl}\) bond in a particular molecule differs slightly from the average. The method also assumes every species is a gas. Direction of the subtraction: broken minus formed. Reversing it gives the right magnitude with the wrong sign, which is a whole-question error.
YOUR TURN 8 — four questions
  1. Is breaking a bond endothermic or exothermic?
    endothermic
  2. If bonds broken total 800 kJ and formed 950 kJ, find \(\Delta H\).
  3. Why is a bond-enthalpy result only approximate?
    tabulated values are averages
  4. Why does the method require all species to be gases?
    otherwise intermolecular forces contribute too

(a) endothermic   (b) \(-150\) kJ   (c) tabulated values are averages   (d) otherwise intermolecular forces contribute too

(a) Endothermic — energy must be supplied to pull bonded atoms apart. Forming bonds is exothermic. (b) \(800 - 950 = \mathbf{-150}\) kJ, exothermic. (c) Because tabulated bond enthalpies are averages taken over many compounds. The true strength of a given bond depends on its molecular environment, so a total assembled from averages will not match the exact value. (d) Because the method accounts only for making and breaking covalent bonds within molecules. If a species were a liquid or solid, intermolecular forces would also have to be overcome or formed, and those are not included in the bond-enthalpy bookkeeping.

Ladder 9 • Enthalpy of formation

CED 6.8 \[ \Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants}) \] An element in its standard state has \(\Delta H_f^\circ = 0\).
I do: a combustion from formation data

Find \(\Delta H^\circ\) for \(\ce{C2H4(g) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}\) using \(\Delta H_f^\circ\): \(\ce{C2H4}\) \(+52.3\), \(\ce{CO2}\) \(-393.5\), \(\ce{H2O(l)}\) \(-285.8\) kJ/mol.

Products, with coefficients applied: \(2(-393.5) + 2(-285.8) = -787.0 - 571.6 = -1358.6\). Reactants: \((+52.3) + 3(0) = 52.3\). Oxygen is an element in its standard state, so its value is zero — it is not omitted, it is genuinely zero. \[ \Delta H^\circ = -1358.6 - 52.3 = \mathbf{-1411~kJ} \] Two things to get right: apply the coefficients (a missed factor of 2 here changes the answer by hundreds of kilojoules), and subtract in the right direction — products minus reactants.
YOUR TURN 9 — four questions
  1. What is \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) for \(\ce{N2(g)}\)? Why?
    zero — element in its standard state
  2. What is \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) for \(\ce{O3(g)}\), and why is it not zero?
    non-zero; \(\ce{O2}\) is the standard state, not \(\ce{O3}\)
  3. Products total \(-500\), reactants \(-120\) kJ. Find \(\Delta H^\circ\).
  4. Why must the coefficients be applied to the tabulated values?
    the table is per mole

(a) zero — element in its standard state   (b) non-zero; \(\ce{O2}\) is the standard state, not \(\ce{O3}\)   (c) \(-380\) kJ   (d) the table is per mole

(a) Zero, because \(\ce{N2}\)(g) is the standard state of the element nitrogen, and forming an element from itself involves no change. (b) Not zero (it is about \(+143\) kJ/mol). Ozone is not the standard state of oxygen — \(\ce{O2}\) is — so making \(\ce{O3}\) from \(\ce{O2}\) is a real chemical change with a real enthalpy. (c) \(-500 - (-120) = \mathbf{-380}\) kJ. (d) Because \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) is tabulated per mole of substance. If the equation makes two moles, twice that enthalpy is involved. Omitting a coefficient is the commonest error in this calculation.

Ladder 10 • Choosing the right method

CED 6.6 CED 6.7 CED 6.8 CED 6.9

Three routes give \(\Delta H\). Knowing which one the data are asking for is half the skill.

I do: matching data to method

State which method to use for each set of data.

Given a temperature change in a calorimeter: use \(q = mc\,\Delta T\), then divide by moles and flip the sign. This is the only experimental route. Given a table of \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) values: use products minus reactants. Most accurate of the three, and the usual exam default. Given a table of bond enthalpies: use broken minus formed. Only an estimate, and only for gases. Given other reactions with their \(\Delta H\) values: use Hess's law — reverse and scale until they add to the target. The tell: the data given is the instruction. Temperatures mean calorimetry; a \(\Delta H_f^\circ\) table means the formation formula; bonds mean the estimate; whole reactions mean Hess.
YOUR TURN 10 — four questions
  1. You are given the mass, specific heat and temperature change. Which method?
    calorimetry
  2. You are given two reactions with known \(\Delta H\). Which method?
    Hess's law
  3. Which method gives only an estimate, and why?
    bond enthalpies — averaged values
  4. Which method is the only truly experimental one?
    calorimetry

(a) calorimetry   (b) Hess's law   (c) bond enthalpies — averaged values   (d) calorimetry

(a) Calorimetry: \(q = mc\,\Delta T\), then per mole with the sign reversed. (b) Hess's law — reverse and scale them until they sum to the target reaction. (c) Bond enthalpies, because the tabulated values are averages across many compounds rather than values for the specific molecules in question. It also assumes all species are gaseous. (d) Calorimetry. The other three all begin from tabulated data that someone else measured; calorimetry is the measurement itself.
