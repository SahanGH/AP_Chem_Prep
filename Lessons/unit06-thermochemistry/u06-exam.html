Unit 6 • Thermochemistry

Unit Exam

CED 6.1–6.9 • Fri Dec 11 • 48 minutes • 28 points

Format

Section I: 20 multiple choice, no calculator, 30 minutes. Section II: two short free response, calculator permitted, 18 minutes. Constants are provided with Section II.

Section I • Multiple Choice 20 questions • 30 minutes • 1 point each

1. A reaction is run in water and the water's temperature rises. The reaction is
  1. exothermic, with \(\Delta H < 0\)
  2. endothermic, with \(\Delta H > 0\)
  3. exothermic, with \(\Delta H > 0\)
  4. at thermal equilibrium
2. For an endothermic process, \(q_{\text{system}}\) is
  1. positive, and the surroundings cool
  2. positive, and the surroundings warm
  3. negative, and the surroundings cool
  4. negative, and the surroundings warm
3. On an energy diagram, products drawn above reactants indicates
  1. an exothermic reaction
  2. an endothermic reaction
  3. a fast reaction
  4. a slow reaction
4. The height of the barrier on an energy diagram affects
  1. the rate but not \(\Delta H\)
  2. \(\Delta H\) but not the rate
  3. both the rate and \(\Delta H\)
  4. neither
5. Two objects at different temperatures are placed in contact. Energy flows
  1. from the hotter to the cooler until both reach the same temperature
  2. from the cooler to the hotter
  3. in whichever direction the larger object dictates
  4. only if they are the same material
6. At thermal equilibrium,
  1. energy is still exchanged, but at equal rates in both directions
  2. all molecular motion stops
  3. energy exchange ceases entirely
  4. the two objects must have equal internal energy
7. In the expression \(q = mc\Delta T\) applied to a coffee-cup calorimeter, \(m\) and \(c\) refer to
  1. the water
  2. the reactants
  3. the calorimeter cup
  4. the limiting reactant only
8. Water's specific heat is unusually large mainly because of
  1. hydrogen bonding
  2. its low molar mass
  3. its bent shape
  4. its high boiling point
9. Heating 50.0 g of water by 60.0 °C requires about
  1. 1.25 kJ
  2. 12.5 kJ
  3. 125 kJ
  4. 250 kJ
10. A calorimetry experiment gives \(q_{\text{water}} = +2170\) J with 0.0250 mol reacting. Then \(\Delta H\) is about
  1. \(+86.9\) kJ/mol
  2. \(-86.9\) kJ/mol
  3. \(-2.17\) kJ/mol
  4. \(-54.3\) kJ/mol
11. Which expression gives the energy to melt a sample at its melting point?
  1. \(mc\Delta T\)
  2. \(n\Delta H_{\text{fus}}\)
  3. \(n\Delta H_{\text{vap}}\)
  4. \(mc\Delta T + n\Delta H_{\text{fus}}\)
12. During boiling, the temperature stays constant because the added energy
  1. overcomes intermolecular forces rather than increasing kinetic energy
  2. is lost to the surroundings
  3. breaks covalent bonds within the molecules
  4. is converted into pressure
13. For water, \(\Delta H_{\text{vap}}\) compared with \(\Delta H_{\text{fus}}\) is
  1. much larger, because vaporizing separates the molecules entirely
  2. much smaller
  3. about equal
  4. larger only above 100 °C
14. For \(\ce{2CO + O2 -> 2CO2}\), \(\Delta H = -566\) kJ. For \(\ce{2CO2 -> 2CO + O2}\), \(\Delta H\) is
  1. \(-566\) kJ
  2. \(+566\) kJ
  3. \(-283\) kJ
  4. \(+283\) kJ
15. Doubling every coefficient in a thermochemical equation
  1. doubles \(\Delta H\)
  2. leaves \(\Delta H\) unchanged
  3. halves \(\Delta H\)
  4. changes the sign of \(\Delta H\)
16. Hess's law is valid because enthalpy is
  1. a state function
  2. always negative
  3. independent of temperature
  4. measured in kilojoules
17. The enthalpy of formation of \(\ce{O2(g)}\) at standard conditions is
  1. zero, by definition
  2. \(-286\) kJ/mol
  3. \(+495\) kJ/mol
  4. not defined
18. \(\Delta H\) from bond enthalpies is calculated as
  1. bonds broken minus bonds formed
  2. bonds formed minus bonds broken
  3. products minus reactants
  4. the sum of all bond energies
19. A bond-enthalpy estimate and a formation-enthalpy calculation for the same reaction differ by about 20 kJ. The best explanation is that
  1. bond enthalpies are averages that ignore molecular environment
  2. one of the calculations must contain an arithmetic error
  3. the reaction is not at standard conditions
  4. bond enthalpies apply only to gases
20. For \(\ce{H2 + Cl2 -> 2HCl}\) with \(D\)(H–H) \(=432\), \(D\)(Cl–Cl) \(=239\), \(D\)(H–Cl) \(=427\) kJ/mol, \(\Delta H\) is
  1. \(-183\) kJ
  2. \(+183\) kJ
  3. \(-244\) kJ
  4. \(+854\) kJ

Free-response 1 • short • 4 points

A 0.0400 mol sample of methane is completely burned in a calorimeter containing 500.0 g of water. The water's temperature rises from 21.00 °C to 38.05 °C.

  1. Calculate the heat absorbed by the water.
  2. Calculate \(\Delta H\) of combustion in kJ/mol.
  3. The accepted value is \(-890.5\) kJ/mol. Give one reason a real calorimetry experiment usually yields a magnitude that is too small.

Free-response 2 • short • 4 points

  1. Calculate the energy needed to convert 36.0 g of ice at 0 °C into liquid water at 25.0 °C.
  2. Explain why the calculation needs two separate expressions.
  3. Given \(\ce{C(gr) + O2 -> CO2}\), \(\Delta H = -393.5\) kJ, and \(\ce{CO + \tfrac{1}{2}O2 -> CO2}\), \(\Delta H = -283.0\) kJ, find \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{C(gr) + \tfrac{1}{2}O2 -> CO}\).
