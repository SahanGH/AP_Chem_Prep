INSTRUCTION A • Two models, one of which AP uses 25 min

Proton donors and acceptors CED 8.1

SP 1
  • Arrhenius: an acid produces \(\ce{H+}\) in water; a base produces \(\ce{OH-}\). Historically important, but it applies only to aqueous solutions.
  • Brønsted–Lowry: an acid is a proton donor; a base is a proton acceptor. This is the model the CED uses.

Water acts as a base because its oxygen carries unshared electron pairs, either of which can bond to a proton. So \(\ce{H+}\) in solution is really hydronium, \(\ce{H3O+}\); writing \(\ce{H+}\) is accepted shorthand.

Conjugate pairs CED 8.1

Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction contains two pairs differing by one proton: \[ \ce{HC2H3O2 + H2O <=> H3O+ + C2H3O2^-} \] \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\)/\(\ce{C2H3O2^-}\) is one pair; \(\ce{H2O}\)/\(\ce{H3O+}\) is the other.

AP trap
The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base. \(\ce{HCl}\) is strong, so \(\ce{Cl-}\) is such a poor base it is effectively inert in water. \(\ce{HCN}\) is very weak, so \(\ce{CN-}\) is a base strong enough to matter.

Quantitatively, \(K_a \times K_b\) equals \(K_w\) for a conjugate pair — adding the two reactions gives the autoionization of water.

GUIDED PRACTICE • Identifying pairs 15 min

  1. Conjugate base of \(\ce{HNO2}\): \(\ce{NO2^-}\)
  2. Conjugate acid of \(\ce{NH3}\): \(\ce{NH4+}\)
  3. Conjugate base of \(\ce{H2PO4^-}\): \(\ce{HPO4^2-}\)
  4. Conjugate acid of \(\ce{H2O}\): \(\ce{H3O+}\)
  5. Rank as bases, weakest first: \(\ce{Cl-}\), \(\ce{F-}\), \(\ce{CN-}\) \(\ce{Cl-}\) \(<\) \(\ce{F-}\) \(<\) \(\ce{CN-}\)

APPLICATION • Strength and \(K_a\) 20 min

  1. A larger \(K_a\) means what about the acid, and about its conjugate base?
  2. Given \(K_a(\ce{HC2H3O2}) = 1.8\times10^{-5}\), find \(K_b\) for acetate.
    \(K_b = K_w/K_a = 1.0\times10^{-14}/1.8\times10^{-5} = \mathbf{5.6\times10^{-10}}\)
Exit ticket
Explain why \(\ce{Cl-}\) does not act as a base in water but \(\ce{F-}\) does.
\(\ce{HCl}\) is a strong acid, so \(\ce{Cl-}\) has essentially no affinity for protons and does not remove them from water. \(\ce{HF}\) is weak, so \(\ce{F-}\) readily accepts a proton from water, generating \(\ce{OH-}\) and making the solution basic.

pH, pOH, and Strong Acids and Bases CED 8.2 • Zumdahl §14.3–14.4, 14.6

By the end you can…
  • Convert among \([\ce{H+}]\), \([\ce{OH-}]\), pH and pOH.
  • Calculate the pH of strong acid and strong base solutions.

Read: Zumdahl §14.3–14.4, 14.6 • PDF pp. 705–724

Retrieval warm-up
  1. \(K_aK_b = \) \(K_w\)
  2. Conjugate base of \(\ce{HF}\): \(\ce{F-}\)
  3. \(K_w\) at 25 °C: \(1.0\times10^{-14}\)

INSTRUCTION A • The logarithmic scale 25 min

pH, pOH, and \(K_w\) CED 8.2

SP 5 \[ \mathrm{pH} = -\log[\ce{H+}] \qquad \mathrm{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}] \qquad \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14.00 \]

Each pH unit is a factor of 10 in \([\ce{H+}]\), so pH 3 is 1000 times more acidic than pH 6.

AP trap
Neutral means \([\ce{H+}] = [\ce{OH-}]\), not pH 7. \(K_w\) rises with temperature, so pure water at 50 °C has a pH below 7 and is still perfectly neutral. The number 7 applies only at 25 °C.

INSTRUCTION B • When ionization is complete 20 min

Strong acids and bases CED 8.2

SP 5

For a strong acid, ionization is complete, so \([\ce{H+}]\) simply equals the acid concentration. No equilibrium calculation is needed.

There are only six strong acids, worth memorizing outright: \[ \ce{HCl} \quad \ce{HBr} \quad \ce{HI} \quad \ce{HNO3} \quad \ce{H2SO4} \quad \ce{HClO4} \] Note that \(\ce{HF}\) is not among them. The strong bases are the Group 1 hydroxides and \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\), \(\ce{Sr(OH)2}\), \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\).

Worked example 1: the factor everyone forgets

0.010 M \(\ce{HCl}\): \([\ce{H+}] = 0.010\), so \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{2.00}\).

0.010 M \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\) produces two hydroxides per formula unit: \[ [\ce{OH-}] = 2(0.010) = 0.020 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{pOH} = 1.70,\ \mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{12.30} \] — not 12.00. Always check the formula for how many ions are released.

APPLICATION • Strong acid and base calculations 20 min

  1. pH of 0.0025 M \(\ce{HNO3}\): 2.60
  2. pH of 0.050 M \(\ce{KOH}\): 12.70
  3. pH of 0.0050 M \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\):
    \([\ce{OH-}] = 2(0.0050) = 0.010\); pOH \(= 2.00\); pH \(= \mathbf{12.00}\)
  4. A student reports pH 12.00 for problem 3 by using \([\ce{OH-}] = 0.0050\). Do they get the right answer by the right method?
Exit ticket
Pure water at 50 °C has pH 6.6. Is it acidic? Explain.
No, it is neutral. \(K_w\) increases with temperature, so both \([\ce{H+}]\) and \([\ce{OH-}]\) rise and the pH falls below 7 — but they remain equal, which is what neutrality means.

Retrieval warm-up

  2. pH of 0.010 M \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\): 12.30
  3. ICE stands for: Initial, Change, Equilibrium

INSTRUCTION A • A weak acid problem is a Unit 7 problem 25 min

\(K_a\) and the ICE table CED 8.3

SP 5 \[ \ce{HA(aq) <=> H+(aq) + A^-(aq)} \qquad K_a = \frac{[\ce{H+}][\ce{A^-}]}{[\ce{HA}]} \]

Because \(K_a\) is small, \(x\) is usually negligible against \(C_0\): \[ K_a \approx \frac{x^2}{C_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = [\ce{H+}] = \sqrt{K_aC_0} \]

AP trap
The 5% check is part of the answer. Compute \(x/C_0\) as a percentage and compare with 5%. An FRQ that asks you to justify the approximation wants the number, not the assertion — exactly as in Unit 7.
Worked example 2: strong is not concentrated

0.10 M acetic acid, \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\): \[ x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.10)} = 1.34\times10^{-3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{2.87} \] Check: \(1.34\times10^{-3}/0.10 = 1.3\%\) ✓

Compare with 0.10 M \(\ce{HCl}\), which has pH 1.00. Same concentration; the difference is entirely strength — the fraction that ionizes. “Strong” and “concentrated” are different words describing different things.

APPLICATION • Weak acid calculations 20 min

  1. pH of 0.250 M \(\ce{HF}\) (\(K_a = 7.2\times10^{-4}\)), with the check.
    \(x = \sqrt{(7.2\times10^{-4})(0.250)} = 1.34\times10^{-2}\); pH \(= \mathbf{1.87}\). Check: \(5.4\%\) — marginally above 5%, so strictly the quadratic should be used
  2. Percent ionization of 0.100 M acetic acid: 1.3%
  3. Diluting a weak acid raises the percent ionization but also raises the pH. Explain how both can be true.
Exit ticket
Two solutions both have 0.10 M concentration: one \(\ce{HCl}\), one acetic acid. Which is more concentrated, and which is more acidic?
They are equally concentrated — that is what 0.10 M means. The \(\ce{HCl}\) is far more acidic, pH 1.00 against 2.87, because it ionizes completely while acetic acid ionizes only about 1.3%.

Weak Bases and Polyprotic Acids CED 8.3 • Zumdahl §14.6–14.7

  • Explain why the first ionization dominates for a polyprotic acid.

Read: Zumdahl §14.6–14.7 • PDF pp. 719–730

Retrieval warm-up
  1. \([\ce{H+}]\) for a weak acid: \(\sqrt{K_aC_0}\)
  2. The approximation threshold: 5%
  3. pH of 0.10 M acetic acid: 2.87

INSTRUCTION A • The same table, one extra step 25 min

Weak bases and \(K_b\) CED 8.3

SP 5 \[ \ce{B(aq) + H2O(l) <=> BH+(aq) + OH^-(aq)} \qquad K_b = \frac{[\ce{BH+}][\ce{OH^-}]}{[\ce{B}]} \]
AP trap
For a base, the ICE \(x\) is \([\ce{OH-}]\), not \([\ce{H+}]\). Taking \(-\log x\) gives the pOH, and you must then subtract from 14.00.

This is the single most-lost point in the unit. Reporting \(-\log x\) as the pH turns a strongly basic answer into a strongly acidic one — a swing of several units in the wrong direction, which any sanity check should catch.

Worked example 3: ammonia

0.100 M \(\ce{NH3}\), \(K_b = 1.8\times10^{-5}\): \[ x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.100)} = 1.34\times10^{-3} = [\ce{OH-}] \] \[ \mathrm{pOH} = 2.87 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.87 = \mathbf{11.13} \] Note the pOH is numerically the same as acetic acid's pH — the two have equal constants. The final answers are wildly different, and the conversion step is the only reason.

INSTRUCTION B • Polyprotic acids 20 min

Why only the first ionization matters CED 8.3

SP 5 \(\ce{H3PO4}\) has \(K_{a1} = 7.5\times10^{-3}\), \(K_{a2} = 6.2\times10^{-8}\), \(K_{a3} = 4.8\times10^{-13}\).

Each successive constant is about \(10^5\) times smaller, because each proton must be pulled from an increasingly negative anion. So the pH may be calculated from \(K_{a1}\) alone.

The exception is \(\ce{H2SO4}\): its first ionization is strong and its second (\(K_{a2} = 1.2\times10^{-2}\)) still contributes measurably.

APPLICATION • Bases and polyprotics 20 min

  1. pH of 0.150 M \(\ce{NH3}\).
    \(x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.150)} = 1.64\times10^{-3}\); pOH \(= 2.78\); pH \(= \mathbf{11.22}\)
  2. A student reports pH 2.78 for that problem. Identify the error.
  3. Justify calculating \(\ce{H3PO4}\)'s pH from \(K_{a1}\) alone.
Exit ticket
Given \(K_a\) for a weak acid, how do you find \(K_b\) for its conjugate base, and why does the relationship hold?
\(K_b = K_w/K_a\). Adding the acid ionization to the conjugate base hydrolysis gives the autoionization of water, and adding reactions multiplies their equilibrium constants — so \(K_aK_b = K_w\).

INSTRUCTION A • p\(K_a\) and structure 25 min

p\(K_a\) CED 8.7

SP 5 \[ \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a \] A lower p\(K_a\) means a stronger acid. Acetic acid's \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\) gives p\(K_a =\) 4.74.

Structure and acid strength CED 8.6

SP 6
  • Binary acids down a group: \(\ce{HF}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HCl}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HBr}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HI}\). The controlling factor is bond strength — the H–X bond weakens down the group.
  • Oxyacids: \(\ce{HClO}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HClO2}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HClO3}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HClO4}\). More electronegative oxygens withdraw electron density from the O–H bond and stabilize the conjugate base.
AP trap
\(\ce{HF}\) is the weakest hydrohalic acid despite fluorine being the most electronegative element. Holding the proton tightly in a very strong H–F bond matters more than pulling electron density away from it. Do not reach for electronegativity when the trend is down a group.

APPLICATION • Structure and salts 20 min

  1. Classify and name the responsible ion: \(\ce{KNO3}\), \(\ce{NH4NO3}\), \(\ce{NaCN}\).
  2. Calculate the pH of 0.30 M \(\ce{NaF}\) (\(K_a(\ce{HF}) = 7.2\times10^{-4}\)).
    \(K_b = 1.0\times10^{-14}/7.2\times10^{-4} = 1.4\times10^{-11}\); \(x = \sqrt{(1.4\times10^{-11})(0.30)} = 2.0\times10^{-6}\); pOH \(= 5.69\); pH \(= \mathbf{8.31}\)
  3. Explain why \(\ce{NaCl}\) solutions are neutral but \(\ce{NaCN}\) solutions are basic.
Exit ticket
Rank \(\ce{HF}\), \(\ce{HCl}\), \(\ce{HBr}\), \(\ce{HI}\) by increasing strength and name the controlling factor.
\(\ce{HF}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HCl}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HBr}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HI}\). The H–X bond weakens down the group, so the proton is released more readily; bond strength outweighs electronegativity here.

  2. \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) solutions are: acidic

INSTRUCTION A • The common-ion effect 25 min

Le Ch\^atelier applied to an acid CED 8.4

SP 6 \[ \ce{HF(aq) <=> H+(aq) + F^-(aq)} \] Dissolve \(\ce{NaF}\) in this solution and you have added a product, so the equilibrium shifts left and the acid ionizes less.

The algebra collapses to a ratio: \[ [\ce{H+}] = K_a\frac{[\ce{HA}]}{[\ce{A^-}]} \] No square root, no quadratic — when both members of a conjugate pair are present in comparable amounts, \(x\) is negligible against both.

INSTRUCTION B • What makes a buffer 20 min

Recognizing one CED 8.4

SP 6

A buffer contains significant amounts of both members of a conjugate pair. Three ways it appears:

  1. Stated outright — \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) plus \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\).
  2. Made by partial neutralization of a weak acid with strong base.
  3. Hiding at the halfway point of a titration.
AP trap
\(\ce{HCl}\) with \(\ce{NaCl}\) is not a buffer. \(\ce{Cl-}\) has no affinity for protons, so nothing absorbs added acid. A buffer needs a weak conjugate pair — reversibility is the whole point.

APPLICATION • Recognition and prediction 20 min

  1. Buffer or not, with a reason: \(\ce{NH3}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) yes — weak base and its conjugate acid
  2. \(\ce{NaOH}\) \(+\) \(\ce{NaCl}\) no — no weak conjugate pair
  3. Find \([\ce{H+}]\) and pH for a solution 0.10 M in \(\ce{HF}\) and 0.30 M in \(\ce{NaF}\).
    \([\ce{H+}] = (7.2\times10^{-4})(0.10/0.30) = 2.4\times10^{-4}\); pH \(= \mathbf{3.62}\)
Exit ticket
Explain why adding \(\ce{NaF}\) to an \(\ce{HF}\) solution raises its pH.
\(\ce{F-}\) is a product of the \(\ce{HF}\) ionization, so adding it shifts that equilibrium to the left and suppresses the production of \(\ce{H+}\). The hydrolysis of \(\ce{F-}\) contributes as well, but the dominant effect is the common-ion shift.

Henderson–Hasselbalch CED 8.8–8.9 • Zumdahl §15.2

By the end you can…
  • Apply the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.
  • Explain why the ratio, not the concentration, sets the pH.

Read: Zumdahl §15.2 • PDF pp. 763–767

Retrieval warm-up
  1. \([\ce{H+}] = K_a \times\) \([\ce{HA}]/[\ce{A^-}]\)
  2. A buffer contains: both members of a conjugate pair
  3. p\(K_a\) of \(\ce{NH4+}\): 9.25

INSTRUCTION A • Taking the logarithm 25 min

The equation CED 8.9

SP 5 \[ \boxed{\;\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{base}]}{[\text{acid}]}\;} \]
  • Equal amounts \(\Rightarrow\) log term is 0, so pH \(=\) p\(K_a\).
  • More base than acid \(\Rightarrow\) pH above p\(K_a\).
  • More acid than base \(\Rightarrow\) pH below p\(K_a\).
AP trap
Base over acid, and the base is the anion. Inverting the ratio flips the sign of the correction, landing the pH the same distance on the wrong side of p\(K_a\). Before dividing, name the two species aloud: \(\ce{C2H3O2^-}\) is the base, \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) is the acid.

INSTRUCTION B • Why the ratio is what matters 20 min

Dilution barely moves a buffer's pH CED 8.8

SP 5

Only the ratio appears, so:

  • Diluting a buffer leaves the pH essentially unchanged — both concentrations fall by the same factor. Contrast a weak acid alone, where dilution does raise the pH.
  • You may use moles instead of molarity, since the shared volume cancels. This saves a step on nearly every titration problem.
Worked example 4: two buffers, one pH

0.25 M \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) with 0.15 M \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\): \[ \mathrm{pH} = 4.74 + \log(0.15/0.25) = 4.74 - 0.22 = \mathbf{4.52} \]

0.050 M with 0.030 M gives the same ratio \(0.60\) and therefore the same pH, 4.52 — at one fifth the concentration. The pH is identical; the capacity is not.

APPLICATION • Buffer calculations 20 min

  1. pH of 0.20 M \(\ce{NH3}\) with 0.30 M \(\ce{NH4Cl}\).
    pH \(= 9.25 + \log(0.20/0.30) = 9.25 - 0.18 = \mathbf{9.07}\)
  2. What ratio \([\ce{A^-}]/[\ce{HA}]\) gives pH 5.00 for acetic acid? 1.8
  3. Two acetate buffers: X is 1.0 M in each component, Y is 0.010 M in each. Compare their pH values and their usefulness.
Exit ticket
Why does diluting a buffer barely change its pH, while diluting a weak acid alone raises it noticeably?
A buffer's pH depends on the ratio of two concentrations, and dilution divides both by the same factor, leaving it unchanged. A weak acid alone has \([\ce{H+}] = \sqrt{K_aC_0}\), which depends on \(C_0\) itself, so lowering \(C_0\) lowers \([\ce{H+}]\).

  2. pH when the ratio is 1: p\(K_a\)

INSTRUCTION A • Stoichiometry first, then equilibrium 25 min

The two-step method CED 8.10

SP 5
  1. Stoichiometry. The strong reagent reacts completely. Added \(\ce{OH-}\) converts \(\ce{HA}\) to \(\ce{A-}\); added \(\ce{H+}\) does the reverse. Work in moles. No \(K\) appears.
  2. Equilibrium. Put the new amounts into Henderson–Hasselbalch.
AP trap
Never put added strong base into an ICE table. Strong base does not sit at equilibrium with a weak acid — it consumes it. Mixing the two steps is the single most reliable way to lose an entire buffer question.
Worked example 5: a buffer absorbing a base

1.00 L of a buffer is 0.50 M in \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) and 0.50 M in \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\). Add 0.010 mol \(\ce{NaOH}\).

Start: ratio 1, so pH \(= 4.74\). Step 1: \(\ce{OH^- + HC2H3O2 -> C2H3O2^- + H2O}\) leaves \(0.49\) mol acid and \(0.51\) mol base. Step 2: pH \(= 4.74 + \log(0.51/0.49) = \mathbf{4.76}\). For contrast, the same 0.010 mol of \(\ce{NaOH}\) in 1.00 L of pure water gives pH \(\mathbf{12.00}\). The buffer moved 0.02; water moved 5.00.

INSTRUCTION B • Capacity and design 20 min

Why equal amounts buffer best CED 8.10

SP 6 Buffering capacity depends on two things: how much of each component is present, and how balanced the ratio is.
Optimal buffering when \([\ce{HA}] = [\ce{A^-}]\), i.e. pH \(=\) p\(K_a\).
Useful range: p\(K_a \pm 1\).

To design a buffer, choose a weak acid whose p\(K_a\) is as close as possible to the target pH, then set the ratio with Henderson–Hasselbalch.

APPLICATION • Action and design 20 min

  1. Add 0.010 mol \(\ce{HCl}\) to the buffer in worked example 5 instead. New pH? 4.72
  2. Design a pH 4.00 buffer given p\(K_a\) values \(\ce{HNO2}\) 3.40, \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) 4.74, \(\ce{HOCl}\) 7.46.
    \(\ce{HNO2}\) is closest (0.60 away). \(4.00 = 3.40 + \log r\), so \(r = 10^{0.60} = \mathbf{4.0}\) — four times as much nitrite as nitrous acid.
  3. Why would \(\ce{HOCl}\) be a poor choice there?
Exit ticket
State the two steps for a buffer receiving strong base, and say what makes each different.
First a stoichiometry step: the strong base reacts to completion, converting weak acid into conjugate base, worked in moles with no equilibrium constant. Then an equilibrium step: substitute the new amounts into Henderson–Hasselbalch.

Halfway point: \([\ce{HA}] = [\ce{A^-}]\), so pH \(=\) p\(K_a\). This is how \(K_a\) is measured from a curve. Equivalence point: moles of base \(=\) moles of acid. That is a stoichiometric definition and says nothing about the pH being 7.
AP trap
A weak-acid equivalence point is not pH 7. All the acid has become its conjugate base, which hydrolyses. Weak acid with strong base gives an equivalence pH above 7; weak base with strong acid gives one below 7. Only strong-with-strong lands on 7.00.

Choosing an indicator CED 8.5

SP 6

An indicator is itself a weak acid whose two forms differ in colour. It changes over roughly p\(K_a \pm 1\), so choose one whose range brackets the equivalence pH.

The equivalence point is fixed by stoichiometry; the end point is where the indicator changes. A good choice makes them nearly coincide.

pH and solubility CED 8.11

SP 6
Qualitative only

CED topic 8.11 carries an exclusion statement: computations of solubility as a function of pH are never assessed. Predict the direction and justify with Le Ch\^atelier — do not calculate.

A salt is more soluble in acid when its anion is a meaningful base — that is, when its conjugate acid is weak. \(\ce{CaCO3}\) dissolves in acid because \(\ce{CO3^2-}\) is consumed; \(\ce{AgCl}\) does not, because \(\ce{Cl-}\) comes from a strong acid and does not react.

Exit ticket
Give the single question to ask before starting any calculation in this unit, and why it settles the method.
“What are the major species after any strong reagent has reacted completely?” The answer places you in one of four cases — strong acid or base, weak acid or base alone, a buffer, or excess strong reagent — and each has its own method. Choosing a method before identifying the species is how students end up applying Henderson–Hasselbalch at an equivalence point, where no buffer exists.
