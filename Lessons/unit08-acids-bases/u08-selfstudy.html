AP Chemistry lessons

Unit 8 • Acids and Bases

Self-Study • CED 8.1–8.11, I do / You do

Twelve ladders • four YOUR TURN questions each • 11–15% of the exam

How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a worked example, then four YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four before checking anything.

This unit is worth 11 %–15 % of the AP exam, second only to Unit 3. The distinction that causes the most lost marks. Strength is about the extent of ionization — a strong acid ionizes essentially completely. Concentration is about how much was dissolved. They are independent: a dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid are both entirely ordinary things. The habit that decides this unit. Before calculating any pH, ask strong or weak? A strong acid gives \([\ce{H+}]\) directly from the label. A weak one requires an equilibrium calculation with \(K_a\). Treating a weak acid as strong is the single commonest error here. \(K_w = 1.0\times10^{-14}\) at 25 °C. One scope note. The CED excludes deriving Henderson–Hasselbalch, computing the pH change on adding acid or base to a buffer, and species computations on polyprotic titration curves. None is asked here.

Ladder 1 • Brønsted–Lowry pairs

CED 8.1

An acid donates a proton; a base accepts one. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one \(\ce{H+}\).

I do: finding both pairs

Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in \(\ce{HCO3^- + H2O <=> H2CO3 + OH^-}\).

Follow the protons. \(\ce{HCO3-}\) gains one to become \(\ce{H2CO3}\): it acted as a base, and \(\ce{H2CO3}\) is its conjugate acid. That is pair one. \(\ce{H2O}\) loses one to become \(\ce{OH-}\): it acted as an acid, and \(\ce{OH-}\) is its conjugate base. That is pair two. Pairs: \(\ce{H2CO3}\)/\(\ce{HCO3-}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\)/\(\ce{OH-}\). The trap: pairing \(\ce{HCO3-}\) with \(\ce{OH-}\). They are on opposite sides and look like a pair, but they differ by more than one proton and are not related as conjugates. Always check the one-proton rule. Amphiprotic species such as \(\ce{HCO3-}\) can act either way — here it is a base, but with a stronger acid present it would donate instead.

  1. Conjugate base of \(\ce{H2SO4}\):
    \(\ce{HSO4-}\)
  2. Conjugate acid of \(\ce{NH3}\):
    \(\ce{NH4+}\)
  3. Identify both pairs in \(\ce{CH3COOH + H2O <=> CH3COO^- + H3O+}\):
    \(\ce{CH3COOH}\)/\(\ce{CH3COO-}\) and \(\ce{H3O+}\)/\(\ce{H2O}\)
  4. Write an equation showing \(\ce{HCO3-}\) acting as an acid.
    \(\ce{HCO3^- + H2O <=> CO3^2- + H3O+}\)

(a) \(\ce{HSO4-}\)   (b) \(\ce{NH4+}\)   (c) \(\ce{CH3COOH}\)/\(\ce{CH3COO-}\) and \(\ce{H3O+}\)/\(\ce{H2O}\)   (d) \(\ce{HCO3^- + H2O <=> CO3^2- + H3O+}\)

(a) \(\ce{HSO4-}\) — remove one proton. (b) \(\ce{NH4+}\) — add one proton. (c) \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) (acid) with \(\ce{CH3COO-}\) (its conjugate base); \(\ce{H2O}\) (base) with \(\ce{H3O+}\) (its conjugate acid). (d) \(\ce{HCO3^-(aq) + H2O(l) <=> CO3^2-(aq) + H3O+(aq)}\). Here it donates a proton, which is what makes it amphiprotic — it did the opposite in the worked example.

Ladder 2 • Strong versus weak

CED 8.1

Strong means essentially complete ionization — write a single arrow. Weak means partial — write a double arrow, and expect an equilibrium calculation.

I do: what is in the beaker

Compare 0.10 M \(\ce{HCl}\) with 0.10 M \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) in terms of the particles present and the conductivity.

\(\ce{HCl}\), strong: essentially every molecule ionizes. \(\ce{HCl -> H+ + Cl^-}\). The solution contains 0.10 M \(\ce{H+}\), 0.10 M \(\ce{Cl-}\) and almost no intact \(\ce{HCl}\). It conducts strongly. \(\ce{CH3COOH}\), weak: only about 1 % ionizes. \(\ce{CH3COOH <=> CH3COO^- + H+}\). The solution is mostly intact acetic acid molecules, with roughly 0.001 M of each ion. It conducts weakly. Same concentration, hundred-fold difference in \([\ce{H+}]\) — and about two pH units between them. Strength, not concentration, made that difference. On a particulate drawing: the weak acid must show mostly whole molecules. Drawing it fully dissociated is the standard error.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Which conducts better, 0.1 M \(\ce{HCl}\) or 0.1 M \(\ce{HF}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{HCl}\) — more mobile ions
  2. Can a weak acid solution be more concentrated than a strong acid solution?
    yes
  3. Which arrow does a weak acid take, and why?
    double — partial ionization
  4. Could a concentrated weak acid have a lower pH than a dilute strong acid?
    yes

(a) \(\ce{HCl}\) — more mobile ions   (b) yes   (c) double — partial ionization   (d) yes

(a) \(\ce{HCl}\). It ionizes essentially completely, so it supplies far more mobile ions; \(\ce{HF}\) is weak and mostly stays intact. (b) Yes. Concentration and strength are independent properties. (c) A double arrow, because the ionization is an equilibrium — substantial amounts of the un-ionized acid remain. (d) Yes. pH depends on \([\ce{H+}]\), not on strength alone. 5 M acetic acid produces more \(\ce{H+}\) than 0.0001 M \(\ce{HCl}\), despite being the weaker acid.

Ladder 3 • pH, pOH and K\textsubscriptw

CED 8.2 \(\text{pH} = -\log[\ce{H+}]\), \(\text{pOH} = -\log[\ce{OH-}]\), and at 25 °C they sum to 14.00.
I do: all four quantities from one

A solution has \([\ce{H+}] = 2.5e-5\,\mathrm{M}\). Find pH, pOH and \([\ce{OH-}]\).

\[ \text{pH} = -\log(2.5\times10^{-5}) = \mathbf{4.60} \] \[ \text{pOH} = 14.00 - 4.60 = \mathbf{9.40} \] \[ [\ce{OH-}] = \frac{1.0\times10^{-14}}{2.5\times10^{-5}} = \mathbf{4.0\times10^{-10}~M} \] Sense check: pH below 7 means acidic, so \([\ce{H+}]\) should exceed \([\ce{OH-}]\). It does, by five orders of magnitude. ✓ Significant figures in a logarithm: only the digits after the decimal point count. \([\ce{H+}]\) has two significant figures, so the pH is quoted to two decimal places — 4.60, not 4.6 or 4.602.

  2. \([\ce{OH-}]\) when pH \(= 9.00\):
  3. pOH when \([\ce{H+}] = 0.020\,\mathrm{M}\):
  4. Is a solution of pH 6.5 at 25 °C acidic, basic or neutral?
    very slightly acidic

(a) 3.00   (b) 1.0e-5 M   (c) 12.30   (d) very slightly acidic

(a) \(-\log(1.0\times10^{-3}) = \mathbf{3.00}\). (b) \(\text{pOH} = 14.00 - 9.00 = 5.00\), so \([\ce{OH-}] = \mathbf{1.0\times10^{-5}}\) M. (c) \(\text{pH} = -\log(0.020) = 1.70\), so \(\text{pOH} = 14.00 - 1.70 = \mathbf{12.30}\). (d) Slightly acidic — below 7, though only just. At 25 °C neutral is exactly 7.00, where \([\ce{H+}] = [\ce{OH-}]\).

Ladder 4 • pH of strong acids and bases

CED 8.2

For a strong acid or base, the concentration is the ion concentration — but watch for a formula supplying more than one ion.

I do: watch the subscript

Find the pH of 0.0050 M \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\), assuming complete dissociation.

Count the hydroxides. Each formula unit gives two: \[ [\ce{OH-}] = 2(0.0050) = 0.010\,\mathrm{M} \] \[ \text{pOH} = -\log(0.010) = 2.00 \qquad \text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.00 = \mathbf{12.00} \] Forgetting the factor of 2 gives pOH 2.30 and pH 11.70 — close enough to look plausible, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. The sanity check that catches sign errors: a group 2 hydroxide is a strong base, so the pH must be well above 7. Any answer below 7 is immediately wrong, and usually means pH and pOH were interchanged.

  3. pH of 0.0025 M \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\):
  4. Why is the concentration used directly for a strong acid but not for a weak one?
    the weak acid only partly ionizes

(a) 2.00   (b) 12.00   (c) 11.70   (d) the weak acid only partly ionizes

(a) Fully ionized, so \([\ce{H+}] = 0.010\) and \(\text{pH} = \mathbf{2.00}\). (b) \([\ce{OH-}] = 0.010\), \(\text{pOH} = 2.00\), so \(\text{pH} = \mathbf{12.00}\). (c) \([\ce{OH-}] = 2(0.0025) = 0.0050\); \(\text{pOH} = 2.30\), so \(\text{pH} = \mathbf{11.70}\). (d) Because a strong acid ionizes essentially completely: every mole dissolved yields a mole of \(\ce{H+}\), so the label gives \([\ce{H+}]\) directly. A weak acid ionizes only slightly, so \([\ce{H+}]\) is far smaller than the label and must be found from the \(K_a\) equilibrium.

Ladder 5 • pH of a weak acid

CED 8.3

Set up the \(K_a\) equilibrium, assume \(x\) is small, solve — then test the assumption.

I do: the full routine

Find the pH of 0.200 M acetic acid, \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\).

Equilibrium: \(\ce{CH3COOH <=> CH3COO^- + H+}\), with \(x = [\ce{H+}]\) formed. \[ K_a = \frac{x^2}{0.200 - x} = 1.8\times10^{-5} \] Assume \(x \ll 0.200\): \[ x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.200)} = 1.90e-3~\text{M} \] Test it: \(1.90\times10^{-3}/0.200 = 0.95\,\mathrm{\%}\), well under 5 %. Valid — no quadratic needed. \[ \text{pH} = -\log(1.897\times10^{-3}) = \mathbf{2.72} \] Compare with a strong acid at the same concentration: pH 0.70. The weak acid sits two units higher because so little of it ionized.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. Find \([\ce{H+}]\) for 0.100 M of an acid with \(K_a = 4.0\times10^{-6}\).
  2. Find the pH in (a).
  3. Check whether the approximation was valid in (a).
    yes — 0.63 %
  4. Why is \([\ce{H+}]\) far less than the acid's concentration?
    only a small fraction ionizes

(a) 6.3e-4 M   (b) 3.20   (c) yes — 0.63 %   (d) only a small fraction ionizes

(a) \(x = \sqrt{(4.0\times10^{-6})(0.100)} = \mathbf{6.3\times10^{-4}}\) M. (b) \(\text{pH} = -\log(6.32\times10^{-4}) = \mathbf{3.20}\). (c) \(6.3\times10^{-4}/0.100 = 0.63\,\mathrm{\%}\), far below 5 % — valid. (d) Because a weak acid ionizes only partially. At equilibrium the great majority of the acid remains as intact molecules, and only the small fraction that has donated a proton contributes to \([\ce{H+}]\).

Ladder 6 • Percent ionization

CED 8.3 \[ \%\text{ ionization} = \frac{[\ce{H+}]_{\text{eq}}} {[\text{acid}]_{\text{initial}}} \times 100 \] It rises on dilution, even though the pH rises too.
I do: the counter-intuitive result

For the 0.200 M acetic acid above, find the percent ionization and predict what happens on dilution.

\[ \frac{1.90\times10^{-3}}{0.200}\times100 = \mathbf{0.95\%} \] On dilution the percent ionization increases. \(K_a\) is fixed, and \(x \approx \sqrt{K_a C}\) — so \(x\) falls only as the square root of the concentration, while the concentration itself falls linearly. The ratio therefore grows. Concretely: at 0.0200 M the ionization is about 3.0 % — three times the fraction, even though \([\ce{H+}]\) itself has fallen and the pH has risen. Two things moving opposite ways. Diluting makes the solution less acidic (higher pH) but the acid more ionized (higher percentage). Both are true, and confusing them is a common error.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. \([\ce{H+}] = 2.0e-3\,\mathrm{M}\) from 0.50 M acid. Percent ionization?
  2. On dilution, does percent ionization rise or fall?
    rises
  3. On dilution, does the pH rise or fall?
    rises
  4. Explain how both answers can be true at once.
    \([\ce{H+}]\) falls, but not as fast as the concentration

(a) 0.40 %   (b) rises   (c) rises   (d) \([\ce{H+}]\) falls, but not as fast as the concentration

(a) \(2.0\times10^{-3}/0.50 \times 100 = \mathbf{0.40}\%\). (b) It rises. (c) The pH also rises — the solution is less acidic. (d) They measure different things. \([\ce{H+}]\) does fall on dilution, which is why the pH rises. But it falls only as \(\sqrt{C}\), while the total acid concentration falls as \(C\). So the fraction ionized — their ratio — goes up, even as the absolute amount of \(\ce{H+}\) goes down.

Ladder 7 • pK\textsubscripta and acid strength

CED 8.7 \(\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a\). A smaller \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) means a stronger acid — the logarithm reverses the order.
I do: converting and comparing

Acid A has \(K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}\); acid B has \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.14\). Which is stronger?

Put them on the same scale. \(\mathrm{p}K_a(\text{A}) = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = \mathbf{4.74}\). Compare: B's 3.14 is smaller, so B is the stronger acid. Check by converting back: \(K_a(\text{B}) = 10^{-3.14} = 7.2\times10^{-4}\), which is indeed larger than \(1.8\times10^{-5}\) — about forty times. ✓ Why the reversal: the minus sign in the definition. Every factor of ten up in \(K_a\) is one unit down in \(\mathrm{p}K_a\). It works exactly as pH does: lower pH means more acidic.

  1. \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) for \(K_a = 1.0\times10^{-7}\):
  2. \(K_a\) for \(\mathrm{p}K_a = 2.00\):
  3. Which is stronger, \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) 4.2 or 9.1?
    the 4.2 one
  4. Two acids differ by 2 in \(\mathrm{p}K_a\). How do their \(K_a\) values compare?
    a factor of 100

(a) 7.00   (b) 1.0e-2    (c) the 4.2 one   (d) a factor of 100

(a) \(-\log(1.0\times10^{-7}) = \mathbf{7.00}\). (b) \(10^{-2.00} = \mathbf{1.0\times10^{-2}}\). (c) The one with \(\mathrm{p}K_a = \mathbf{4.2}\) — smaller means stronger. (d) They differ by a factor of \(10^2 = \mathbf{100}\). The \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) scale is logarithmic, so each unit is a tenfold change in \(K_a\).

Ladder 8 • Structure and acid strength

CED 8.6

Two separate trends, governed by two different factors — and one of them runs opposite to what electronegativity alone would suggest.

I do: the two trends

Explain why acid strength increases along \(\ce{HF}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HCl}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HBr}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HI}\), and along \(\ce{HClO}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HClO2}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HClO3}\) \(<\) \(\ce{HClO4}\).

Down the halogens: bond strength decides. The halogen gets larger, so the \(\ce{H-X}\) bond is longer and weaker and the proton is released more easily. Electronegativity falls down this group, so it would predict the opposite order — it is not the controlling factor here. Across the oxoacids: electron withdrawal decides. Each added oxygen is highly electronegative and pulls electron density away from the \(\ce{O-H}\) bond, weakening it. The extra oxygens also spread the negative charge of the resulting anion over more atoms, so the anion accommodates it better and the ionization proceeds further. The lesson: identify which factor is varying before reaching for a rule. Electronegativity, bond strength and charge delocalization each dominate in different comparisons.
YOUR TURN 8 — four questions
  1. Stronger acid, \(\ce{HBr}\) or \(\ce{HCl}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{HBr}\) — weaker bond
  2. Stronger acid, \(\ce{HClO4}\) or \(\ce{HClO}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{HClO4}\) — more electron withdrawal
  3. Stronger acid, \(\ce{HBrO3}\) or \(\ce{HClO3}\)?
    \(\ce{HClO3}\) — Cl more electronegative
  4. Why does electronegativity fail to explain the hydrohalic trend?
    it predicts the reverse order

(a) \(\ce{HBr}\) — weaker bond   (b) \(\ce{HClO4}\) — more electron withdrawal   (c) \(\ce{HClO3}\) — Cl more electronegative   (d) it predicts the reverse order

(a) \(\ce{HBr}\). Bromine is larger, so the \(\ce{H-Br}\) bond is longer and weaker and the proton leaves more readily. (b) \(\ce{HClO4}\). Three additional oxygens withdraw far more electron density from the \(\ce{O-H}\) bond and spread the anion's charge over four oxygens rather than one. (c) \(\ce{HClO3}\). With the same number of oxygens, the more electronegative central atom withdraws more density; chlorine is more electronegative than bromine. (d) Because fluorine is the most electronegative halogen, so an electronegativity argument predicts \(\ce{HF}\) to be the strongest — yet it is by far the weakest of the four. Bond strength, not polarity, is what varies decisively down that group.

Ladder 9 • Acid–base properties of salts

CED 8.7

Look at where each ion came from. An ion from a weak parent reacts with water; an ion from a strong parent does not.

I do: three salts, three verdicts

Predict whether solutions of \(\ce{NaCl}\), \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) and \(\ce{NaCH3COO}\) are acidic, basic or neutral.

\(\ce{NaCl}\): neutral. \(\ce{Na+}\) comes from the strong base \(\ce{NaOH}\); \(\ce{Cl-}\) from the strong acid \(\ce{HCl}\). Both are the conjugates of strong parents, so neither reacts appreciably with water. \(\ce{NH4Cl}\): acidic. \(\ce{NH4+}\) is the conjugate acid of the weak base ammonia, so it donates a proton to water: \(\ce{NH4+ + H2O <=> NH3 + H3O+}\). \(\ce{Cl-}\) is inert. \(\ce{NaCH3COO}\): basic. Acetate is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it accepts a proton from water: \(\ce{CH3COO^- + H2O <=> CH3COOH + OH^-}\). \(\ce{Na+}\) is inert. The rule in one line: the conjugate of a weak species is itself reactive; the conjugate of a strong species is not.

    acidic — \(\ce{NH4+}\) donates
  4. Why is \(\ce{Cl-}\) inert in water while \(\ce{F-}\) is not?
    \(\ce{HCl}\) is strong, \(\ce{HF}\) is weak

(a) neutral   (b) basic — \(\ce{F-}\) is from weak \(\ce{HF}\)   (c) acidic — \(\ce{NH4+}\) donates   (d) \(\ce{HCl}\) is strong, \(\ce{HF}\) is weak

(a) Neutral — \(\ce{K+}\) from strong \(\ce{KOH}\), \(\ce{NO3-}\) from strong \(\ce{HNO3}\). (b) Basic. Fluoride is the conjugate base of the weak acid \(\ce{HF}\), so it accepts a proton from water and produces \(\ce{OH-}\). (d) Because \(\ce{HCl}\) is a strong acid, so its conjugate base \(\ce{Cl-}\) is an extremely weak base — it has no tendency to take a proton back. \(\ce{HF}\) is weak, so \(\ce{F-}\) is a strong enough base to remove a proton from water appreciably.

Ladder 10 • Buffers

CED 8.4 CED 8.8 CED 8.9

A buffer holds appreciable amounts of both members of a conjugate pair, so it can neutralize either added acid or added base. \[ \text{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{base}]}{[\text{acid}]} \]

I do: pH and mechanism

A buffer is 0.40 M in \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) and 0.25 M in \(\ce{CH3COO-}\) (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.74\)). Find its pH, and explain how it resists change.

\[ \text{pH} = 4.74 + \log\frac{0.25}{0.40} = 4.74 + (-0.20) = \mathbf{4.54} \]

Below the \(\mathrm{p}K_a\), as it must be when the acid form dominates.

Added \(\ce{H+}\) is consumed by the base component: \(\ce{CH3COO^- + H+ -> CH3COOH}\). Strong acid becomes weak acid, so free \([\ce{H3O+}]\) barely rises. Added \(\ce{OH-}\) is consumed by the acid component: \(\ce{CH3COOH + OH^- -> CH3COO^- + H2O}\). Why the pH barely moves: it depends on the ratio of the pair, and a small addition changes that ratio only slightly. A logarithm of a slightly-changed ratio is a barely-changed number.

Ladder 11 • Buffer capacity

CED 8.10

Capacity is how much acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH moves sharply. It depends on the absolute amounts of the pair, not on their ratio.

I do: same pH, different capacity

Two buffers both have pH 4.74. Buffer X is 1.0 M in each component; buffer Y is 0.010 M in each. Compare them.

Same pH: both have a \(1\!:\!1\) ratio, and pH depends on the ratio, so both read 4.74. Very different capacity: X holds a hundred times more of each component. Adding 0.005 mol of strong acid barely dents X's reservoir, but would consume half of Y's base component and move its pH substantially. The distinction to hold on to: ratio sets the pH; amount sets the capacity. Two independent properties, controlled by two different things. Best capacity when \(\text{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a\), because the ratio is then 1 and the buffer has equal reserves in both directions. Useful range is roughly \(\mathrm{p}K_a \pm 1\).

  3. At what pH is a buffer most effective?
    at its \(\mathrm{p}K_a\)
  4. Choose a buffer for pH 5.0 from acids with \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) 3.1, 4.8 and 9.2.
    the 4.8 one

(a) the 0.50 M   (b) yes — same ratio   (c) at its \(\mathrm{p}K_a\)   (d) the 4.8 one

(c) At its \(\mathrm{p}K_a\), where the pair is present in equal amounts and reserves against acid and base are balanced. The 3.1 acid would need a ratio near 80:1, leaving almost no reserve of the acid form.

Ladder 12 • Titration curves

CED 8.5

Four landmarks on a weak-acid titration: the start, the half-equivalence point (pH \(= \mathrm{p}K_a\)), the equivalence point (above 7), and the excess-base region.

I do: reading the four regions

25.00 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.74\)) is titrated with 0.100 M \(\ce{NaOH}\). Describe each landmark.

Start (0 mL): weak acid alone. pH 2.87 from the \(K_a\) calculation — not 1.00, because it is weak. Half-equivalence (12.50 mL): half the acid has become acetate, so \([\ce{HA}] = [\ce{A-}]\) and \(\text{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a = \mathbf{4.74}\). Reading a \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) straight off a curve at this point is a standard exam task. Equivalence (25.00 mL): all the acid is now acetate, a weak base. It hydrolyses water, so the pH is above 7 — here 8.72. This is the landmark most often got wrong; equivalence does not mean pH 7 unless both acid and base were strong. Beyond equivalence: excess strong base dominates and the pH approaches that of the titrant. Indicator choice follows: phenolphthalein (8.3–10.0) brackets 8.72; methyl orange (3.1–4.4) would change far too early.

    at 7
  3. Why is a weak-acid equivalence point above 7?

  4. A titration curve's vertical rise is centred at 20.0 mL, and the pH at 10.0 mL is 5.20. Find \(\mathrm{p}K_a\).

(a) \(\mathrm{p}K_a\)   (b) at 7   (c) the conjugate base hydrolyses   (d) 5.20

(a) The \(\mathrm{p}K_a\) of the acid, since \([\ce{HA}] = [\ce{A-}]\) there. (b) At 7. Both ions produced are conjugates of strong species and neither reacts with water. (c) Because at equivalence the solution contains only the conjugate base of a weak acid, which reacts with water, \(\ce{A^- + H2O <=> HA + OH^-}\), generating \(\ce{OH-}\) and making the solution basic. (d) 10.0 mL is exactly half of 20.0 mL, so it is the half-equivalence point and \(\mathrm{p}K_a = \mathbf{5.20}\).
