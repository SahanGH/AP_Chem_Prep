AP Chemistry lessons

Unit 7 • Equilibrium

Self-Study • CED 7.1–7.12, I do / You do

Twelve ladders • four YOUR TURN questions each • work alone, check after all four

How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a worked example, then four YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four before checking anything.

The distinction this whole unit rests on. \(K\) is a constant at a given temperature; \(Q\) is the same expression evaluated right now, at whatever concentrations happen to exist. Comparing the two tells you which way the reaction will move. Every Le Ch\^atelier question is really a \(Q\)-versus-\(K\) question underneath. The habit that decides this unit. Only temperature changes the value of \(K\). Adding reactant, removing product, changing the volume or adding a catalyst all leave \(K\) exactly where it was — they move the system along a fixed \(K\), they do not move \(K\). One scope note. Converting between \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) is excluded by the CED. The conceptual difference between them is examinable; the \(K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}\) conversion is not, and does not appear here.

Ladder 1 • What equilibrium is

CED 7.1 CED 7.2

Equilibrium is dynamic: both reactions continue, at equal rates. Concentrations stop changing; the molecules do not stop reacting.

I do: what stops and what does not

Describe what is happening at equilibrium, macroscopically and at the particle level.

Macroscopically: nothing changes. Concentrations, colour and pressure are all constant. On a concentration-versus-time graph, every curve has flattened. At the particle level: everything continues. The forward reaction is still converting reactants to products, and the reverse is still converting them back — at exactly the same rate. Every molecule consumed one way is replaced by one made the other way. Why the graph cannot prove this. If the reaction had simply stopped, the curves would also flatten. The evidence for dynamic behaviour comes from experiments such as isotopic labelling, where a labelled molecule placed on one side later appears on both. Also note: reactants are still present. A reaction at equilibrium has not gone to completion, and never will — which is why the arrow is drawn both ways.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. At equilibrium, what is true of the forward and reverse rates?
    equal
  2. Are reactants still present at equilibrium?
    yes
  3. What does a concentration-time graph look like once equilibrium is reached?
    horizontal lines at non-zero values
  4. Why does that graph not prove the process is dynamic?
    a stopped reaction would look identical

(a) equal   (b) yes   (c) horizontal lines at non-zero values   (d) a stopped reaction would look identical

(a) They are equal. That equality is what makes the concentrations constant. (b) Yes. Equilibrium is not completion; both reactants and products are present in fixed amounts. (c) All curves become horizontal at non-zero concentrations — constant, but not zero. (d) Because a reaction that had genuinely stopped would produce exactly the same flat lines. The graph shows only that nothing is changing macroscopically. Distinguishing the two requires evidence at the molecular level, such as isotopic labelling.

Ladder 2 • Writing equilibrium expressions

CED 7.3

Products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient. Pure solids and pure liquids are omitted.

I do: two expressions, one omission

Write \(K\) for \(\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}\) and for \(\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\).

First: all three are gases, so all appear, each raised to its coefficient. \[ K = \frac{[\ce{NH3}]^2}{[\ce{N2}][\ce{H2}]^3} \] Second: both \(\ce{CaCO3}\) and \(\ce{CaO}\) are pure solids and are omitted. \[ K = [\ce{CO2}] \] Why solids drop out. A pure solid has a fixed density and composition, so its “concentration” does not change no matter how much is present. Adding more solid cannot shift the equilibrium, so it is absorbed into the constant rather than written. The consequence worth noticing: in the second reaction, the pressure of \(\ce{CO2}\) above the solid at a given temperature is fixed by \(K\) alone — however much or little \(\ce{CaCO3}\) is in the flask.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Write \(K\) for \(\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}\):
    \([\ce{SO3}]^2/([\ce{SO2}]^2[\ce{O2}])\)
  2. Write \(K\) for \(\ce{H2O(l) <=> H2O(g)}\):
    \(K = [\ce{H2O}(g)]\)
  3. Write \(K\) for \(\ce{Fe3O4(s) + 4H2(g) <=> 3Fe(s) + 4H2O(g)}\):
    \([\ce{H2O}]^4/[\ce{H2}]^4\)
  4. Why does adding more solid not shift such an equilibrium?
    its concentration is constant

(a) \([\ce{SO3}]^2/([\ce{SO2}]^2[\ce{O2}])\)   (b) \(K = [\ce{H2O}(g)]\)   (c) \([\ce{H2O}]^4/[\ce{H2}]^4\)   (d) its concentration is constant

(a) \(K = \dfrac{[\ce{SO3}]^2} {[\ce{SO2}]^2[\ce{O2}]}\). (b) \(K = [\ce{H2O}(g)]\) — the liquid is omitted. This constant is just the vapour pressure of water at that temperature. (c) \(K = \dfrac{[\ce{H2O}]^4}{[\ce{H2}]^4}\) — both solids omitted. (d) Because a pure solid's concentration is fixed by its density and does not appear in \(K\). Adding more increases the amount but not the concentration, so there is nothing for the system to respond to.

Ladder 3 • Calculating K from equilibrium concentrations

CED 7.4

If you are handed the equilibrium concentrations, substitute them straight in. The only difficulty is remembering the exponents.

I do: substitution

At equilibrium, \(\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}\) has \([\ce{HI}] = 0.50\,\mathrm{M}\) and \([\ce{H2}] = [\ce{I2}] = 0.10\,\mathrm{M}\). Find \(K\).

\[ K = \frac{[\ce{HI}]^2}{[\ce{H2}][\ce{I2}]} = \frac{(0.50)^2}{(0.10)(0.10)} = \frac{0.25}{0.010} = \mathbf{25} \] The exponent is the whole difficulty. Forgetting to square \(\ce{HI}\) gives 50 — exactly double, and a common wrong answer. Sense check: \(K = 25\) is comfortably greater than 1, so products should dominate at equilibrium. Indeed 0.50 M of \(\ce{HI}\) against 0.10 M of each reactant. ✓ \(K\) has no units on this exam. Report it as a bare number.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. \([\ce{NH3}] = 0.40\), \([\ce{N2}] = 0.20\), \([\ce{H2}] = 0.20\) for \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}\). Find \(K\).
  2. For \(\ce{2NO2 <=> N2O4}\) with \([\ce{N2O4}] = 0.60\) and \([\ce{NO2}] = 0.20\), find \(K\).
  3. If \(K\) for a reaction is 4, what is \(K\) for its reverse?
    \(1/4 = 0.25\)
  4. Why is the coefficient used as an exponent rather than a multiplier?
    \(K\) derives from a product of concentrations

(a) 100   (b) 15   (c) \(1/4 = 0.25\)   (d) \(K\) derives from a product of concentrations

(a) \(K = (0.40)^2/[(0.20)(0.20)^3] = 0.16/0.0016 = \mathbf{100}\). (b) \(K = 0.60/(0.20)^2 = 0.60/0.040 = \mathbf{15}\). (c) \(\mathbf{0.25}\). Reversing a reaction inverts \(K\), since numerator and denominator swap. (d) Because the equilibrium expression comes from a product of concentration terms, one for each particle taking part. Two \(\ce{NO2}\) molecules contribute \([\ce{NO2}] \times [\ce{NO2}]\), which is \([\ce{NO2}]^2\) — multiplication, not addition.

Ladder 4 • What the magnitude of K tells you

CED 7.5 \(K\) measures extent — how far the reaction proceeds — and nothing else. It says nothing about rate.
I do: reading three values

Interpret \(K = 1\times10^{10}\), \(K = 1\) and \(K = 1\times10^{-8}\).

\(K = 10^{10}\): equilibrium lies far to the right. At equilibrium the mixture is essentially all products, with only traces of reactant. Such a reaction is often said to “go to completion”, though strictly a little reactant always remains. \(K = 1\): neither side dominates — comparable amounts of both are present. \(K = 10^{-8}\): far to the left. Almost nothing reacts; the mixture is essentially all reactant. What none of these tells you: the rate. A reaction with \(K = 10^{10}\) may be immeasurably slow if its activation energy is large — the conversion of diamond to graphite is exactly that. Extent and rate are independent properties.
YOUR TURN 4 — four questions
  1. \(K = 5\times10^{6}\). Which side is favoured?
    products
  2. \(K = 3\times10^{-5}\). Which side?
    reactants
  3. Does a large \(K\) mean a fast reaction?
    no
  4. Two reactions have \(K = 100\) and \(K = 0.01\). How are they related?
    they are the reverse of each other

(a) products   (b) reactants   (c) no   (d) they are the reverse of each other

(a) Products — \(K \gg 1\). (b) Reactants — \(K \ll 1\). (c) No. \(K\) describes only how far the reaction goes, not how quickly. Rate depends on activation energy, which is entirely separate. (d) They are reverses of one another: \(0.01 = 1/100\). Inverting a reaction inverts its equilibrium constant.

Ladder 5 • Q versus K

CED 7.3 CED 7.8 \(Q\) has exactly the same form as \(K\) but is evaluated at the current concentrations. Comparing them predicts the direction of change.
I do: predicting the shift

For \(\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}\) with \(K = 25\), a mixture holds \([\ce{HI}] = 0.20\), \([\ce{H2}] = 0.30\), \([\ce{I2}] = 0.30\) M. Which way does it move?

\[ Q = \frac{(0.20)^2}{(0.30)(0.30)} = \frac{0.040}{0.090} = \mathbf{0.44} \] Compare: \(Q = 0.44\) is much less than \(K = 25\). There is too little product relative to equilibrium, so the reaction proceeds forward, to the right, making more \(\ce{HI}\) until \(Q\) climbs to 25. The three cases, once and for all: \(Q < K\) shift right (make products); \(Q = K\) already at equilibrium, no net change; \(Q > K\) shift left (make reactants). Why this is the master tool: every Le Ch\^atelier prediction is this comparison in disguise. Adding a reactant lowers \(Q\); the system responds by moving right. Nothing else is going on.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. \(Q = 0.5\), \(K = 2.0\). Which way?
    right
  2. \(Q = 150\), \(K = 25\). Which way?
    left
  3. \(Q = K\). What happens?
    nothing — already at equilibrium
  4. Adding more reactant to a mixture at equilibrium: what happens to \(Q\), and then to the system?
    \(Q\) falls; the system shifts right

(a) right   (b) left   (c) nothing — already at equilibrium   (d) \(Q\) falls; the system shifts right

(a) Right — \(Q < K\), too little product. (b) Left — \(Q > K\), too much product. (c) Nothing. The system is already at equilibrium and there is no net change in either direction. (d) Reactant appears in the denominator of \(Q\), so increasing it makes \(Q\) smaller. Now \(Q < K\), and the system shifts right to consume some of the added reactant and restore \(Q = K\).

Ladder 6 • ICE tables

CED 7.7

Initial, Change, Equilibrium. The change row carries the coefficients as multipliers of \(x\) — that is where most errors enter.

I do: setting one up correctly

0.500 M \(\ce{PCl5}\) is placed in a flask and dissociates by \(\ce{PCl5 <=> PCl3 + Cl2}\). Build the ICE table.

\(\ce{PCl5}\)\(\ce{PCl3}\)\(\ce{Cl2}\)
Initial0.50000
Change\(-x\)\(+x\)\(+x\)
Equilibrium\(0.500-x\)\(x\)\(x\)
Here every coefficient is 1, so every change is \(\pm x\). But if the equation were \(\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}\), the change row would read \(-x\), \(-x\), \(+\mathbf{2}x\) — the coefficient 2 multiplies. Writing \(+x\) for \(\ce{HI}\) is the single commonest ICE error and corrupts every later line. Reading the table: the equilibrium row is what goes into \(K\). Nothing else does.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. For \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}\) starting from 1.0 M of each reactant, write the change row.
    \(-x\), \(-3x\), \(+2x\)
  2. For \(\ce{2NO2 <=> N2O4}\) starting from 0.80 M \(\ce{NO2}\), write the equilibrium row.
    \(0.80 - 2x\) and \(x\)
  3. Which row is substituted into the \(K\) expression?
    the equilibrium row
  4. Why must coefficients appear in the change row?
    species react in fixed proportions

(a) \(-x\), \(-3x\), \(+2x\)   (b) \(0.80 - 2x\) and \(x\)   (c) the equilibrium row   (d) species react in fixed proportions

(a) \(-x\), \(-3x\), \(+2x\). (b) \(\ce{NO2}\): \(0.80 - 2x\); \(\ce{N2O4}\): \(x\). (c) The equilibrium row. (d) Because the balanced equation fixes the proportions in which species are consumed and produced. If \(x\) moles of \(\ce{N2}\) react, exactly \(3x\) of \(\ce{H2}\) must go with it and \(2x\) of \(\ce{NH3}\) must appear. Using \(x\) everywhere would describe a reaction with different stoichiometry.

Ladder 7 • The 5% approximation

CED 7.7

When \(K\) is very small, \(x\) is tiny compared with the initial concentration and the subtraction can be dropped. Always test the assumption afterwards.

I do: assume, solve, then check

For \(\ce{A <=> B + C}\) with \(K = 1.0\times10^{-5}\) and \([\ce{A}]_0 = 0.100\,\mathrm{M}\), find \(x\).

Set up: \(\dfrac{x^2}{0.100 - x} = 1.0\times10^{-5}\). Assume \(x \ll 0.100\), so the denominator is about 0.100: \[ x = \sqrt{(1.0\times10^{-5})(0.100)} = \mathbf{1.0\times10^{-3}} \] Now test it. As a fraction of the initial concentration, \(1.0\times10^{-3}/0.100 = 1.0\,\mathrm{\%}\) — comfortably below the 5 % threshold, so the assumption is valid and no quadratic is needed. What “valid” means: solving the quadratic properly gives \(x = 9.95\times10^{-4}\), differing by 0.5 % — far less than the precision of the data. When it fails, as it does whenever \(K\) is not small, you must use the quadratic. Testing takes ten seconds; skipping the test is what costs marks.
YOUR TURN 7 — four questions
  1. What is the usual threshold for accepting the approximation?
  2. \(x = 0.004\) from an initial 0.200 M. Is it valid?
    yes — 2 %
  3. \(x = 0.03\) from an initial 0.10 M. Is it valid?
    no — 30 %
  4. For which kind of \(K\) is the approximation generally safe?
    very small \(K\)

(a) 5 %   (b) yes — 2 %   (c) no — 30 %   (d) very small \(K\)

(a) \(x\) must be under 5 % of the initial concentration. (b) \(0.004/0.200 = 2.0\,\mathrm{\%}\) — valid. (c) \(0.03/0.10 = 30\,\mathrm{\%}\) — not valid; the quadratic is required. (d) When \(K\) is very small. A small \(K\) means the reaction barely proceeds, so \(x\) stays tiny relative to what was there initially. When \(K\) is large the reaction goes far, \(x\) is a big fraction of the initial amount, and the approximation collapses.

Ladder 8 • Le Ch\^atelier: concentration

CED 7.9 CED 7.10

Add something and the system consumes it; remove something and the system replaces it. \(K\) does not change.

I do: four changes to one equilibrium

For \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}\), state the effect of: adding \(\ce{N2}\); removing \(\ce{NH3}\); adding \(\ce{NH3}\); adding argon at constant volume.

Adding \(\ce{N2}\): shifts right. The system consumes some of the added reactant. (\(Q\) falls below \(K\).) Removing \(\ce{NH3}\): shifts right. The system replaces what was taken. (\(Q\) falls.) Adding \(\ce{NH3}\): shifts left. (\(Q\) rises above \(K\).) Adding argon at constant volume: no shift. Argon takes no part in the reaction and does not change the concentration of anything that does. The total pressure rises, but every partial pressure that matters is unchanged, so \(Q\) is unchanged. Throughout, \(K\) is constant — only the position along it moved.
YOUR TURN 8 — four questions
  1. For \(\ce{2SO2 + O2 <=> 2SO3}\), effect of adding \(\ce{O2}\)?
    right
  2. Effect of removing \(\ce{SO3}\)?
    right
  3. Effect of adding helium at constant volume?
    none
  4. Does adding a reactant change \(K\)?
    no

(a) right   (b) right   (c) none   (d) no

(a) Shifts right, consuming some of the added oxygen. (b) Shifts right, replacing the removed product. (c) No effect. Helium is inert and, at constant volume, does not change the concentration or partial pressure of any participating species. (d) No. \(K\) depends only on temperature. Adding a reactant moves the system to a new position along the same \(K\).

Ladder 9 • Le Ch\^atelier: volume and pressure

CED 7.9 CED 7.10

Compressing a gaseous equilibrium shifts it toward the side with fewer moles of gas. Count the gas coefficients on each side — solids and liquids do not count.

I do: counting moles of gas

Predict the effect of halving the volume for: (i) \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 <=> 2NH3}\); (ii) \(\ce{H2 + I2 <=> 2HI}\).

(i) Left has \(1 + 3 = 4\) moles of gas; right has 2. Compressing raises all pressures, and the system relieves that by moving to the side with fewer particles: shifts right. (ii) Left has \(1 + 1 = 2\); right has 2. Equal, so compression has no effect on the position — there is no side with fewer moles to move toward. The rule in one line: equal moles of gas on both sides means volume changes do nothing. And note what a volume change never does: it does not alter \(K\). Only temperature does that.
YOUR TURN 9 — four questions
  1. Effect of compressing \(\ce{2NO2 <=> N2O4}\)?
    right, toward 1 mole
  2. Effect of expanding that same system?
    left
  3. Effect of compressing \(\ce{CO(g) + H2O(g) <=> CO2(g) + H2(g)}\)?
    none — 2 vs 2
  4. In \(\ce{CaCO3(s) <=> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\), how many moles of gas are on each side?
    0 left, 1 right

(a) right, toward 1 mole   (b) left   (c) none — 2 vs 2   (d) 0 left, 1 right

(a) Shifts right: 2 moles of gas become 1, so the product side relieves the pressure. (b) Shifts left, toward the side with more gas. (c) No effect — two moles of gas on each side. (d) Zero on the left (\(\ce{CaCO3}\) is a solid) and one on the right. Compressing therefore shifts it left, toward the solids. Solids are excluded from the count for exactly the same reason they are excluded from \(K\).

Ladder 10 • Le Ch\^atelier: temperature and catalysts

CED 7.9

Temperature is the only change that alters \(K\). A catalyst alters neither \(K\) nor the position.

I do: heat as a participant

For the exothermic \(\ce{2SO2 + O2 <=> 2SO3}\), \(\Delta H < 0\), state the effect of raising the temperature, and of adding a catalyst.

Treat heat as a product for an exothermic reaction: \(\ce{2SO2 + O2 <=> 2SO3 + heat}\). Raising the temperature adds heat, so the system shifts to consume it — to the left. Less \(\ce{SO3}\) at equilibrium. And \(K\) itself decreases. This is the one case where the constant genuinely changes value, because \(K\) is defined at a temperature. Adding a catalyst: nothing changes. It lowers the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions equally, so both rates rise in the same proportion. Equilibrium arrives sooner and lies in exactly the same place. The industrial tension this creates: a lower temperature would give more \(\ce{SO3}\), but too slowly to be useful. The Contact process compromises — moderate temperature plus a catalyst to make that temperature fast enough.
YOUR TURN 10 — four questions
  1. For an endothermic reaction, effect of raising the temperature?
    shifts right; \(K\) increases
  2. Which change alters the value of \(K\)?
    temperature only
  3. Effect of a catalyst on the equilibrium yield?
    none
  4. Why does a catalyst not shift the position?
    it speeds both directions equally

(a) shifts right; \(K\) increases   (b) temperature only   (c) none   (d) it speeds both directions equally

(a) Shifts right. For an endothermic reaction heat behaves as a reactant, so supplying it drives the reaction forward — and \(K\) increases. (b) Temperature, and only temperature. (c) None. The yield at equilibrium is unchanged; only the time taken to reach it is shorter. (d) Because it lowers the activation energy for the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount — both directions cross the same transition state. Both rates rise in the same proportion, so the point at which they become equal is exactly where it was.

Ladder 11 • Solubility equilibria and K\textsubscriptsp

CED 7.11 \(K_{sp}\) is just an equilibrium constant for a dissolving solid. The solid is omitted, and the stoichiometric coefficients become exponents — and multipliers of \(s\).
I do: two different stoichiometries

Find the molar solubility of \(\ce{AgCl}\) (\(K_{sp} = 1.8\times10^{-10}\)) and of \(\ce{PbI2}\) (\(K_{sp} = 7.1\times10^{-9}\)).

\(\ce{AgCl}\): \(\ce{AgCl(s) <=> Ag+ + Cl^-}\), a \(1:1\) dissolution, so \([\ce{Ag+}] = [\ce{Cl-}] = s\). \[ K_{sp} = s^2 \;\Rightarrow\; s = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-10}} = \mathbf{1.3\times10^{-5}~M} \] \(\ce{PbI2}\): \(\ce{PbI2(s) <=> Pb^2+ + 2I^-}\), so \([\ce{Pb^2+}] = s\) but \([\ce{I-}] = \mathbf{2}s\). \[ K_{sp} = (s)(2s)^2 = 4s^3 = 7.1\times10^{-9} \] \[ s^3 = 1.775\times10^{-9} \;\Rightarrow\; s = \mathbf{1.2\times10^{-3}~M} \] The factor of 2 is the entire difficulty. Writing \(K_{sp} = s^3\) instead of \(4s^3\) gives 1.9e-3 — wrong, and a very common answer. A warning about comparing: \(\ce{PbI2}\) has the smaller \(K_{sp}\) yet is nearly a hundred times more soluble than \(\ce{AgCl}\). \(K_{sp}\) values may only be compared directly between salts of the same stoichiometric type.
YOUR TURN 11 — four questions
  1. Write the \(K_{sp}\) expression for \(\ce{Ag2CrO4}\):
    \([\ce{Ag+}]^2[\ce{CrO4^2-}]\)
  2. Its \(K_{sp}\) is \(1.1\times10^{-12}\). Find its molar solubility.
  3. In that saturated solution, what is \([\ce{Ag+}]\)?
  4. Why can \(K_{sp}\) values not always be compared directly?
    different stoichiometries

(a) \([\ce{Ag+}]^2[\ce{CrO4^2-}]\)   (b) 6.5e-5 M   (c) 1.3e-4 M   (d) different stoichiometries

(a) \(K_{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}]^2[\ce{CrO4^2-}]\). (b) \([\ce{Ag+}] = 2s\), \([\ce{CrO4^2-}] = s\), so \(K_{sp} = (2s)^2(s) = 4s^3 = 1.1\times10^{-12}\); \(s^3 = 2.75\times10^{-13}\), giving \(s = \mathbf{6.5\times10^{-5}}\) M. (c) \(2s = \mathbf{1.3\times10^{-4}}\) M. (d) Because the relationship between \(K_{sp}\) and solubility depends on the dissolution stoichiometry. For an \(\ce{MX}\) salt \(K_{sp} = s^2\), for \(\ce{MX2}\) it is \(4s^3\). A smaller \(K_{sp}\) of a different type can still correspond to a larger solubility, as \(\ce{PbI2}\) against \(\ce{AgCl}\) shows.

Ladder 12 • The common-ion effect

CED 7.12

Adding an ion that already appears in the equilibrium pushes it backwards. It is Le Ch\^atelier applied to dissolving.

I do: how much less soluble?
\(\ce{AgCl}\) has \(K_{sp} = 1.8\times10^{-10}\). Compare its solubility in pure water with that in 0.10 M \(\ce{NaCl}\). In pure water: \(s = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-10}} = 1.3e-5\,\mathrm{M}\). In 0.10 M \(\ce{NaCl}\): chloride is already present at 0.10 M, overwhelmingly more than the dissolution could supply, so \([\ce{Cl-}] \approx 0.10\). \[ K_{sp} = [\ce{Ag+}](0.10) \;\Rightarrow\; [\ce{Ag+}] = s = \frac{1.8\times10^{-10}}{0.10} = \mathbf{1.8\times10^{-9}~M} \] That is about 7000 times less soluble. The common ion suppresses dissolution dramatically. And \(K_{sp}\) is unchanged throughout. What changed is how the fixed product of concentrations is distributed between the two ions — much more chloride, so far less silver.
YOUR TURN 12 — four questions
  1. Is \(\ce{AgCl}\) more or less soluble in 0.10 M \(\ce{AgNO3}\) than in water?
    less soluble
  2. Does \(K_{sp}\) change when a common ion is added?
    no
  3. Which direction does the dissolution equilibrium shift?
    left
  4. Would adding \(\ce{NaNO3}\) change \(\ce{AgCl}\)'s solubility?
    no — neither ion is common

(a) less soluble   (b) no   (c) left   (d) no — neither ion is common

(a) Less soluble. \(\ce{Ag+}\) is a common ion, so the equilibrium is pushed back toward the solid. (b) No. \(K_{sp}\) depends only on temperature. (c) To the left, toward undissolved solid. (d) No (to a first approximation). Neither \(\ce{Na+}\) nor \(\ce{NO3-}\) appears in the \(\ce{AgCl}\) dissolution equilibrium, so neither is a common ion and \(Q\) is unaffected. Only ions that appear in the equilibrium expression can shift it.
