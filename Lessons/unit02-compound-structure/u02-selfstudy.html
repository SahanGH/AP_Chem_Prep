Unit 2 • Compound Structure and Properties

Self-Study • CED 2.1–2.7, I do / You do

Eleven ladders • four YOUR TURN questions each • work alone, check after all four

How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a worked example in the solid-framed box, then four YOUR TURN questions in the dashed box — same skill, new substances, no help. Work all four before checking anything.

The habit that decides this unit. Almost every question here is answered in two moves: count the electrons, then reason about the attraction. Counting means valence electrons, domains, or charges. Reasoning means Coulomb's law — the product of the charges over the distance between them.

An explanation that says a species “wants a full octet” or “is more stable” earns zero on the AP exam. The octet rule tells you what the structure is; it never tells you why a property follows.

One scope note. For a central atom with more than four electron domains the CED asks for the shape only. You will not be asked for an \(\ce{sp^3d}\) or \(\ce{sp^3d^2}\) label, and none appears here.

Ladder 1 • Bond type from electronegativity

CED 2.1

Bonding is a continuum, not three boxes. The electronegativity difference \(\Delta\)EN tells you where on it a bond sits: near zero, the pair is shared equally; large, the electron is effectively transferred.

I do: placing three bonds on the continuum

Classify \(\ce{K-F}\), \(\ce{C-O}\) and \(\ce{H-H}\) using EN: H 2.1, C 2.5, O 3.5, F 4.0, K 0.8.

\(\ce{K-F}\): \(\Delta\)EN \(= 4.0 - 0.8 = 3.2\). Very large — the electron is transferred, giving \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{F-}\). Ionic. \(\ce{C-O}\): \(\Delta\)EN \(= 3.5 - 2.5 = 1.0\). Moderate — the pair is shared but pulled toward oxygen, leaving \(\delta+\) on C and \(\delta-\) on O. Polar covalent. \(\ce{H-H}\): \(\Delta\)EN \(= 0\). Identical atoms share equally. Nonpolar covalent. Read the boundaries as guides, not laws. Roughly: below 0.4 nonpolar, 0.4–1.7 polar, above 1.7 ionic. A bond at 1.6 is not categorically different from one at 1.8; the character shifts gradually.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. Classify \(\ce{Na-Br}\) (EN Na 0.9, Br 2.8):
    ionic, \(\Delta\)EN 1.9
  2. Classify \(\ce{N-H}\) (EN N 3.0, H 2.1):
    polar covalent, 0.9
  3. In \(\ce{P-Cl}\) (EN P 2.1, Cl 3.0), which atom carries \(\delta-\), and why?
    Cl, it is more electronegative
  4. Two bonds have \(\Delta\)EN of 1.6 and 1.8. Explain why calling one “covalent” and the other “ionic” is misleading.
    the continuum has no sharp boundary

(a) ionic, \(\Delta\)EN 1.9   (b) polar covalent, 0.9   (c) Cl, it is more electronegative   (d) the continuum has no sharp boundary

(a) \(\Delta\)EN \(= 2.8 - 0.9 = 1.9\), above 1.7: ionic. (b) \(\Delta\)EN \(= 3.0 - 2.1 = 0.9\): polar covalent. (c) Chlorine. It is the more electronegative atom, so it draws the shared pair closer and gains the partial negative charge; phosphorus is left \(\delta+\). (d) Because bonding is a continuum. A bond at 1.6 and one at 1.8 have almost identical character — both are strongly polar with substantial charge separation. The 1.7 line is a convenient convention, not a physical boundary where the nature of the bond suddenly changes.

Ladder 2 • Coulomb's law and lattice energy

CED 2.2

Lattice energy is the energy released when gaseous ions come together into a crystal. It is Coulombic, so only two things matter: the product of the charges and the distance between ion centres. \[ \text{attraction} \;\propto\; \frac{q_1 q_2}{d} \]

I do: one factor at a time

Rank \(\ce{LiF}\), \(\ce{NaF}\) and \(\ce{KF}\) by lattice energy, then compare \(\ce{NaF}\) with \(\ce{MgO}\).

Within the fluoride series, every compound pairs a \(1+\) with a \(1-\), so the charge product is the same and cannot decide anything. Distance decides. Cation radii rise \(\ce{Li+}\) (76) \(<\) \(\ce{Na+}\) (102) \(<\) \(\ce{K+}\) (138) pm, so \(d\) rises and the attraction falls: \[ \ce{LiF} > \ce{NaF} > \ce{KF} \quad (1030 > 910 > 808~\text{kJ/mol}) \] \(\ce{NaF}\) against \(\ce{MgO}\): now the charges change. \(\ce{NaF}\) is \((+1)(-1) = 1\); \(\ce{MgO}\) is \((+2)(-2) = 4\) — four times the charge product. The ions are also smaller (212 pm against 235 pm), so \(d\) is shorter too. Both factors point the same way, and the gap is enormous: 3795  against 910 kJ/mol. The method: check charge first. If the charges match, distance decides. If they differ, charge almost always dominates.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Larger lattice energy, \(\ce{NaCl}\) or \(\ce{NaF}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{NaF}\) — \(\ce{F-}\) smaller, shorter \(d\)
  2. Larger lattice energy, \(\ce{CaO}\) or \(\ce{KF}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{CaO}\) — charge product 4 vs 1
  3. \(\ce{MgO}\) melts far higher than \(\ce{NaF}\). Connect this to lattice energy.
    stronger attractions need more energy to break
  4. Explain why comparing \(\ce{MgO}\) with \(\ce{NaCl}\) does not isolate the effect of charge.
    charge and size both differ

(a) \(\ce{NaF}\) — \(\ce{F-}\) smaller, shorter \(d\)   (b) \(\ce{CaO}\) — charge product 4 vs 1   (c) stronger attractions need more energy to break   (d) charge and size both differ

(a) \(\ce{NaF}\). Same charges, so distance decides: \(\ce{F-}\) (133 pm) is smaller than \(\ce{Cl-}\) (181 pm), so \(d\) is shorter and the attraction stronger. (b) \(\ce{CaO}\). Charge product \((+2)(-2) = 4\) against \(\ce{KF}\)'s \((+1)(-1) = 1\). Charge dominates, and \(\ce{CaO}\)'s ions are smaller too. (c) Melting means giving ions enough energy to break out of the lattice. A larger lattice energy means stronger electrostatic attractions holding them, so more energy — a higher temperature — is needed. (d) Because both variables change at once: \(\ce{MgO}\) has the larger charge product and the shorter internuclear distance. Any difference cannot be attributed to charge alone. To isolate charge you would compare species of similar size but different charge.

Ladder 3 • Bond order, length and energy

CED 2.2

Within the same pair of atoms, more shared pairs means a shorter, stronger bond. Bond order runs with energy and against length.

I do: a carbon–carbon series

Rank \(\ce{C-C}\), \(\ce{C=C}\) and \(\ce{C#C}\) by bond length and by bond energy.

Bond energies: 347, 614, 839 kJ/mol.

Energy rises with bond order: \(\ce{C-C}\) \(<\) \(\ce{C=C}\) \(<\) \(\ce{C#C}\). Each extra shared pair adds attraction between the two nuclei and the electron density between them. Length falls as order rises: \(\ce{C#C}\) \(<\) \(\ce{C=C}\) \(<\) \(\ce{C-C}\). More shared electron density pulls the nuclei closer together. Note the energies are not simple multiples — 614 is not twice 347, and 839 is not three times it. The second and third pairs are pi bonds, whose side-on overlap is less effective than the first bond's end-on overlap.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. Rank \(\ce{N-N}\) (163), \(\ce{N=N}\) (418), \(\ce{N#N}\) (941) by bond length, shortest first.
    \(\ce{N#N}\) \(<\) \(\ce{N=N}\) \(<\) \(\ce{N-N}\)
  2. \(\ce{H-F}\) 567, \(\ce{H-Cl}\) 431, \(\ce{H-Br}\) 366, \(\ce{H-I}\) 299 kJ/mol. Explain the trend.
    larger halogen, longer bond, weaker attraction
  3. Why is \(\ce{N2}\) so unreactive?
    the triple bond is extremely strong
  4. In \(\ce{O2}\) (495) and \(\ce{F2}\) (154), the bond order falls from 2 to 1. Is that the whole explanation for the energy difference?
    largely, but lone-pair repulsion in \(\ce{F2}\) also weakens it

(a) \(\ce{N#N}\) \(<\) \(\ce{N=N}\) \(<\) \(\ce{N-N}\)   (b) larger halogen, longer bond, weaker attraction   (c) the triple bond is extremely strong   (d) largely, but lone-pair repulsion in \(\ce{F2}\) also weakens it

(a) \(\ce{N#N}\) \(<\) \(\ce{N=N}\) \(<\) \(\ce{N-N}\). Higher bond order draws the nuclei closer. (b) Down the halogen group the atom gets larger, so the \(\ce{H-X}\) bond is longer and the shared pair sits further from both nuclei. The Coulombic attraction holding the bond is correspondingly weaker, so the bond energy falls steadily. (c) Its \(\ce{N#N}\) triple bond is one of the strongest known (941 kJ/mol). Any reaction must begin by breaking it, which demands an enormous energy input, so nitrogen is kinetically sluggish even where reaction is thermodynamically favourable. (d) Largely, but not entirely. Bond order accounts for most of it. \(\ce{F2}\) is also anomalously weak for a single bond because the two small fluorine atoms are close together and their lone pairs repel strongly, further destabilizing the bond — which is why \(\ce{F-F}\) (154) is weaker even than \(\ce{Cl-Cl}\) (243).

Ladder 4 • Properties of ionic solids

CED 2.3

Every property of an ionic solid traces back to the same fact: charged particles locked in a rigid lattice, held by strong electrostatic attractions.

I do: four properties, one cause

Explain why \(\ce{NaCl}\) is hard, brittle, high-melting, and conducts only when molten or dissolved.

Hard and high-melting: each ion is attracted to all its oppositely charged neighbours throughout the lattice. Deforming or melting the solid means overcoming many strong Coulombic attractions at once. Brittle: a blow displaces one plane of ions by a single lattice position. Ions of like charge are then brought face to face, attraction along that plane becomes strong repulsion, and the crystal splits cleanly. Conductivity: conduction requires mobile charges. In the solid the ions are fixed — charges are present but cannot move. Melting or dissolving frees them, and the liquid or solution conducts. The contrast that makes the point: a metal, struck the same way, bends. Its delocalized electrons flow with the displaced layer and keep binding it, so no like-charge repulsion ever arises.
YOUR TURN 4 — four questions
  1. Why does solid \(\ce{KBr}\) not conduct, while molten \(\ce{KBr}\) does?
    ions fixed vs mobile
  2. \(\ce{MgO}\) is harder than \(\ce{NaCl}\). Explain Coulombically.
    charge product 4 vs 1
  3. Why does an ionic crystal shatter rather than bend?
    like charges brought adjacent
  4. A solid dissolves in water and the solution conducts. Does that prove the solid was ionic?
    no — a molecular acid also ionizes

(a) ions fixed vs mobile   (b) charge product 4 vs 1   (c) like charges brought adjacent   (d) no — a molecular acid also ionizes

(a) Conduction needs mobile charge carriers. In solid \(\ce{KBr}\) the ions sit in fixed lattice positions; melting frees them to migrate, so the melt conducts. (b) \(\ce{MgO}\) pairs \(2+\) with \(2-\), a charge product of 4, against \(\ce{NaCl}\)'s 1; its ions are also smaller, so \(d\) is shorter. Much stronger attractions make the lattice far harder to deform. (c) A blow shifts one layer by one ion position, bringing like charges adjacent. Attraction along that plane is replaced by strong repulsion, which drives the layers apart and splits the crystal. (d) No. A molecular substance that ionizes in water — HCl, for instance — also gives a conducting solution despite being covalent as a pure substance. The stronger evidence for an ionic solid is that it conducts when molten, since that requires ions to have been present in the solid itself.

Ladder 5 • Metals and alloys

CED 2.4

A metal is a lattice of cations in a sea of delocalized electrons. Every metallic property follows from those electrons being free to move.

I do: conductivity, malleability, alloys

Explain metallic conduction and malleability, and why adding a second element hardens the metal.

Conduction: the valence electrons belong to the whole lattice, not to individual atoms. Under a potential difference they drift through the solid, carrying charge. Malleability: when layers of cations slide over one another, the electron sea flows with them and continues to bind the lattice. Nothing brings like charges together, so the metal deforms instead of fracturing. Alloying: atoms of a different size disrupt the regular layers. Layers can no longer slide smoothly past one another, so the alloy resists deformation — it is harder and stronger, though usually less ductile.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. Why do metals conduct heat as well as electricity?
    mobile electrons carry kinetic energy
  2. Brass is copper with zinc. Why is it harder than pure copper?
    zinc atoms disrupt the layers
  3. Why is an interstitial alloy such as steel usually harder than a substitutional one?
    small atoms wedge into gaps and pin the layers
  4. Both metals and molten ionic solids conduct. State how the carriers differ.
    electrons vs ions

(a) mobile electrons carry kinetic energy   (b) zinc atoms disrupt the layers   (c) small atoms wedge into gaps and pin the layers   (d) electrons vs ions

(a) The same delocalized electrons carry kinetic energy as well as charge. Heated at one end, they move faster and transfer that energy rapidly through the lattice by collision. (b) Zinc atoms differ in size from copper, disrupting the regular layers so that they cannot slide over one another as easily. (c) An interstitial alloy adds small atoms (carbon in iron) that wedge into the gaps between the metal atoms, pinning the layers directly. A substitutional alloy merely swaps in atoms of similar size, which disrupts the packing less. (d) In a metal the mobile carriers are electrons, and the cations stay put. In a molten ionic solid the carriers are the ions themselves, which physically migrate through the liquid.

Ladder 6 • Drawing Lewis structures

CED 2.5

Count the valence electrons first — adjusting for charge — then build a skeleton, complete the outer atoms' octets, and convert lone pairs to multiple bonds only if the central atom is short.

I do: a neutral molecule and an anion

Draw \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{CO3^2-}\).

\(\ce{CO2}\). Valence electrons: \(4 + 2(6) = \mathbf{16}\). Skeleton \(\ce{O-C-O}\) uses 4. Completing octets on both oxygens uses the other 12 — but carbon then has only 4. Convert one lone pair on each oxygen into a bond: \(\ce{O=C=O}\), with two lone pairs on each oxygen. Every atom now has an octet, and the total is still 16. ✓ \(\ce{CO3^2-}\). Valence electrons: \(4 + 3(6) + \mathbf{2} = \mathbf{24}\) — the \(2-\) charge adds two. Carbon central, three oxygens around it. One \(\ce{C=O}\) and two \(\ce{C-O}\), with the whole structure in brackets carrying \(2-\). The single most common error is forgetting to adjust for the charge. An anion has more electrons than the neutral atoms supply; a cation has fewer.

Ladder 7 • Formal charge

CED 2.6 \[ \text{formal charge} = \text{valence electrons} - \text{lone-pair electrons} - \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{bonding electrons}) \] Formal charges must sum to the overall charge on the species. The best structure keeps them small, and puts any negative charge on the most electronegative atom.
I do: nitrate

Assign formal charges in \(\ce{NO3-}\), drawn with one \(\ce{N=O}\) and two \(\ce{N-O}\).

Nitrogen: 5 valence, no lone pairs, 8 bonding electrons: \(5 - 0 - 4 = \mathbf{+1}\). Doubly bonded O: 6 valence, 4 lone-pair electrons, 4 bonding: \(6 - 4 - 2 = \mathbf{0}\). Each singly bonded O: 6 valence, 6 lone-pair electrons, 2 bonding: \(6 - 6 - 1 = \mathbf{-1}\). Check the sum: \((+1) + 0 + (-1) + (-1) = -1\), matching the ion's charge. ✓ If your formal charges do not sum to the overall charge, the structure is drawn wrong — go back before doing anything else.

Oxygen is more electronegative than nitrogen and so accommodates negative charge better; the arrangement placing \(-1\) on the more electronegative atom is the larger contributor.

Ladder 8 • Resonance

CED 2.6

When a double bond can be placed in more than one equivalent position, no single Lewis structure is correct. The real species is the average — the electrons are delocalized. It does not flip between structures.

I do: nitrate's three structures

Explain why \(\ce{NO3-}\) needs three resonance structures, and predict its bond lengths.

Why three: the double bond can go to any one of the three oxygens, and the three choices are equivalent. What is real: not one structure, and not rapid switching between them. The extra bonding pair is delocalized over all three \(\ce{N-O}\) positions simultaneously. Bond lengths: all three are identical, and each is intermediate between a normal \(\ce{N-O}\) single bond and a normal \(\ce{N=O}\) double bond. Every position has the same bond order, about \(1\tfrac{1}{3}\). The experimental point: measurement finds three equal bond lengths. A structure with one short and two long bonds would predict otherwise, which is exactly why the single Lewis structure is known to be inadequate.
YOUR TURN 8 — four questions
  1. How many equivalent resonance structures does \(\ce{CO3^2-}\) have?
    three
  2. Predict the relative \(\ce{C-O}\) bond lengths in \(\ce{CO3^2-}\).
    all equal, between single and double
  3. \(\ce{O3}\) has two resonance structures. What does this predict about its two \(\ce{O-O}\) bonds?
    identical, bond order 1.5
  4. Why is “the ion rapidly switches between structures” wrong?
    the electrons are delocalized, not alternating

(a) three   (b) all equal, between single and double   (c) identical, bond order 1.5   (d) the electrons are delocalized, not alternating

(a) Three — the double bond can sit on any of the three oxygens. (b) All three identical, each intermediate between a \(\ce{C-O}\) single and a \(\ce{C=O}\) double bond; bond order about \(1\tfrac{1}{3}\). (c) The two \(\ce{O-O}\) bonds are identical, each of bond order 1.5 — intermediate between single and double. Measurement confirms this. (d) Because nothing is moving between structures. The resonance structures are limitations of the Lewis notation, not states the molecule occupies. The real electron distribution is a single, unchanging, delocalized one that no individual Lewis structure can draw.

Ladder 9 • VSEPR: shapes and angles

CED 2.7

Count the electron domains on the central atom — each bond (single, double or triple) and each lone pair is one domain. Domains repel and spread as far apart as possible. The shape is then named from the positions of the atoms only.

I do: same domain count, three different shapes

Give the shape and approximate bond angle for \(\ce{CH4}\), \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\).

All three have four domains, so all three are based on a tetrahedron. What differs is how many domains are lone pairs.

\(\ce{CH4}\) — 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs: tetrahedral, 109.5 °. \(\ce{NH3}\) — 3 bonds, 1 lone pair: trigonal pyramidal, about 107 °. \(\ce{H2O}\) — 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs: bent, about 104.5 °. Why the angle shrinks: a lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it spreads out closer to the central atom and takes up more angular space than a bonding pair. Each lone pair squeezes the remaining bonds together, so the angle drops with every one added.
YOUR TURN 9 — four questions
  1. Shape and angle of \(\ce{BF3}\):
    trigonal planar, 120 °
  2. Shape and angle of \(\ce{SO2}\):
    bent, just under 120 °
  3. Shape of \(\ce{SF6}\) (six domains, no lone pairs):
    octahedral
  4. \(\ce{CO2}\) has two double bonds yet only two domains. Explain.
    a multiple bond counts as one domain

(a) trigonal planar, 120 °   (b) bent, just under 120 °   (c) octahedral   (d) a multiple bond counts as one domain

(a) Three domains, no lone pair: trigonal planar, 120 °. (b) Three domains, one lone pair: bent, a little under 120 ° because the lone pair takes more space. (c) Octahedral, with 90 ° between adjacent fluorines. (The CED asks only for the shape here — no orbital label is required.) (d) A domain is a region of electron density, not a count of pairs. All the electrons of a double bond lie between the same two nuclei, so they occupy one region and repel other domains as a single unit. Carbon therefore has two domains and \(\ce{CO2}\) is linear.

Ladder 10 • Hybridization and sigma/pi counting

CED 2.7

The central atom mixes exactly as many atomic orbitals as it has domains: 2 domains \(\ce{sp}\), 3 domains \(\ce{sp^2}\), 4 domains \(\ce{sp^3}\). Every single bond is one sigma; every double bond is one sigma plus one pi; every triple bond is one sigma plus two pi.

I do: hybridization and bond count together

For \(\ce{HCN}\), give the hybridization of carbon and count the sigma and pi bonds.

Domains on carbon: one bond to H, one triple bond to N. The triple bond is a single domain, so carbon has two domains — hence \(\ce{sp}\) hybridized, and the molecule is linear. Bond count: \(\ce{H-C}\) is one sigma. \(\ce{C#N}\) is one sigma plus two pi. Total: 2 sigma, 2 pi. The rule that never fails: the first bond between any two atoms is sigma; every additional bond between that same pair is pi.
YOUR TURN 10 — four questions
  1. Hybridization of B in \(\ce{BF3}\):
    \(\ce{sp^2}\)
  2. Hybridization of O in \(\ce{H2O}\):
    \(\ce{sp^3}\)
  3. Sigma and pi bonds in \(\ce{C2H4}\) (ethene):
    five sigma, one pi
  4. Why does the \(\ce{C=C}\) bond in ethene resist rotation while the \(\ce{C-C}\) bond in ethane does not?
    rotation would destroy the pi overlap

(a) \(\ce{sp^2}\)   (b) \(\ce{sp^3}\)   (c) five sigma, one pi   (d) rotation would destroy the pi overlap

(a) Three domains, no lone pair: \(\ce{sp^2}\). (b) Four domains — two bonds and two lone pairs: \(\ce{sp^3}\). Counting only the bonds would wrongly give \(\ce{sp^2}\). (c) Four \(\ce{C-H}\) sigma plus one \(\ce{C-C}\) sigma \(=\) five sigma; the second component of the \(\ce{C=C}\) is one pi. (d) Ethane's \(\ce{C-C}\) is a single sigma bond, and sigma overlap is cylindrically symmetric about the bond axis — twisting does not reduce it, so rotation is nearly free. Ethene's double bond includes a pi bond formed by two parallel \(p\) orbitals; rotating one end turns them out of alignment, and at 90 ° the overlap is zero. Rotation would mean breaking the pi bond, which costs far too much energy.

Ladder 11 • Molecular polarity

CED 2.1 CED 2.7

A molecule is polar only if it has polar bonds and a shape that prevents their dipoles cancelling. Shape decides — which is why this ladder comes after VSEPR.

I do: same bonds, opposite verdicts
\(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) both contain polar bonds. Explain why only water is polar. \(\ce{CO2}\) is linear. The two \(\ce{C=O}\) bond dipoles are equal and point in exactly opposite directions, so they cancel completely. Net dipole zero: nonpolar, despite strongly polar bonds. \(\ce{H2O}\) is bent, because oxygen's two lone pairs force it so. The two \(\ce{O-H}\) dipoles no longer oppose each other and add to a net dipole pointing toward the oxygen: polar. The method: identify the polar bonds, then ask whether the shape is symmetric enough for them to cancel. Never answer from the bonds alone.
YOUR TURN 11 — four questions
  1. Is \(\ce{CCl4}\) polar? Justify.
    no — tetrahedral, dipoles cancel
  2. Is \(\ce{CH3Cl}\) polar? Justify.
    yes — asymmetric
  3. Is \(\ce{NH3}\) polar? Justify.
    yes — pyramidal
  4. \(\ce{BF3}\) and \(\ce{NH3}\) both have three bonds to the central atom, but only one is polar. Explain.
    \(\ce{BF3}\) is planar and symmetric; \(\ce{NH3}\) has a lone pair and is pyramidal

(a) no — tetrahedral, dipoles cancel   (b) yes — asymmetric   (c) yes — pyramidal   (d) \(\ce{BF3}\) is planar and symmetric; \(\ce{NH3}\) has a lone pair and is pyramidal

(a) Nonpolar. The four \(\ce{C-Cl}\) bonds are polar, but the tetrahedral arrangement is perfectly symmetric and the four dipoles cancel. (b) Polar. Replacing one chlorine with hydrogen destroys that symmetry: the \(\ce{C-Cl}\) dipole is no longer balanced by an identical bond opposite it, so a net dipole remains. (c) Polar. Three \(\ce{N-H}\) dipoles plus a lone pair on a pyramidal nitrogen; the dipoles do not cancel and the net points along the lone-pair axis. (d) \(\ce{BF3}\) has three domains and no lone pair, so it is trigonal planar — perfectly symmetric, and the three dipoles cancel. \(\ce{NH3}\) has four domains, one being a lone pair, so it is pyramidal — unsymmetric, and the dipoles add. Same number of bonds, different domain count, opposite result.
