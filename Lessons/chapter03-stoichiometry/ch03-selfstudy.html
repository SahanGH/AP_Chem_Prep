Ladder 1 • Formula from percent composition

ZUM §3.7

The four steps, always the same: assume 100 g so percents become grams; convert each element to moles; divide everything by the smallest; multiply to whole numbers if needed. The molecular formula is the empirical formula times \(M/M_{\text{empirical}}\).

I do: caffeine

A stimulant is 49.47% C, 5.19% H, 28.86% N, and 16.48% O, with molar mass 194.20 g/mol. Find both formulas.

Assume 100 g, convert to moles: \[ \ce{C}: \frac{49.47}{12.01} = 4.119 \qquad \ce{H}: \frac{5.19}{1.008} = 5.15 \qquad \ce{N}: \frac{28.86}{14.01} = 2.060 \qquad \ce{O}: \frac{16.48}{16.00} = 1.030 \] Divide by the smallest (1.030): \[ \ce{C}: 4.00 \qquad \ce{H}: 5.00 \qquad \ce{N}: 2.00 \qquad \ce{O}: 1.00 \] Empirical \(\ce{C4H5N2O}\), with \(M_{\text{emp}} = 4(12.01) + 5(1.008) + 2(14.01) + 16.00 = 97.10\,\mathrm{g/mol}\). Molecular: \(194.20 / 97.10 = 2.00\), so \(\boxed{\ce{C8H10N4O2}}\) — caffeine.

Ladder 2 • Combustion analysis, C and H only

ZUM §3.7

In excess oxygen every C atom lands in a \(\ce{CO2}\) and every H atom in an \(\ce{H2O}\) — so count the atoms there. Two H per water is the step most often dropped.

I do: an unknown fuel gas

Burning 0.5812 g of a compound of C and H gives 1.760 g \(\ce{CO2}\) and 0.901 g \(\ce{H2O}\); the molar mass is 58.12 g/mol.

Carbon: \(\dfrac{1.760}{44.01} = 0.03999\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{CO2} \Rightarrow 0.03999\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{C} \Rightarrow 0.4803\,\mathrm{g}\) Hydrogen (two per water): \(\dfrac{0.901}{18.02} = 0.05000\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{H2O} \Rightarrow 0.1000\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{H} \Rightarrow 0.1008\,\mathrm{g}\) Mass check: \(0.4803 + 0.1008 = 0.5811\,\mathrm{g}\) — the whole sample. C and H really are all there is. Ratio: \(\ce{H}/\ce{C} = 0.1000/0.03999 = 2.50 = 5/2\), so the empirical formula is \(\ce{C2H5}\), \(M_{\text{emp}} = 29.06\,\mathrm{g/mol}\). Then \(58.12/29.06 = 2.00\): \(\boxed{\ce{C4H10}}\), butane. Note the 2.50 was an instruction to multiply by 2, never to round.

\(\ce{H}/\ce{C} = 3.00 \to\) empirical \(\ce{CH3}\); \(30.07/15.03 = 2 \to\) molecular \(\ce{C2H6}\) (ethane)

\(\ce{H}/\ce{C} = 3.00 \to\) empirical \(\ce{CH3}\); \(30.07/15.03 = 2 \to\) molecular \(\ce{C2H6}\) (ethane)

Ladder 3 • Combustion analysis with oxygen in the compound

ZUM §3.7

Oxygen in the sample cannot be measured directly — it ends up in the same \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) as the oxygen from the air. Find C and H as before; oxygen is whatever sample mass is left.

I do: a compound of C, H, and O

Burning 1.000 g of a compound of C, H, and O gives 1.911 g \(\ce{CO2}\) and 1.174 g \(\ce{H2O}\); the molar mass is 46.07 g/mol.

C and H first: \[ \ce{C}: \frac{1.911}{44.01} = 0.04342\,\mathrm{mol} \to 0.5215\,\mathrm{g} \qquad \ce{H}: 2 \times \frac{1.174}{18.02} = 0.1303\,\mathrm{mol} \to 0.1313\,\mathrm{g} \] Oxygen by difference: \[ m_{\ce{O}} = 1.000 - 0.5215 - 0.1313 = 0.347\,\mathrm{g} \Rightarrow \frac{0.347}{16.00} = 0.0217\,\mathrm{mol} \] Divide by the smallest (0.0217): \(\ce{C}: 2.00\), \(\ce{H}: 6.00\), \(\ce{O}: 1.00\) — empirical \(\ce{C2H6O}\), \(M_{\text{emp}} = 46.07\,\mathrm{g/mol} = M\), so \(\boxed{\ce{C2H6O}}\), ethanol. Multiplier 1 again.

Ladder 4 • Reading a chemical equation

ZUM §3.8

An equation states how atoms rearrange. Coefficients are ratios — of molecules, or equally of moles — never an inventory of what you have. Atoms and mass are conserved; molecules and moles need not be. And balancing is done only with coefficients: changing a subscript changes the substance.

I do: reading an equation aloud
\[ \ce{4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) -> 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)} \] Everything this equation asserts:
  • Four ammonia molecules react with five oxygen molecules to give four nitrogen monoxide molecules and six water molecules — all as gases.
  • Equally: 4 mol \(\ce{NH3}\) react with 5 mol \(\ce{O2}\) per 4 mol \(\ce{NO}\) and 6 mol \(\ce{H2O}\). Or 1 mol \(\ce{NH3}\) per \(\tfrac{5}{4}\) mol \(\ce{O2}\) — the ratio is the content, not the absolute numbers.
  • Atom check: N \(4 = 4\); H \(12 = 12\); O \(10 = 4 + 6\). Conserved.
  • Nine molecules become ten. Fine — molecule count is not conserved; atoms are.
What it does not assert: that 4 grams of ammonia react with 5 grams of oxygen. Coefficients never count grams.

Ladder 5 • Balancing by inspection

ZUM §3.9

Strategy: start from the most complicated molecule; save lone elements (\(\ce{O2}\), metals) for last, because their coefficient can be set freely; if an odd atom count forces a fraction, use it, then double everything. Count every atom at the end.

I do: methanol combustion
\[ \ce{CH3OH(l) + O2(g) -> CO2(g) + H2O(g)} \qquad\text{(unbalanced)} \] Methanol first — 1 C gives \(1\,\ce{CO2}\); 4 H give \(2\,\ce{H2O}\): \[ \ce{CH3OH + O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O} \] Oxygen last: right side \(2 + 2 = 4\) O; methanol already carries 1, so \(\ce{O2}\) must supply 3 atoms \(= \tfrac{3}{2}\) molecules: \[ \ce{CH3OH + \tfrac{3}{2}O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O} \] Clear the fraction — double every coefficient: \[ \boxed{\;\ce{2CH3OH(l) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 4H2O(g)}\;} \] Count: C \(2 = 2\); H \(8 = 8\); O \(2 + 6 = 4 + 4\). Balanced.

Ladder 6 • Mass-to-mass stoichiometry

ZUM §3.10
grams of A \(\xrightarrow{\;\div M_A\;}\) moles of A \(\xrightarrow{\;\text{coefficients}\;}\) moles of B \(\xrightarrow{\;\times M_B\;}\) grams of B

Grams never talk to grams directly — the equation's coefficients only convert moles.

I do: oxygen from potassium chlorate, both directions

Heating potassium chlorate releases oxygen: \(\ce{2KClO3(s) -> 2KCl(s) + 3O2(g)}\), with \(M_{\ce{KClO3}} = 122.55\,\mathrm{g/mol}\).

(a) Mass of \(\ce{O2}\) from 12.25 g \(\ce{KClO3}\): \[ \frac{12.25}{122.55} = 0.09996\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{KClO3} \;\xrightarrow{\times 3/2}\; 0.1499\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{O2} \;\xrightarrow{\times 32.00}\; \mathbf{4.80~g} \] (b) Mass of \(\ce{KClO3}\) needed for 1.00 g \(\ce{O2}\) — same machine, run backward: \[ \frac{1.00}{32.00} = 0.03125\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{O2} \;\xrightarrow{\times 2/3}\; 0.02083\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{KClO3} \;\xrightarrow{\times 122.55}\; \mathbf{2.55~g} \]

The ratio flipped from \(\tfrac{3}{2}\) to \(\tfrac{2}{3}\) when the direction flipped — read it off the coefficients each time; never guess a direction.

Ladder 7 • Limiting reactant, yield, and leftovers

ZUM §3.11

When amounts of two reactants are given: convert both to moles, then use the mole ratio to ask whether what is present covers what is required. The limiting reactant fixes everything — theoretical yield comes from it alone, and the leftover is \((\text{present}) - (\text{consumed})\). The reactant with fewer grams, or even fewer moles, is not automatically the limiting one.

I do: burning magnesium, the whole problem

4.50 g of Mg burns in 6.00 g of \(\ce{O2}\): \(\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}\). Limiting reactant, theoretical yield, leftover mass — and then the percent yield if 6.50 g of MgO is actually collected.

Moles first: \[ \ce{Mg}: \frac{4.50}{24.31} = 0.1851\,\mathrm{mol} \qquad \ce{O2}: \frac{6.00}{32.00} = 0.1875\,\mathrm{mol} \] Limiting? The Mg present needs \(0.1851 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.0926\,\mathrm{mol}\) of \(\ce{O2}\); 0.1875 mol is available — plenty. So Mg is limiting even though the two mole amounts are nearly equal: the \(2{:}1\) ratio decides, not the raw numbers. Theoretical yield (from Mg only): \(0.1851 \times \tfrac{2}{2} = 0.1851\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\ce{MgO}\) \(\times\, 40.31 = \mathbf{7.46~g}\). Leftover \(\ce{O2}\): \(0.1875 - 0.0926 = 0.0949\,\mathrm{mol}\) \(\times\, 32.00 = \mathbf{3.04~g}\). Mass check: in \(4.50 + 6.00 = 10.50\) g; out \(7.46 + 3.04 = 10.50\) g. Books balanced. Percent yield: \(\dfrac{6.50}{7.46} \times 100 = \mathbf{87.1\%}\) — actual over theoretical, always.
