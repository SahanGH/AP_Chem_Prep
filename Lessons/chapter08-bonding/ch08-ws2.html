1. Bond order, length, and energy:
  1. Rank \(\ce{C-C}\), \(\ce{C=C}\), \(\ce{C#C}\) by increasing bond length.
  2. Is a triple bond three times as strong as a single bond? Support with the data.
  3. Explain why breaking a bond is always endothermic.
2. Compute \(\Delta H\) from bond energies:
  1. \(\ce{H2(g) + Br2(g) -> 2 HBr(g)}\)
  2. \(\ce{N2(g) + 3 H2(g) -> 2 NH3(g)}\)
  3. \(\ce{2 H2(g) + O2(g) -> 2 H2O(g)}\)
3. The accepted value for problem 2(b) is -92.2 kJ, but bond energies give -109 kJ.
  1. Explain the discrepancy.
  2. Would you expect better or worse agreement for \(\ce{H2 + F2 -> 2 HF}\), where every species is diatomic? Explain.
4. Compute \(\Delta H\) for the hydrogenation of ethene, \(\ce{C2H4(g) + H2(g) -> C2H6(g)}\). Ethene contains one \(\ce{C=C}\) and four \(\ce{C-H}\); ethane contains one \(\ce{C-C}\) and six \(\ce{C-H}\).
5. A student computes \(\Delta H\) as “bonds formed minus bonds broken” and reports +109 kJ for ammonia synthesis. Explain the error and how to catch it without redoing the arithmetic.
6. Predict, without calculating, whether \(\ce{2 HCl(g) -> H2(g) + Cl2(g)}\) is exothermic or endothermic. Justify from the bond energies alone.
