How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a fully worked example in the solid-framed box, then four YOUR TURN questions in the dashed box — same skill, new numbers, no help.

  1. Work all four before checking anything.
  2. Check against the gray check: line. All four right first time earns the tick on the tracker (last page).
  3. Any misses: re-read the worked example, then redo the missed ones from a blank page — not by reading the answer again.
Two habits that prevent most lost marks in this unit. Always convert to moles before doing anything else with a mass — almost every Unit 1 error is a comparison made in grams that should have been made in moles. And when you explain a periodic trend, name nuclear charge, distance and shielding. An answer that says an atom “wants a full shell” earns nothing on the AP exam.

Ladder 1 • Moles, mass and particles

CED 1.1

The mole is a counting unit: one mole is \(6.022\times10^{23}\) particles. The molar mass in g/mol is the bridge between the mass you can weigh and the number of particles you cannot.

I do: mass to moles to atoms

How many moles, and how many atoms, are in 12.5 g of calcium?

Mass to moles — divide by the molar mass: \[ n = \frac{12.5}{40.08} = \mathbf{0.312~mol} \] Moles to atoms — multiply by Avogadro's number: \[ 0.3119 \times 6.022\times10^{23} = \mathbf{1.88\times10^{23}} \text{ atoms} \] Sense check: 12.5 g is well under one molar mass (40.08 g), so the answer must be under one mole. It is.

(a) 0.150 mol   (b) 5.57e22 atoms   (c) 11.5 g   (d) helium — far smaller molar mass, so the same mass is many more moles

(a) \(8.40/55.85 = \mathbf{0.150}\) mol. (b) \(2.25/24.31 = 0.0926\) mol; \(\times 6.022\times10^{23} = \mathbf{5.57\times10^{22}}\) atoms. Ladder 2 • Molar mass of a compound

CED 1.1

A subscript outside a bracket multiplies everything inside it. That single rule is where most molar-mass errors come from.

I do: a compound with brackets

Find the molar mass of \(\ce{Ca(NO3)2}\).

Read the formula first. There is one calcium, and two nitrate groups. Each nitrate is one N and three O, so the compound holds 2 N and 6 O — not 1 N and 3 O. \[ 40.08 + 2\,[\,14.01 + 3(16.00)\,] = 40.08 + 2(62.01) = \mathbf{164.1~g/mol} \] The trap: forgetting to distribute the 2 gives 102.1, and every later answer built on it is wrong.

Ladder 3 • Mass spectra and average atomic mass

CED 1.2

A mass spectrum plots mass on the horizontal axis and abundance on the vertical. Peak position gives isotope mass; peak height gives how common that isotope is. The tabulated atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average of the two.

I do: two isotopes to one atomic mass

Copper has two isotopes: \(\ce{^{63}Cu}\), 62.930 u, 69.17 %; and \(\ce{^{65}Cu}\), 64.928 u, 30.83 %. Find the average atomic mass.

Weight each mass by its fractional abundance and add: \[ (62.930)(0.6917) + (64.928)(0.3083) = 43.53 + 20.02 = \mathbf{63.55~u} \] Sense check: the answer must land between the two isotope masses, and closer to the more abundant one. 63.55 sits nearer 62.930, as it must with 69 % of atoms being the lighter isotope. The trap: averaging 62.930 and 64.928 without weighting gives 63.93 — wrong, because it assumes equal abundances.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. Lithium: 6.015 u at 7.59 %, 7.016 u at 92.41 %. Average?
  2. Chlorine: 34.969 u at 75.78 %, 36.966 u at 24.22 %. Average?
  3. An element has isotopes at 20 u and 22 u and an average atomic mass of 20.2. Which peak is taller, and roughly by how much?
    the 20 u peak, by roughly 9:1
  4. Explain why no single atom of chlorine has a mass of 35.45 u.
    it is an average over isotopes, not a real particle mass

(a) 6.94   (b) 35.45   (c) the 20 u peak, by roughly 9:1   (d) it is an average over isotopes, not a real particle mass

(a) \((6.015)(0.0759) + (7.016)(0.9241) = \mathbf{6.94}\) u. (b) \((34.969)(0.7578) + (36.966)(0.2422) = \mathbf{35.45}\) u. (c) The 20 u peak is much taller. The average sits only 0.2 above 20 but 1.8 below 22, so the light isotope must dominate — about 90 % against 10 %, a ratio near \(9\!:\!1\). (d) Chlorine atoms are either \(\ce{^{35}Cl}\) or \(\ce{^{37}Cl}\). The tabulated 35.45 is the abundance-weighted average of those two, a property of a bulk sample rather than of any individual atom.

Ladder 4 • Percent composition

CED 1.3

Percent composition asks what fraction of a compound's mass each element contributes. Work from one mole of the compound.

I do: percent composition of a nitrate

Find the percent by mass of each element in \(\ce{Ca(NO3)2}\) (\(M = 164.1\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)).

Mass of each element in one mole: Ca \(= 40.08\);   N \(= 2(14.01) = 28.02\);   O \(= 6(16.00) = 96.00\). \[ \%\text{Ca} = \frac{40.08}{164.1}\times100 = \mathbf{24.4\%} \qquad \%\text{N} = \frac{28.02}{164.1}\times100 = \mathbf{17.1\%} \] \[ \%\text{O} = \frac{96.00}{164.1}\times100 = \mathbf{58.5\%} \] Sense check: the three must sum to 100 %. \(24.4 + 17.1 + 58.5 = 100.0\). ✓

Ladder 5 • Empirical and molecular formulas

CED 1.3

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. The molecular formula is the actual count. Percent composition gives you the first; only the molar mass gives you the second.

I do: percentages to a molecular formula

A compound is 40.00 % C, 6.71 % H and 53.29 % O by mass, with \(M = 180.2\,\mathrm{g/mol}\).

Step 1 — assume 100.0 g, so the percentages become masses in grams. Step 2 — convert each to moles: \[ \text{C: } \frac{40.00}{12.011} = 3.331 \qquad \text{H: } \frac{6.71}{1.008} = 6.657 \qquad \text{O: } \frac{53.29}{16.00} = 3.331 \] Step 3 — divide by the smallest (3.331): C 1.00, H 2.00, O 1.00, so the empirical formula is \(\ce{CH2O}\). Step 4 — scale to the molar mass. Empirical mass \(= 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 30.03\), and \(180.2/30.03 = 2\)… in fact \(\mathbf{6.00}\). \[ \ce{CH2O} \times 6 = \mathbf{\ce{C6H12O6}} \] The trap: stopping at \(\ce{CH2O}\). That ratio is shared by formaldehyde, acetic acid and glucose — the molar mass is what separates them.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. 92.26 % C, 7.74 % H, \(M = 78.11\,\mathrm{g/mol}\). Empirical and molecular formulas?
    \(\ce{CH}\), \(\ce{C6H6}\)
  2. 5.93 % H, 94.07 % O, \(M = 34.01\,\mathrm{g/mol}\). Empirical and molecular?
    \(\ce{HO}\), \(\ce{H2O2}\)
  3. 69.94 % Fe, 30.06 % O. Empirical formula?
    \(\ce{Fe2O3}\)
  4. Why can two different compounds share an empirical formula but never a molecular formula and the same structure?
    the empirical formula fixes only the ratio

(a) \(\ce{CH}\), \(\ce{C6H6}\)   (b) \(\ce{HO}\), \(\ce{H2O2}\)   (c) \(\ce{Fe2O3}\)   (d) the empirical formula fixes only the ratio

(a) C: \(92.26/12.011 = 7.681\); H: \(7.74/1.008 = 7.679\). Ratio \(1:1\), so \(\ce{CH}\) (mass 13.02). \(78.11/13.02 = 6\), giving \(\ce{C6H6}\), benzene. (b) H: \(5.93/1.008 = 5.883\); O: \(94.07/16.00 = 5.879\). Ratio \(1:1\), so \(\ce{HO}\) (mass 17.01). \(34.01/17.01 = 2\), giving \(\ce{H2O2}\). (c) Fe: \(69.94/55.85 = 1.252\); O: \(30.06/16.00 = 1.879\). Dividing by the smaller: Fe 1.00, O 1.50. Multiply both by 2 to clear the half: \(\ce{Fe2O3}\). (d) The empirical formula fixes only the ratio of atoms, so any number of compounds can share it. The molecular formula fixes the actual count — and two compounds can still share that (isomers) while differing in how the atoms are connected.

Ladder 6 • Composition of mixtures

CED 1.4

A mixture has no formula. Its composition is whatever was put in, so it is described by percent by mass — and its components keep their own properties, which is how they can be separated.

I do: separating and weighing

A 8.00 g mixture of \(\ce{KCl}\) and sand is stirred with water, filtered, and the filtrate evaporated to dryness, leaving 4.60 g of \(\ce{KCl}\). Find the percent by mass of \(\ce{KCl}\), and explain why the method works.

\[ \frac{4.60}{8.00}\times100 = \mathbf{57.5\%~KCl} \] Why it works: \(\ce{KCl}\) is ionic and dissolves as hydrated \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) ions; sand is a covalent network solid whose bonds water cannot break. The difference in solubility is the property being exploited, and filtration separates the two.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. A 12.0 g mixture yields 7.20 g of soluble salt. Percent salt?
  2. A mixture is 2.00 g \(\ce{NaCl}\) and 3.00 g \(\ce{KCl}\). What is the percent by mass of sodium in the whole mixture?
  3. Name a technique that separates two miscible liquids, and the property it exploits.
    distillation; difference in boiling point / volatility
  4. Why can a mixture's composition vary while a compound's cannot?
    mixtures are not chemically combined

(a) 60.0 %   (b) 15.7 %   (c) distillation; difference in boiling point / volatility   (d) mixtures are not chemically combined

(a) \(7.20/12.0 \times 100 = \mathbf{60.0}\%\). (b) Sodium in the \(\ce{NaCl}\): \(2.00 \times (22.99/58.44) = 0.787\,\mathrm{g}\). As a fraction of the whole mixture: \(0.787/5.00 \times 100 = \mathbf{15.7}\%\). Note the denominator is the total mixture mass, not the \(\ce{NaCl}\) mass. (c) Distillation, exploiting the difference in volatility (boiling point): the more volatile liquid vaporizes first and is condensed separately. (d) A mixture's components are only physically combined in whatever proportion was used, so the proportion can be anything. A compound's atoms are chemically bonded in a fixed whole-number ratio, so its composition is fixed by its formula.

Ladder 7 • Electron configurations

CED 1.5

Fill lowest-energy orbitals first: \(\ce{1s}\), \(\ce{2s}\), \(\ce{2p}\), \(\ce{3s}\), \(\ce{3p}\), \(\ce{4s}\), \(\ce{3d}\), \(\ce{4p}\). Capacities are \(s\) 2, \(p\) 6, \(d\) 10. The superscripts must sum to the electron count.

I do: an atom and an ion

Write the configuration of phosphorus (\(Z = 15\)) and of the sulfide ion, \(\ce{S^2-}\).

Phosphorus, 15 electrons: \[ \ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^3} \qquad (2+2+6+2+3 = 15 \text{ \checkmark}) \] Sulfide. Neutral sulfur has 16 electrons; the \(2-\) charge means it has gained two, giving 18: \[ \ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6} \qquad (=18 \text{ \checkmark}) \] The trap: writing the ion's configuration as though it were the atom. Always adjust the electron count for the charge first — negative means more electrons, positive means fewer.
YOUR TURN 7 — four questions
  1. Configuration of calcium (\(Z = 20\)):
    \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2}\)
  2. Configuration of \(\ce{Al^3+}\):
    \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6}\)
  3. Configuration of iron (\(Z = 26\)):
    \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^6}\)
  4. Explain why \(\ce{Al^3+}\) and \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) have identical configurations but different sizes.
    same electrons, different nuclear charge

(a) \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2}\)   (b) \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6}\)   (c) \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^6}\)   (d) same electrons, different nuclear charge

(a) \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2}\) (\(= 20\)). (b) Aluminium has 13 electrons; losing three leaves 10: \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6}\). (c) \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^6}\) (\(= 26\)). Note \(\ce{4s}\) fills before \(\ce{3d}\). (d) Both are isoelectronic — ten electrons in the same arrangement — so shielding is essentially identical. But aluminium has 13 protons against magnesium's 12. The greater nuclear charge pulls the same electron cloud in more tightly, so \(\ce{Al^3+}\) is the smaller ion.

Ladder 8 • Reading a photoelectron spectrum

CED 1.6

A PES spectrum has one peak per occupied subshell. Peak height gives the number of electrons in that subshell; peak position gives their binding energy. Summing the heights gives the atomic number — which identifies the element.

I do: peaks to an element

A spectrum shows five peaks of relative height \(2\), \(2\), \(6\), \(2\) and \(3\), running from highest binding energy to lowest. Identify the element and explain which peak is highest in binding energy.

Sum the heights: \(2+2+6+2+3 = 15\) electrons, so \(Z = 15\) — phosphorus, \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^3}\). Highest binding energy: the first \(\ce{1s}\) peak. Those electrons lie closest to the nucleus and are shielded by no others, so they feel the largest effective nuclear charge over the shortest distance. The Coulombic attraction is strongest, so the most energy is needed to remove one. Lowest binding energy: the \(\ce{3p}\) peak of height 3. Outermost and most shielded, so held least tightly — these are the electrons phosphorus loses or shares in chemistry.
YOUR TURN 8 — four questions
  1. Peaks of height \(2, 2, 6, 1\). Identify the element.
    sodium
  2. Peaks of height \(2, 2, 6, 2, 4\). Identify the element.
    sulfur
  3. An element's spectrum has four peaks, the last of height 2. Identify it and give its configuration.
    magnesium, \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2}\)
  4. Two elements both show a \(\ce{1s}\) peak. Explain why the peak sits at a higher binding energy for the element of larger \(Z\).
    more protons pull the \(\ce{1s}\) electrons harder

(a) sodium   (b) sulfur   (c) magnesium, \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2}\)   (d) more protons pull the \(\ce{1s}\) electrons harder

(a) \(2+2+6+1 = 11\), so \(Z = 11\): sodium. (b) \(2+2+6+2+4 = 16\): sulfur. (c) \(2+2+6+2 = 12\): magnesium, \(\ce{1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2}\). (d) The \(\ce{1s}\) electrons are essentially unshielded in both, so the attraction they feel is set almost entirely by the number of protons. More protons means a larger effective nuclear charge at the same tiny distance, hence a stronger Coulombic attraction and a higher binding energy.

Ladder 9 • Periodic trends, explained Coulombically

CED 1.7

Across a period, protons are added to the same shell: nuclear charge rises, shielding barely changes, so the atom contracts and holds its electrons more tightly. Down a group, a new shell is added: distance and shielding both rise, so the atom expands and holds its outer electrons more loosely.

I do: ranking and explaining

Rank \(\ce{Na}\), \(\ce{Mg}\) and \(\ce{K}\) by first ionization energy, and justify.

Order: \(\ce{K}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Mg}\) (419, 496, 738 kJ/mol). \(\ce{Na}\) against \(\ce{Mg}\) — same shell, but magnesium has one more proton. The extra nuclear charge holds the valence electrons more tightly, so more energy is needed: \(\ce{Mg}\) is higher. \(\ce{Na}\) against \(\ce{K}\) — potassium's valence electron is in the next shell out, further from the nucleus and shielded by an extra filled shell. The attraction is weaker, so it is easier to remove: \(\ce{K}\) is lower. What earns zero: “\(\ce{Mg}\) wants to lose two electrons” or “\(\ce{K}\) is more reactive”. Name charge, distance, shielding — nothing else counts.
YOUR TURN 9 — four questions
  1. Rank \(\ce{Li}\), \(\ce{Na}\), \(\ce{K}\) by atomic radius, smallest first.
    \(\ce{Li}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\) \(<\) \(\ce{K}\)
  2. Rank \(\ce{Na}\), \(\ce{Mg}\), \(\ce{Al}\) by atomic radius, smallest first.
    \(\ce{Al}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Mg}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\)
  3. The first ionization energy of sulfur (1000) is lower than that of phosphorus (1012), breaking the trend. Explain.
    the paired \(\ce{3p}\) electron is repelled
  4. Why is the second ionization energy of sodium enormously larger than its first?
    the second comes from a core shell

(a) \(\ce{Li}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\) \(<\) \(\ce{K}\)   (b) \(\ce{Al}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Mg}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\)   (c) the paired \(\ce{3p}\) electron is repelled   (d) the second comes from a core shell

(a) \(\ce{Li}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\) \(<\) \(\ce{K}\). Each step down adds a shell, so distance and shielding both increase. (b) \(\ce{Al}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Mg}\) \(<\) \(\ce{Na}\). Across the period the nuclear charge rises while the shell stays the same, pulling the cloud inward. (c) Phosphorus is \(\ce{3p^3}\), with one electron in each of the three \(\ce{3p}\) orbitals. Sulfur is \(\ce{3p^4}\), so one orbital holds a pair. Two electrons confined to the same orbital repel each other strongly, raising that electron's energy and making it easier to remove — enough to outweigh sulfur's extra proton. (This is an argument about electron–electron repulsion, not about “half-filled subshells being stable”.) (d) The first electron removed is sodium's single \(\ce{3s}\) valence electron, far out and well shielded. Once it is gone, \(\ce{Na+}\) has a filled \(\ce{2p}\) shell, and the next electron must come from that core shell — much closer to the nucleus and shielded by only two electrons. Far greater effective nuclear charge at a far shorter distance means an enormously larger energy.

Ladder 10 • Valence electrons and ionic compounds

CED 1.8

A main-group element's group number gives its valence-electron count, and that fixes the ion it forms. Combine ions so the charges cancel; the lattice energy that results is Coulombic.

I do: from groups to a formula and a comparison

Magnesium reacts with nitrogen. Give the ions, the formula, and predict whether the compound's lattice energy exceeds that of \(\ce{NaF}\).

Ions: Mg is group 2, two valence electrons, forms \(\ce{Mg^2+}\). N is group 15, five valence electrons, forms \(\ce{N^3-}\). Formula: the charges must cancel. Three \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) (\(+6\)) with two \(\ce{N^3-}\) (\(-6\)) gives \(\ce{Mg3N2}\). Lattice energy against \(\ce{NaF}\): compare Coulombically. The attraction is proportional to the product of the ionic charges. For \(\ce{Mg3N2}\) that product is \((2)(3) = 6\); for \(\ce{NaF}\) it is \((1)(1) = 1\). \(\ce{Mg3N2}\) therefore has much the larger lattice energy.
YOUR TURN 10 — four questions
  1. Formula of the compound of aluminium and oxygen:
    \(\ce{Al2O3}\)
  2. Formula of the compound of potassium and sulfur:
    \(\ce{K2S}\)
  3. Which has the larger lattice energy, \(\ce{MgO}\) or \(\ce{NaF}\)? Justify Coulombically.
    \(\ce{MgO}\) — charge product 4 vs 1, and shorter distance
  4. \(\ce{MgO}\) melts at 2852 °C, \(\ce{NaF}\) at 993 °C. Explain how this follows from your answer to (c).
    stronger attractions take more energy to overcome

(a) \(\ce{Al2O3}\)   (b) \(\ce{K2S}\)   (c) \(\ce{MgO}\) — charge product 4 vs 1, and shorter distance   (d) stronger attractions take more energy to overcome

(a) \(\ce{Al^3+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\): \(2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0\), so \(\ce{Al2O3}\). (b) \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{S^2-}\): \(2(+1) + (-2) = 0\), so \(\ce{K2S}\). (c) \(\ce{MgO}\), on both Coulombic counts. Charge: the product is \((+2)(-2) = 4\) against \(\ce{NaF}\)'s \((+1)(-1) = 1\). Distance: \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\) are the smaller pair, so the internuclear separation is shorter (\(\approx\)212 pm against \(\approx\)235 pm) and the attraction, going as \(1/d\), is stronger again. (d) Melting an ionic solid means giving the ions enough energy to break free of the lattice's electrostatic attractions. \(\ce{MgO}\) has much the stronger attractions, so far more energy — a far higher temperature — is required.
