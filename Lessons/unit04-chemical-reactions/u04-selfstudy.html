AP Chemistry lessons

Unit 4 • Chemical Reactions

Self-Study • CED 4.1–4.9, I do / You do

Twelve ladders • four YOUR TURN questions each • work alone, check after all four

How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a worked example, then four YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four before checking anything.

The habit that decides this unit. Every quantitative question here runs the same three-step road: to moles, through the balanced equation, back out. Grams never talk to grams directly. If you find yourself comparing two masses, or two volumes, you have skipped the middle step. One scope note. The CED excludes the terms “oxidizing agent” and “reducing agent”, and excludes memorizing solubility rules beyond a short list. You will be asked which species is oxidized and which is reduced — never to label an “agent”. Lewis acid–base theory is also excluded; Brønsted–Lowry is the model used throughout.

Ladder 1 • Balancing equations

CED 4.1

Change coefficients only. Changing a subscript changes the substance, so it is never allowed.

I do: a combustion

Balance \(\ce{C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}\).

Carbon first (it appears in one place on each side): 3 carbons left, so 3 \(\ce{CO2}\). Hydrogen next: 8 hydrogens left, so 4 \(\ce{H2O}\). Oxygen last — it is in two products, so leave it until the rest is fixed. Right side now has \(3(2) + 4(1) = 10\) oxygens, so 5 \(\ce{O2}\). \[ \ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O} \] Check every element: C 3=3, H 8=8, O 10=10. ✓ Why oxygen last: balancing the element that appears in the most places first forces you to redo it every time something else changes.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. Balance \(\ce{Fe + O2 -> Fe2O3}\):
    4, 3, 2
  2. Balance \(\ce{C2H6 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}\):
    2, 7, 4, 6
  3. Balance \(\ce{Al + HCl -> AlCl3 + H2}\):
    2, 6, 2, 3
  4. A student balances \(\ce{H2 + O2 -> H2O}\) by writing \(\ce{H2O2}\). Explain the error.
    changing a subscript changes the substance

(a) 4, 3, 2   (b) 2, 7, 4, 6   (c) 2, 6, 2, 3   (d) changing a subscript changes the substance

(a) \(\ce{4Fe + 3O2 -> 2Fe2O3}\). (b) \(\ce{2C2H6 + 7O2 -> 4CO2 + 6H2O}\). (c) \(\ce{2Al + 6HCl -> 2AlCl3 + 3H2}\). (d) Balancing may adjust only the coefficients in front of formulas. A subscript is part of the formula's identity: \(\ce{H2O2}\) is hydrogen peroxide, a different compound from water, so the equation would no longer describe the reaction that happened.

Ladder 2 • Net ionic equations

CED 4.2

Write what actually changes. Strong electrolytes in solution are already separated into ions; anything that appears unchanged on both sides is a spectator and cancels.

I do: molecular to net ionic
\(\ce{AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) -> AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq)}\). Write the net ionic equation. Split the strong electrolytes. \(\ce{AgNO3}\), \(\ce{NaCl}\) and \(\ce{NaNO3}\) are all soluble and ionic, so all are separated. \(\ce{AgCl}\) is a solid and stays written as one unit. \[ \ce{Ag+ + NO3^- + Na+ + Cl^- -> AgCl(s) + Na+ + NO3^-} \] Cancel the spectators: \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\) appear unchanged on both sides. \[ \ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) -> AgCl(s)} \] The rule for what stays together: solids, liquids, gases and weak electrolytes are written as whole units. Only dissolved strong electrolytes get split.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Net ionic for \(\ce{BaCl2(aq) + Na2SO4(aq) -> BaSO4(s) + 2NaCl(aq)}\):
    \(\ce{Ba^2+ + SO4^2- -> BaSO4(s)}\)
  2. Net ionic for \(\ce{HNO3(aq) + KOH(aq) -> KNO3(aq) + H2O(l)}\):
    \(\ce{H+ + OH^- -> H2O}\)
  3. Which species stay written as whole units in a net ionic equation?
    solids, liquids, gases, weak electrolytes
  4. Why is the net ionic equation the same for any strong acid with any strong hydroxide base?
    only \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{OH-}\) actually react

(a) \(\ce{Ba^2+ + SO4^2- -> BaSO4(s)}\)   (b) \(\ce{H+ + OH^- -> H2O}\)   (c) solids, liquids, gases, weak electrolytes   (d) only \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{OH-}\) actually react

(a) \(\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}\); \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) are spectators. (b) \(\ce{H+(aq) + OH^-(aq) -> H2O(l)}\); \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\) are spectators. (c) Solids, liquids, gases and weak electrolytes — anything not present as free ions in solution. (d) Both are fully dissociated, so the only chemical change is \(\ce{H+}\) combining with \(\ce{OH-}\) to form water. Every other ion is present and unchanged before and after, whatever its identity, so all of them cancel.

Ladder 3 • Physical versus chemical change

CED 4.4

A chemical change makes a new substance — new bonds, new identity. A physical change rearranges what is already there.

I do: the hard case

Classify: (i) ice melting, (ii) iron rusting, (iii) \(\ce{NaCl}\) dissolving.

(i) Physical. The \(\ce{H2O}\) molecules are unchanged; only the arrangement and the intermolecular forces between them change. (ii) Chemical. Iron and oxygen form iron(III) oxide — a new substance with new bonds. (iii) Physical, and this is the one that causes arguments. The ionic lattice is pulled apart and each ion is surrounded by water, so strong new ion–dipole interactions do form. But no new substance is made: the ions in solution are the same \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) that were in the crystal, and evaporating the water returns the original solid unchanged. Recoverability is the deciding test.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. Classify: sugar dissolving in tea.
    physical
  2. Classify: a candle burning.
    chemical
  3. Classify: dry ice subliming.
    physical
  4. Give one observation that suggests a chemical change has occurred, and one reason it is not conclusive.
    e.g. colour change; but dissolving a dye also changes colour

(a) physical   (b) chemical   (c) physical   (d) e.g. colour change; but dissolving a dye also changes colour

(a) Physical — the sucrose molecules are dispersed, not altered, and can be recovered by evaporation. (b) Chemical — hydrocarbons and oxygen become \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\). (c) Physical — solid \(\ce{CO2}\) becomes gaseous \(\ce{CO2}\). Same molecules, different phase. (d) A colour change, a gas evolving, a precipitate forming, or a temperature change all suggest chemical change. None is conclusive: mixing a dye into water changes colour, boiling produces bubbles, and dissolving \(\ce{NH4NO3}\) makes the beaker cold — all physical changes.

Ladder 4 • Mole-to-mole stoichiometry

CED 4.5

The coefficients of a balanced equation are a mole ratio, never a mass ratio. That ratio is the only bridge between one substance and another.

I do: the three-step road

For \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}\), how many grams of \(\ce{NH3}\) form from 28.0 g of \(\ce{N2}\), with hydrogen in excess?

To moles: \(28.0/28.02 = 0.999\) mol \(\ce{N2}\). Through the equation: the ratio \(\ce{N2}\):\(\ce{NH3}\) is \(1:2\), so \(0.999 \times 2 = 2.00\) mol \(\ce{NH3}\). Back out: \(2.00 \times 17.03 = \mathbf{34.0~g}\). The check that catches the commonest error: mass is conserved overall, but it is not transferred one-for-one between species. 28.0 g of \(\ce{N2}\) does not give 28.0 g of \(\ce{NH3}\); the extra mass comes from the hydrogen.

  1. For \(\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}\), moles of \(\ce{H2O}\) from 0.500 mol \(\ce{O2}\):
  2. For \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}\), moles of \(\ce{H2}\) needed for 0.400 mol \(\ce{N2}\):
  3. For \(\ce{4Fe + 3O2 -> 2Fe2O3}\), grams of \(\ce{Fe2O3}\) from 11.2 g \(\ce{Fe}\):
  4. Why can the coefficients not be read as a mass ratio?
    different substances have different molar masses

(a) 1.00 mol   (b) 1.20 mol   (c) 16.0 g   (d) different substances have different molar masses

(a) Ratio \(\ce{O2}\):\(\ce{H2O}\) is \(1:2\), so \(\mathbf{1.00}\) mol. (b) Ratio \(\ce{N2}\):\(\ce{H2}\) is \(1:3\), so \(0.400 \times 3 = \mathbf{1.20}\) mol. (c) \(11.2/55.85 = 0.2005\) mol Fe; ratio \(\ce{Fe}\):\(\ce{Fe2O3}\) is \(4:2\), so \(0.2005/2 = 0.1003\) mol \(\ce{Fe2O3}\); \(\times 159.7 = \mathbf{16.0}\) g. (d) Because a coefficient counts particles, and particles of different substances have different masses. Two moles of \(\ce{H2}\) (4 g) reacting with one mole of \(\ce{O2}\) (32 g) is a \(2\!:\!1\) mole ratio but a \(1\!:\!8\) mass ratio.

Ladder 5 • Limiting reactant

CED 4.5

Convert both reactants to moles, then test them against the equation's ratio. Comparing raw moles, or masses, is the standard way to get this wrong.

I do: testing against the ratio

28.0 g \(\ce{N2}\) and 4.00 g \(\ce{H2}\) react by \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}\). Which is limiting, and what mass of \(\ce{NH3}\) forms?

To moles: \(\ce{N2}\) \(= 28.0/28.02 = 0.999\); \(\ce{H2}\) \(= 4.00/2.016 = 1.98\). Test against the ratio. Consuming all the \(\ce{N2}\) would require \(3 \times 0.999 = 3.00\) mol of \(\ce{H2}\) — but only 1.98 is available. So hydrogen is limiting.

Note that hydrogen is limiting despite there being twice as many moles of it. The ratio, not the raw count, decides.

Yield from the limiting reactant only: \(n(\ce{NH3}) = \tfrac{2}{3}(1.984) = 1.32\) mol, so \(m = 1.32 \times 17.03 = \mathbf{22.5~g}\). Excess left over: \(\ce{N2}\) used \(= 1.984/3 = 0.661\) mol, so \(0.999 - 0.661 = 0.338\) mol remains \(= 9.47\,\mathrm{g}\).
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. 0.300 mol \(\ce{H2}\) with 0.200 mol \(\ce{O2}\) by \(\ce{2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O}\). Limiting?
    \(\ce{H2}\)
  2. How many moles of water form in (a)?
  3. How much of the excess reactant is left in (a)?
    0.050 mol \(\ce{O2}\)
  4. Why is it wrong to say “the reactant present in the smaller amount is limiting”?
    the stoichiometric ratio decides

(a) \(\ce{H2}\)   (b) 0.300 mol   (c) 0.050 mol \(\ce{O2}\)   (d) the stoichiometric ratio decides

(a) All the \(\ce{H2}\) would need \(0.300/2 = 0.150\) mol \(\ce{O2}\), and 0.200 is available — so oxygen is in excess and \(\ce{H2}\) is limiting. (b) Ratio \(\ce{H2}\):\(\ce{H2O}\) is \(2:2\), so \(\mathbf{0.300}\) mol. (c) \(\ce{O2}\) used \(= 0.150\) mol, so \(0.200 - 0.150 = \mathbf{0.050}\) mol remains. (d) Because the reactants are not consumed one-for-one. In the ammonia example hydrogen was limiting even though there was twice as much of it in moles, because the equation demands three times as much. Only a comparison against the coefficients answers the question.

Ladder 6 • Percent yield

CED 4.5 \[ \%\text{ yield} = \frac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}} \times 100 \] The theoretical yield always comes from the limiting reactant.
I do: yield from the previous ladder

The ammonia synthesis above has a theoretical yield of 22.5 g. A run produces 18.0 g. Find the percent yield and give two reasons it is below 100 %.

\[ \frac{18.0}{22.5}\times100 = \mathbf{80.0\%} \] Reasons: the reaction may not have gone to completion — ammonia synthesis is an equilibrium and does not consume all its reactants; product is lost during separation and purification; side reactions may consume reactants; or the product may not be fully dry when weighed. What a yield above 100 % means: not a better reaction — an error. Almost always the product was still wet, or contained impurities.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. Theoretical 5.00 g, actual 4.10 g. Percent yield?
  2. A 85.0 % yield on a theoretical 12.0 g. Actual mass?
  3. Which reactant's amount sets the theoretical yield?
    the limiting reactant
  4. A student reports 105 %. What has most likely happened?
    the product was wet or impure

(a) 82.0 %   (b) 10.2 g   (c) the limiting reactant   (d) the product was wet or impure

(a) \(4.10/5.00 \times 100 = \mathbf{82.0}\%\). (b) \(0.850 \times 12.0 = \mathbf{10.2}\) g. (c) The limiting reactant — it runs out first and so caps how much product can form. (d) An error, since a reaction cannot make more than the theoretical amount. The usual cause is that the product was weighed while still wet with solvent, or contained an impurity. Mis-identifying the limiting reactant would also do it.

Ladder 7 • Solution stoichiometry

CED 4.5

Volume and concentration give moles: \(n = MV\). From there it is the same three-step road as always.

I do: two solutions, a precipitate

30.0 mL of 0.200 M \(\ce{AgNO3}\) is mixed with 25.0 mL of 0.150 M \(\ce{NaCl}\). What mass of \(\ce{AgCl}\) forms?

To moles: \(n(\ce{Ag+}) = (0.0300)(0.200) = 0.00600\); \(n(\ce{Cl-}) = (0.0250)(0.150) = 0.00375\). Test the ratio: the net ionic equation \(\ce{Ag+ + Cl^- -> AgCl}\) is \(1:1\), so the smaller amount limits — chloride. Back out: \(n(\ce{AgCl}) = 0.00375\) mol; \(m = 0.00375 \times 143.32 = \mathbf{0.537~g}\). If asked for a leftover concentration: excess \(\ce{Ag+}\) is \(0.00600 - 0.00375 = 0.00225\) mol in the combined 55.0 mL, giving 0.0409 M. Always use the total volume.

  1. Moles in 45.0 mL of 0.250 M:
  2. Volume of 0.500 M containing 0.0300 mol:
  3. In the worked example, what is \([\ce{NO3-}]\) after mixing?
  4. Why must the combined volume be used for a leftover concentration?
    the solute is now spread through both volumes

(a) 0.0113 mol   (b) 60.0 mL   (c) 0.109 M   (d) the solute is now spread through both volumes

(a) \((0.0450)(0.250) = \mathbf{0.0113}\) mol. (b) \(V = n/M = 0.0300/0.500 = 0.0600\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{60.0}\) mL. (c) Nitrate is a spectator, so all 0.00600 mol remains, now in 55.0 mL: \(0.00600/0.0550 = \mathbf{0.109}\) M. (d) Because on mixing, the solute is dispersed through the sum of the two volumes. Dividing by only one of them would overstate the concentration.

Ladder 8 • Titration

CED 4.6

At the equivalence point the moles of acid and base have reacted in exactly the ratio the equation demands. That single sentence solves every titration question.

I do: finding an unknown concentration

25.00 mL of \(\ce{HCl}\) requires 22.40 mL of 0.150 M \(\ce{NaOH}\) to reach the endpoint. Find \([\ce{HCl}]\).

Moles of base: \((0.02240)(0.150) = 0.00336\) mol. Through the ratio: \(\ce{HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O}\) is \(1:1\), so \(n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.00336\) mol. Back out: \([\ce{HCl}] = 0.00336/0.02500 = \mathbf{0.134~M}\). Watch for a ratio that is not \(1:1\). With \(\ce{H2SO4}\), one mole of acid supplies two of \(\ce{H+}\), so it takes twice as much base — and forgetting that halves or doubles your answer.
YOUR TURN 8 — four questions
  1. 20.00 mL of \(\ce{NaOH}\) needs 18.60 mL of 0.200 M \(\ce{HCl}\). \([\ce{NaOH}]\)?
  2. How many moles of \(\ce{NaOH}\) neutralize 0.0100 mol of \(\ce{H2SO4}\)?
  3. What is true of the moles of \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{OH-}\) at the equivalence point?
    they are stoichiometrically equal
  4. A student overshoots the endpoint. Is the calculated concentration too high or too low?
    too high

(a) 0.186 M   (b) 0.0200 mol   (c) they are stoichiometrically equal   (d) too high

(a) \(n(\ce{HCl}) = (0.01860)(0.200) = 0.00372\) mol; \(1:1\), so \([\ce{NaOH}] = 0.00372/0.02000 = \mathbf{0.186}\) M. (b) \(\ce{H2SO4}\) is diprotic: \(2 \times 0.0100 = \mathbf{0.0200}\) mol. (c) They have reacted in exactly the ratio the balanced equation requires — for a monoprotic acid with a single hydroxide base, in equal amounts. Neither is in excess. (d) Too high. An overshoot records a larger titre than the true equivalence volume, so more base appears to have been needed, and the calculated acid concentration is inflated.

Ladder 9 • Recognizing reaction types

CED 4.7

Three families cover almost everything on this exam: precipitation (two solutions, an insoluble solid), acid–base (proton transfer), and redox (electron transfer, shown by changing oxidation numbers).

I do: classify and justify

Classify each: (i) \(\ce{Pb(NO3)2 + 2KI -> PbI2 + 2KNO3}\); (ii) \(\ce{HNO3 + NaOH -> NaNO3 + H2O}\); (iii) \(\ce{Zn + CuSO4 -> ZnSO4 + Cu}\).

(i) Precipitation. Two aqueous solutions meet and an insoluble solid, \(\ce{PbI2}\), forms. (ii) Acid–base. A proton moves from the acid to the hydroxide; the net ionic equation is \(\ce{H+ + OH^- -> H2O}\). (iii) Redox. Zinc goes from 0 to \(+2\) (oxidized, loses electrons); copper goes from \(+2\) to 0 (reduced, gains them). Changing oxidation numbers is the signature. The quick test for redox: if any element's oxidation number changes, it is redox. If none changes, it is not.
YOUR TURN 9 — four questions
  1. Classify \(\ce{AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3}\):
    precipitation
  2. Classify \(\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}\):
    redox
  3. Classify \(\ce{CH3COOH + NaOH -> CH3COONa + H2O}\):
    acid–base
  4. What single test distinguishes a redox reaction from the other two?
    does any oxidation number change?

(a) precipitation   (b) redox   (c) acid–base   (d) does any oxidation number change?

(a) Precipitation — insoluble \(\ce{AgCl}\) forms. (b) Redox — Mg goes 0 to \(+2\), O goes 0 to \(-2\). (c) Acid–base — a proton transfers from the weak acid to the hydroxide. (d) Whether any element's oxidation number changes. In precipitation and acid–base reactions the ions are rearranged but no element is oxidized or reduced.

Ladder 10 • Acid–base reactions

CED 4.8

Brønsted–Lowry: an acid donates a proton, a base accepts one. A conjugate pair differs by exactly one \(\ce{H+}\).

I do: pairs and strength

Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in \(\ce{NH3 + H2O <=> NH4+ + OH^-}\), and explain why \(\ce{NH3}\) is a weak base.

Pairs. \(\ce{NH3}\) accepts a proton to become \(\ce{NH4+}\): that is one pair, base and its conjugate acid. \(\ce{H2O}\) donates a proton to become \(\ce{OH-}\): that is the other, acid and its conjugate base. Weak, not strong. At equilibrium most of the ammonia is still \(\ce{NH3}\). Only a small fraction has accepted a proton, so the solution contains far fewer \(\ce{OH-}\) ions than a strong base of the same concentration would give — which is why the equation is written with a double arrow. Strength is about extent, not concentration. A concentrated solution of a weak base is entirely possible.
YOUR TURN 10 — four questions
  1. Conjugate base of \(\ce{HNO3}\):
    \(\ce{NO3-}\)
  2. Conjugate acid of \(\ce{HCO3-}\):
    \(\ce{H2CO3}\)
  3. Identify both pairs in \(\ce{HF + H2O <=> F^- + H3O+}\):
    \(\ce{HF}\)/\(\ce{F-}\) and \(\ce{H3O+}\)/\(\ce{H2O}\)
  4. Distinguish a strong acid from a concentrated acid.
    extent of ionization vs amount per litre

(a) \(\ce{NO3-}\)   (b) \(\ce{H2CO3}\)   (c) \(\ce{HF}\)/\(\ce{F-}\) and \(\ce{H3O+}\)/\(\ce{H2O}\)   (d) extent of ionization vs amount per litre

(a) \(\ce{NO3-}\) — remove one proton. (b) \(\ce{H2CO3}\) — add one proton. (c) \(\ce{HF}\) (acid) with \(\ce{F-}\) (its conjugate base); \(\ce{H2O}\) (base) with \(\ce{H3O+}\) (its conjugate acid). (d) Strength is the extent of ionization: a strong acid ionizes essentially completely. Concentration is how much acid was dissolved per litre. A dilute solution of a strong acid and a concentrated solution of a weak acid are both perfectly possible.

Ladder 11 • Oxidation numbers

CED 4.9

Rules in order of priority: free elements 0; monatomic ions equal their charge; oxygen \(-2\) (peroxides \(-1\)); hydrogen \(+1\) (metal hydrides \(-1\)); the sum equals the overall charge.

I do: three species

Find the oxidation number of the central atom in \(\ce{MnO4-}\), \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) and \(\ce{H2SO4}\).

\(\ce{MnO4-}\): four oxygens at \(-2\) give \(-8\); the total must be \(-1\). \[ x + (-8) = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+7} \] \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\): seven oxygens give \(-14\); the total is \(-2\); and there are two chromiums. \[ 2x + (-14) = -2 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x = +12 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+6} \] \(\ce{H2SO4}\): two hydrogens give \(+2\), four oxygens \(-8\), total 0. \[ 2 + x - 8 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+6} \] The slip to avoid: forgetting to divide by the number of central atoms. In dichromate the answer is \(+6\), not \(+12\).

Ladder 12 • Balancing redox in acid

CED 4.9

Balance electrons first — the number lost must equal the number gained — then use \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) to finish. Check the charge as well as the atoms.

I do: permanganate and iron

Balance \(\ce{MnO4^- + Fe^2+ + H+ -> Mn^2+ + Fe^3+ + H2O}\).

Electron bookkeeping. Mn goes \(+7 \to +2\): a gain of 5. Fe goes \(+2 \to +3\): a loss of 1. To make these equal, multiply the iron by 5. Then balance O with water and H with \(\ce{H+}\). Four oxygens on the left need 4 \(\ce{H2O}\) on the right, which needs 8 \(\ce{H+}\) on the left: \[ \ce{MnO4^- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O} \] Charge check — do this every time. Left: \((-1) + 5(+2) + 8(+1) = +17\). Right: \((+2) + 5(+3) = +17\). ✓

If the charges do not match, the equation is wrong even if every atom balances.

YOUR TURN 12 — four questions
  1. How many electrons does each Cr gain going from \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) to \(\ce{Cr^3+}\)?
    3 each, 6 total
  2. How many \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) are needed per \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\)?
    six
  3. In \(\ce{Zn + Cu^2+ -> Zn^2+ + Cu}\), which species is oxidized?
    \(\ce{Zn}\)
  4. Why is a charge check necessary even when all atoms balance?
    electrons must balance too

(a) 3 each, 6 total   (b) six   (c) \(\ce{Zn}\)   (d) electrons must balance too

(a) Cr goes \(+6 \to +3\), a gain of 3 each; with two chromiums the ion takes 6 electrons. (b) Each \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) supplies one electron, so six are needed. (c) \(\ce{Zn}\) — it goes from 0 to \(+2\), losing electrons. (d) Because balancing atoms alone does not guarantee that electrons have been accounted for. A redox equation transfers charge, and if the totals differ between the two sides, electrons have been created or destroyed somewhere — which is impossible. The charge check is the only reliable test that the electron bookkeeping was right.
