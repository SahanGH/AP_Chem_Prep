Scope — read before Part E

Two CED boundaries apply to this paper.

CED 4.7 excludes the meaning of the terms “oxidizing agent” and “reducing agent” — the AP exam asks only which species is oxidized and which is reduced. Question 14(c) asks you to name the agents anyway, because the vocabulary is standard in chemistry beyond this course. That part is flagged [beyond CED]. CED 3.8 excludes percent-by-mass for solutions. Percent composition of a solid sample — questions 12 and 18 — is Unit 1 material and is fair game. Question 19 asks for percent by mass of a solution (blood plasma), which the AP exam will not ask; it is here because the redox stoichiometry leading up to it is exactly on syllabus.

Section A • Moles, Formulas, and Concentration 5 questions •\ 25 minutes

Free-response 1 • short • 4 points

To measure the alcohol content of a wine sample, a chemist needs 1.00 L of aqueous 0.200 M \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\).

Determine the mass of solid \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) that must be weighed out.
Step 1 — moles needed from the target concentration. \(n = M \times V = 0.200\,\mathrm{mol/L} \times 1.00\,\mathrm{L} = 0.200\) mol Step 2 — molar mass of \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\). \(2(39.10) + 2(52.00) + 7(16.00) = 78.20 + 104.00 + 112.00 = 294.20\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) Step 3 — convert moles to mass. \(m = 0.200 \times 294.20 = \mathbf{58.8}\) g

Weigh 58.8 g of \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), dissolve it in less than 1 L of water, then dilute to the 1.00 L mark of a volumetric flask. Adding 1.00 L of water instead would give a final volume larger than 1.00 L and a concentration below 0.200 M.

Free-response 2 • short • 4 points

A solution is made by dissolving 75.0 mL of ethanol, \(\ce{C2H5OH}\) (density 0.79 g/cm³), in enough water to make 250.0 mL of solution.

Calculate the molarity of ethanol in the solution.
Step 1 — volume of ethanol to mass, using density as the conversion factor: \(m = 75.0\;\mathrm{cm^3} \times 0.79\,\mathrm{g/cm^3} = 59.25\,\mathrm{g}\) Step 2 — molar mass of \(\ce{C2H5OH}\). \(2(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 16.00 = 46.07\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) Step 3 — mass to moles. \(n = 59.25 / 46.07 = 1.286\) mol Step 4 — divide by the solution volume, not the ethanol volume: \(M = 1.286 / 0.2500\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{5.1}\) M

Two significant figures: the density 0.79 g/cm³ is the weakest datum and limits the whole chain.

Free-response 3 • long • 10 points

Each solution below contains only a strong electrolyte. Calculate the concentration of every ion present.

  1. 0.0200 mol of sodium phosphate in 10.0 mL of solution.
    Formula \(\ce{Na3PO4}\); it dissociates \(\ce{Na3PO4(s) -> 3Na^+(aq) + PO4^3-(aq)}\). \([\ce{Na3PO4}] = 0.0200 / 0.0100\,\mathrm{L} = 2.00\,\mathrm{M}\) \([\ce{Na+}] = 3 \times 2.00 = \mathbf{6.00}\) M   \([\ce{PO4^3-}] = \mathbf{2.00}\) M
  2. 0.300 mol of barium nitrate in 600.0 mL of solution.
    Formula \(\ce{Ba(NO3)2}\): \(\ce{Ba(NO3)2(s) -> Ba^2+(aq) + 2NO3^-(aq)}\). \([\ce{Ba(NO3)2}] = 0.300 / 0.6000\,\mathrm{L} = 0.500\,\mathrm{M}\) \([\ce{Ba^2+}] = \mathbf{0.500}\) M   \([\ce{NO3-}] = 2 \times 0.500 = \mathbf{1.00}\) M
  3. 1.00 g of potassium chloride in 0.500 L of solution.
    \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{KCl}) = 39.10 + 35.45 = 74.55\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) \(n = 1.00 / 74.55 = 0.01341\) mol \([\ce{KCl}] = 0.01341 / 0.500 = 0.0268\,\mathrm{M}\)

    One of each ion per formula unit, so \([\ce{K+}] = [\ce{Cl-}] = \mathbf{0.0268}\) M

  4. 132 g of ammonium sulfate in 1.50 L of solution.
    Formula \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\): \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4(s) -> 2NH4^+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq)}\). \(\mathcal{M} = 2(18.04) + 32.07 + 4(16.00) = 132.15\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) \(n = 132 / 132.15 = 0.999\) mol \([\ce{(NH4)2SO4}] = 0.999 / 1.50 = 0.666\,\mathrm{M}\) \([\ce{NH4+}] = 2 \times 0.666 = \mathbf{1.33}\) M   \([\ce{SO4^2-}] = \mathbf{0.666}\) M

Free-response 4 • short • 4 points

Which of these three solutions contains the largest total number of ions? Support your choice with a calculation for each.

  1. 100.0 mL of 0.100 M \(\ce{NaOH}\)
  2. 50.0 mL of 0.200 M \(\ce{BaCl2}\)
  3. 75.0 mL of 0.150 M \(\ce{Na3PO4}\)
Step 1 — moles of formula units in each.

(a) \(0.1000 \times 0.100 = 0.0100\) mol   (b) \(0.0500 \times 0.200 = 0.0100\) mol   (c) \(0.0750 \times 0.150 = 0.01125\) mol

Step 2 — ions released per formula unit. \(\ce{NaOH -> Na+ + OH-}\) gives 2;   \(\ce{BaCl2 -> Ba^2+ + 2Cl-}\) gives 3;   \(\ce{Na3PO4 -> 3Na+ + PO4^3-}\) gives 4. Step 3 — multiply.

(a) \(0.0100 \times 2 = 0.0200\) mol ions

(b) \(0.0100 \times 3 = 0.0300\) mol ions

(c) \(0.01125 \times 4 = 0.0450\) mol ions

(c), the \(\ce{Na3PO4}\) solution, contains the most ions. It wins on both counts — it has the most formula units and the most ions per formula unit.

Free-response 5 • short • 6 points

50.0 mL of 0.250 M \(\ce{CoCl2}\) is added to 25.0 mL of 0.350 M \(\ce{NiCl2}\). No reaction occurs. Assume the volumes are additive.

Calculate the concentration of each ion present after mixing.
Step 1 — recognise there is no reaction. Both salts are soluble and no precipitate, gas, or water forms. Every ion that goes in stays in solution; only dilution changes. Step 2 — moles of each cation. \(n(\ce{Co^2+}) = 0.0500 \times 0.250 = 0.0125\) mol \(n(\ce{Ni^2+}) = 0.0250 \times 0.350 = 0.00875\) mol Step 3 — moles of chloride, remembering two per formula unit of each chloride salt: \(n(\ce{Cl-}) = 2(0.0125) + 2(0.00875) = 0.0250 + 0.0175 = 0.0425\) mol Step 4 — new total volume. \(V = 50.0 + 25.0 = 75.0\,\mathrm{mL} = 0.0750\,\mathrm{L}\) Step 5 — divide each by the total volume. \([\ce{Co^2+}] = 0.0125/0.0750 = \mathbf{0.167}\) M \([\ce{Ni^2+}] = 0.00875/0.0750 = \mathbf{0.117}\) M \([\ce{Cl-}] = 0.0425/0.0750 = \mathbf{0.567}\) M Check: charge must balance. \(2(0.167) + 2(0.117) = 0.568 \approx 0.567\) M of positive charge against 0.567 M negative. ✓

Section B • Balancing and Mass Relationships 2 questions •\ 20 minutes

Free-response 6 • long • 10 points

For each reaction, write the formula equation, the complete ionic equation, and the net ionic equation. Include all phase labels.

  1. Aqueous potassium chloride is added to aqueous silver nitrate, forming a silver chloride precipitate and aqueous potassium nitrate.
    Formula equation \(\ce{KCl(aq) + AgNO3(aq) -> AgCl(s) + KNO3(aq)}\)

    Already balanced 1:1:1:1.

    Complete ionic — split every soluble strong electrolyte, leave the solid intact: \(\ce{K^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) + Ag^+(aq) + NO3^-(aq) -> AgCl(s) + K^+(aq) + NO3^-(aq)}\) Net ionic — cancel \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\), which appear unchanged on both sides: \(\ce{Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}\)
  2. Aqueous potassium hydroxide is mixed with aqueous iron(III) nitrate, forming an iron(III) hydroxide precipitate and aqueous potassium nitrate.
    Formula equation — iron(III) needs three hydroxides, so the coefficients are 3 and 3: \(\ce{3KOH(aq) + Fe(NO3)3(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s) + 3KNO3(aq)}\) Complete ionic \(\ce{3K^+(aq) + 3OH^-(aq) + Fe^3+(aq) + 3NO3^-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s) + 3K^+(aq) + 3NO3^-(aq)}\) Net ionic — \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\) are spectators: \(\ce{Fe^3+(aq) + 3OH^-(aq) -> Fe(OH)3(s)}\)

    Note the charge check: \(+3 + 3(-1) = 0\), matching the neutral solid.

Free-response 7 • short • 5 points

Calculate the mass of solid \(\ce{NaCl}\) that must be added to 1.50 L of 0.100 M \(\ce{AgNO3}\) to precipitate all of the \(\ce{Ag+}\) as \(\ce{AgCl}\).
Step 1 — net ionic equation, which gives the ratio that matters: \(\ce{Ag^+(aq) + Cl^-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}\)   — a 1:1 ratio. Step 2 — moles of \(\ce{Ag+}\) to remove. \(n = 0.100 \times 1.50 = 0.150\) mol Step 3 — moles of \(\ce{Cl-}\), hence of \(\ce{NaCl}\).

1:1, so 0.150 mol of \(\ce{Cl-}\) is needed, supplied by 0.150 mol \(\ce{NaCl}\) (one \(\ce{Cl-}\) per formula unit).

Step 4 — convert to mass. \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{NaCl}) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) \(m = 0.150 \times 58.44 = \mathbf{8.77}\) g

“All” the silver means at least this much; any excess \(\ce{NaCl}\) simply stays dissolved as spectator \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\).

Section C • Limiting Reactant and Yield 1 question •\ 15 minutes

Free-response 8 • long • 10 points

When aqueous \(\ce{Na2SO4}\) and \(\ce{Pb(NO3)2}\) are mixed, \(\ce{PbSO4}\) precipitates. 1.25 L of 0.0500 M \(\ce{Pb(NO3)2}\) is mixed with 2.00 L of 0.0250 M \(\ce{Na2SO4}\). Assume the volumes are additive.

  1. Write the net ionic equation.
    Both salts are soluble; the precipitate is \(\ce{PbSO4}\). Keep the solid intact and cancel the spectators: \(\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> PbSO4(s)}\)
  2. Identify the limiting reactant, showing your reasoning.
    Step 1 — moles of each reacting ion. \(n(\ce{Pb^2+}) = 1.25 \times 0.0500 = 0.0625\) mol \(n(\ce{SO4^2-}) = 2.00 \times 0.0250 = 0.0500\) mol Step 2 — compare against the 1:1 stoichiometry. Consuming all the \(\ce{Pb^2+}\) would need 0.0625 mol of sulfate, but only 0.0500 mol is available. Sulfate is the limiting reactant. Note that the larger volume belongs to the limiting reagent — volume alone decides nothing, only moles do.
  3. Calculate the mass of \(\ce{PbSO4}\) formed.
    Step 1 — moles of product from the limiting reactant, 1:1: \(n(\ce{PbSO4}) = 0.0500\) mol Step 2 — molar mass. \(207.2 + 32.07 + 4(16.00) = 303.3\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) Step 3 — mass. \(m = 0.0500 \times 303.3 = \mathbf{15.2}\) g
  4. Calculate the concentration of the excess reactant ion remaining in solution.
    Step 1 — leftover moles. \(n(\ce{Pb^2+})_{\text{excess}} = 0.0625 - 0.0500 = 0.0125\) mol Step 2 — total volume. \(V = 1.25 + 2.00 = 3.25\,\mathrm{L}\) Step 3 — divide. \([\ce{Pb^2+}] = 0.0125 / 3.25 = \mathbf{0.00385}\) M
  5. Identify the spectator ions and explain what makes them spectators.
    \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\).

    Both are dissolved and unchanged before and after the reaction — same species, same charge, same aqueous phase — so they take no part in the chemical change and cancel from the complete ionic equation.

Section D • Acid–Base Reactions and Titration 5 questions •\ 35 minutes

Free-response 9 • short • 4 points

What volume of 0.100 M \(\ce{HCl}\) is needed to neutralise exactly 25.0 mL of 0.350 M \(\ce{NaOH}\)?
Step 1 — net ionic equation. \(\ce{H^+(aq) + OH^-(aq) -> H2O(l)}\)   — 1:1. Step 2 — moles of \(\ce{OH-}\). \(n = 0.0250 \times 0.350 = 0.00875\) mol Step 3 — moles of \(\ce{H+}\) required — the same, 0.00875 mol. Step 4 — back-solve the volume. \(V = n/M = 0.00875 / 0.100 = 0.0875\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{87.5}\) mL

The answer is larger than 25.0 mL because the acid is the more dilute solution — it takes more of it to deliver the same number of moles.

Free-response 10 • short • 6 points

28.0 mL of 0.250 M \(\ce{HNO3}\) is mixed with 53.0 mL of 0.320 M \(\ce{KOH}\).

Determine which ion is in excess after the reaction goes to completion, and calculate its concentration.
Step 1 — moles of each. \(n(\ce{H+}) = 0.0280 \times 0.250 = 0.00700\) mol \(n(\ce{OH-}) = 0.0530 \times 0.320 = 0.01696\) mol Step 2 — react them 1:1. \(\ce{OH-}\) is in excess, so all the \(\ce{H+}\) is consumed: \(n(\ce{OH-})_{\text{excess}} = 0.01696 - 0.00700 = 0.00996\) mol Step 3 — total volume. \(V = 28.0 + 53.0 = 81.0\,\mathrm{mL} = 0.0810\,\mathrm{L}\) Step 4 — concentration of the surviving ion. \([\ce{OH-}] = 0.00996 / 0.0810 = \mathbf{0.123}\) M

The solution is basic. \([\ce{H+}]\) is not zero, but it is set by the autoionisation of water, not by the leftover acid — that is a Unit 8 calculation.

Free-response 11 • long • 10 points

A student standardises a sodium hydroxide solution. They weigh out 1.3009 g of potassium hydrogen phthalate (\(\ce{KHC8H4O4}\), “KHP”, \(\mathcal{M} = 204.22\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)), which has one acidic hydrogen. The KHP is dissolved in distilled water, phenolphthalein is added, and the solution is titrated with the \(\ce{NaOH}\). The burette readings show that 41.20 mL of \(\ce{NaOH}\) is required to reach the endpoint.

  1. Calculate the concentration of the sodium hydroxide solution.
    Step 1 — balanced reaction. One acidic hydrogen means 1:1: \(\ce{KHC8H4O4(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> KNaC8H4O4(aq) + H2O(l)}\) Step 2 — moles of KHP, the primary standard whose mass we know exactly: \(n = 1.3009 / 204.22 = 6.3701e-3\) mol Step 3 — moles of \(\ce{NaOH}\) at the equivalence point — equal, by the 1:1 ratio: \(6.3701e-3\) mol. Step 4 — divide by the delivered volume. \(M = 6.3701e-3 / 0.04120\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{0.1546}\) M

    Four significant figures are justified — the mass, the titre, and the molar mass all carry at least four.

  2. Explain why KHP is weighed as a solid rather than the \(\ce{NaOH}\) being weighed directly.
    Solid \(\ce{NaOH}\) is hygroscopic — it absorbs water and \(\ce{CO2}\) from the air — so a weighed mass of it is not a known mass of \(\ce{NaOH}\).

    KHP is a stable, non-hygroscopic solid of high purity and high molar mass, so its weighed mass gives moles reliably. That is exactly what makes it a primary standard.

  3. The student rinses the burette with distilled water and does not rinse it with the \(\ce{NaOH}\) solution before filling. State the effect on the calculated concentration and justify it.
    Residual water dilutes the \(\ce{NaOH}\) in the burette. Each millilitre delivered then contains fewer moles of \(\ce{OH-}\), so a larger volume is needed to reach the endpoint.

    In \(M = n / V\), the numerator \(n\) is fixed by the KHP mass while \(V\) is too large, so the calculated concentration is too low.

Free-response 12 • long • 8 points

An environmental chemist analyses effluent known to contain carbon tetrachloride (\(\ce{CCl4}\)) and benzoic acid (\(\ce{HC7H5O2}\)), a weak acid with one acidic hydrogen per molecule. A 0.3518 g sample is shaken with water; the resulting aqueous solution requires 10.59 mL of 0.1546 M \(\ce{NaOH}\) for neutralisation.

  1. Calculate the mass percent of \(\ce{HC7H5O2}\) in the original sample.
    Step 1 — what actually reacts. \(\ce{CCl4}\) is non-polar and has no acidic hydrogen; it neither dissolves appreciably nor reacts with \(\ce{NaOH}\). Only the benzoic acid is titrated. \(\ce{HC7H5O2(aq) + NaOH(aq) -> NaC7H5O2(aq) + H2O(l)}\)   — 1:1. Step 2 — moles of \(\ce{NaOH}\) delivered. \(n = 0.01059 \times 0.1546 = 1.6372e-3\) mol Step 3 — moles of benzoic acid — the same, 1.6372e-3 mol. Step 4 — mass of benzoic acid. \(\mathcal{M}(\ce{HC7H5O2}) = 7(12.01) + 6(1.008) + 2(16.00) = 122.12\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) \(m = 1.6372e-3 \times 122.12 = 0.19993\,\mathrm{g}\) Step 5 — percent of the whole sample. \(\dfrac{0.19993}{0.3518} \times 100 = \mathbf{56.83\%}\)

    The remaining 43.17 % is the \(\ce{CCl4}\).

  2. Benzoic acid is a weak acid. Explain why the titration still reaches a sharp, well-defined equivalence point.
    Strength governs how far the acid ionises at equilibrium, not how many moles of \(\ce{OH-}\) it can eventually neutralise. \(\ce{OH-}\) removes \(\ce{H+}\) from solution, which pulls the ionisation of the weak acid forward until every molecule has been deprotonated. The stoichiometry at the equivalence point is therefore still exactly 1:1.

Section E • Oxidation–Reduction 6 questions • 45 minutes

Free-response 14 • long • 10 points

Producing a metal from its ore always involves oxidation–reduction. In the metallurgy of galena (\(\ce{PbS}\)), the principal lead ore, the first step is roasting:

\(\ce{2PbS(s) + 3O2(g) -> 2PbO(s) + 2SO2(g)}\)

The oxide is then treated with carbon monoxide to give the free metal:

\(\ce{PbO(s) + CO(g) -> Pb(s) + CO2(g)}\)
  1. For the roasting reaction, assign oxidation numbers to every element on both sides, and identify the atoms oxidised and reduced.
    Step 1 — assign, using the rules in order. \(\ce{PbS}\): sulfide is \(-2\), so \(\ce{Pb}\) is \(\mathbf{+2}\), S is \(\mathbf{-2}\). \(\ce{O2}\): free element, O is \(\mathbf{0}\). \(\ce{PbO}\): O is \(-2\), so \(\ce{Pb}\) is \(\mathbf{+2}\). \(\ce{SO2}\): O is \(-2\) each (\(-4\) total), so S is \(\mathbf{+4}\). Step 2 — find what changed.

    S: \(-2 \to +4\), a loss of 6 electrons — sulfur is oxidised.

    O: \(0 \to -2\), a gain of 2 electrons each — oxygen is reduced.

    Pb stays \(+2\) throughout and is not involved in the electron transfer, despite lead being the point of the whole process.

    Step 3 — electron check. 2 S atoms \(\times\) 6 e\(^-\) lost \(= 12\) e\(^-\); 6 O atoms \(\times\) 2 e\(^-\) gained \(= 12\) e\(^-\). Balanced. ✓
  2. For the carbon monoxide reaction, identify the atoms oxidised and reduced.
    \(\ce{PbO}\): \(\ce{Pb}\) \(+2\), O \(-2\).   \(\ce{CO}\): O is \(-2\), so C is \(+2\). \(\ce{Pb}\): free element, \(0\).   \(\ce{CO2}\): O \(-2\) each, so C is \(+4\).

    Pb: \(+2 \to 0\), gains 2 electrons — lead is reduced.

    C: \(+2 \to +4\), loses 2 electrons — carbon is oxidised.

    Oxygen is \(-2\) on both sides and takes no part.

    Electron check: 2 lost by C, 2 gained by Pb. ✓

  3. [beyond CED] Name the oxidising agent and the reducing agent in each reaction.
    The agent is the whole substance that contains the atom which changed, and it is named for what it does to its partner. Roasting: \(\ce{O2}\) is the oxidising agent (it is reduced); \(\ce{PbS}\) is the reducing agent (it is oxidised). Reduction step: \(\ce{PbO}\) is the oxidising agent (it is reduced); \(\ce{CO}\) is the reducing agent (it is oxidised).

    The trap: the oxidising agent is the species that is reduced. The AP exam will not test this vocabulary — it asks only which species is oxidised and which is reduced.

Free-response 15 • long • 8 points

For each equation, state whether it is an oxidation–reduction reaction. If it is, identify the species oxidised and the species reduced. If it is not, justify that verdict.

  1. \(\ce{CH4(g) + H2O(g) -> CO(g) + 3H2(g)}\)
    Redox. C: \(-4 \to +2\), oxidised. H: \(+1 \to 0\) in \(\ce{H2}\), reduced. All six hydrogens are reduced — the four from \(\ce{CH4}\) and the two from \(\ce{H2O}\) — because the only hydrogen-bearing product is \(\ce{H2}\). That is exactly what the electron count demands: carbon loses 6 e\(^-\), so 6 H must each gain one.
  2. \(\ce{2AgNO3(aq) + Cu(s) -> Cu(NO3)2(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\)
    Redox. Cu: \(0 \to +2\), oxidised. Ag: \(+1 \to 0\), reduced. \(\ce{NO3-}\) is a spectator, unchanged at \(-1\) overall. This is a single-replacement reaction, which is always redox.
  3. \(\ce{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g)}\)
    Redox. Zn: \(0 \to +2\), oxidised. H: \(+1 \to 0\), reduced.

    Chlorine stays \(-1\) throughout.

  4. \(\ce{2H^+(aq) + 2CrO4^2-(aq) -> Cr2O7^2-(aq) + H2O(l)}\)
    Not redox. Check chromium in both: \(\ce{CrO4^2-}\): \(x + 4(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{+6}\) \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\): \(2x + 7(-2) = -2 \Rightarrow 2x = +12 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{+6}\)

    Chromium is \(+6\) on both sides; H stays \(+1\) and O stays \(-2\). No oxidation number changes, so no electrons are transferred. This is a condensation (equilibrium) reaction, not a redox one.

Free-response 16 • long • 10 points

Potassium dichromate is a bright orange solid, reduced here to \(\ce{Cr^3+}\). In water alone that ion is blue-violet, as \(\ce{[Cr(H2O)6]^3+}\); in the sulfate and chloride media used below, those ligands displace water and the solution looks green instead. Under acidic conditions dichromate reacts with ethanol:

\(\ce{H^+(aq) + Cr2O7^2-(aq) + C2H5OH(aq) -> Cr^3+(aq) + CO2(g) + H2O(l)}\)   (unbalanced)
Balance this equation using the half-reaction method, showing both half reactions.

Step 2 — reduction half-reaction. Balance Cr, then O with \(\ce{H2O}\), then H with \(\ce{H+}\), then charge with electrons: \(\ce{Cr2O7^2- + 14H^+ + 6e^- -> 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\) Step 3 — oxidation half-reaction, the same way: \(\ce{C2H5OH + 3H2O -> 2CO2 + 12H^+ + 12e^-}\) Step 4 — equalise the electrons. Adding gives \(28\,\ce{H+}\) on the left against \(12\) on the right (net 16 left), and \(14\,\ce{H2O}\) on the right against 3 on the left (net 11 right):

  4. A 25.00 mL sample of 0.0100 M \(\ce{KIO3}\) is reacted with excess \(\ce{KI}\). Titrating the \(\ce{I3-}\) produced takes 32.04 mL of \(\ce{Na2S2O3}\). Calculate its molarity.
    Step 1 — moles of iodate. \(n = 0.02500 \times 0.0100 = 2.500e-4\) mol Step 2 — moles of \(\ce{I3-}\), 3 per iodate from part (a): \(n = 3(2.500e-4) = 7.500e-4\) mol Step 3 — moles of thiosulfate, 2 per \(\ce{I3-}\) from part (c): \(n = 2(7.500e-4) = 1.500e-3\) mol Step 4 — divide by the titre. \(M = 1.500e-3/0.03204\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{0.0468}\) M

    Note the chain: one iodate becomes three triiodides becomes six thiosulfates. The overall ratio is 1:6.

  5. Describe how you would prepare 500.0 mL of the \(\ce{KIO3}\) solution in part (d) from the solid.
    \(n = 0.5000 \times 0.0100 = 5.00e-3\) mol \(m = 5.00e-3 \times 214.00 = 1.07\,\mathrm{g}\)

    Weigh 1.07 g of \(\ce{KIO3}\) on an analytical balance, transfer it quantitatively into a 500.0 mL volumetric flask, rinsing the weighing vessel into the flask. Add distilled water to dissolve, swirl, then fill to the calibration mark and invert to mix.

    A beaker or graduated cylinder will not do — neither is accurate enough to define 500.0 mL to four significant figures.

Free-response 29 • long • 12 points

Chromium has been investigated as a coating for steel cans. The film thickness is found by dissolving a sample of the can in acid and oxidising the resulting \(\ce{Cr^3+}\) to \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) with peroxydisulfate:

\(\ce{S2O8^2-(aq) + Cr^3+(aq) + H2O(l) -> Cr2O7^2-(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) + H^+(aq)}\)   (unbalanced)

Unreacted \(\ce{S2O8^2-}\) is then removed. An excess of ferrous ammonium sulfate is added next, reducing the \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\); the salt is \(\ce{Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2 * 6H2O}\). The \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) left over is titrated with a separate \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) solution:

\(\ce{H^+(aq) + Fe^2+(aq) + Cr2O7^2-(aq) -> Fe^3+(aq) + Cr^3+(aq) + H2O(l)}\)   (unbalanced)
  1. Balance both equations.
    First reaction. Cr: \(+3 \to +6\), loses 3 e\(^-\) each, so 6 e\(^-\) per \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) formed.

    Peroxydisulfate is \(\ce{O3S-O-O-SO3^2-}\): it carries a peroxide linkage, so six oxygens are \(-2\) and two are \(-1\). Sulfur is therefore \(+6\) — the same as in \(\ce{SO4^2-}\), and the group maximum. It is the two peroxide oxygens that are reduced, \(-1 \to -2\), so each \(\ce{S2O8^2-}\) gains 2 e\(^-\). LCM 6: 3 \(\ce{S2O8^2-}\) per 2 \(\ce{Cr^3+}\).

    \(\ce{3S2O8^2- + 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O -> Cr2O7^2- + 6SO4^2- + 14H^+}\)

    O: \(24 + 7 = 31\) vs \(7 + 24 = 31\). Charge: \(-6 + 6 = 0\) vs \(-2 - 12 + 14 = 0\). ✓

    Second reaction. \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) gains 6 e\(^-\); \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) loses 1. Ratio 1:6.
    \(\ce{14H^+ + 6Fe^2+ + Cr2O7^2- -> 6Fe^3+ + 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\)

    Charge: \(+14 + 12 - 2 = +24\) vs \(+18 + 6 = +24\). ✓

  2. A 40.0 cm² sample of chromium-plated can was treated this way. After the excess \(\ce{S2O8^2-}\) was removed, 3.000 g of the ferrous salt above (\(\mathcal{M} = 392.17\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)) was added, and 8.58 mL of 0.0520 M \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) was needed to react with the excess \(\ce{Fe^2+}\). The density of chromium is 7.19 g/cm³. Calculate the thickness of the chromium film.
    This is a back-titration: find the total, find the leftover, subtract. Step 1 — total \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) added. \(n = 3.000/392.17 = 7.650e-3\) mol Step 2 — excess \(\ce{Fe^2+}\), from the titration at 6:1: \(n(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}) = 0.00858 \times 0.0520 = 4.462e-4\) mol \(n(\ce{Fe^2+})_{\text{excess}} = 6(4.462e-4) = 2.677e-3\) mol Step 3 — \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) that reacted with the sample's dichromate. \(7.650e-3 - 2.677e-3 = 4.973e-3\) mol Step 4 — dichromate from the sample, again 6:1: \(n(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}) = 4.973e-3/6 = 8.288e-4\) mol Step 5 — chromium on the can, 2 Cr per dichromate: \(n(\ce{Cr}) = 2(8.288e-4) = 1.658e-3\) mol \(m = 1.658e-3 \times 52.00 = 0.08619\,\mathrm{g}\) Step 6 — volume, then thickness. \(V = 0.08619/7.19 = 0.011988\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) \(t = V/A = 0.011988/40.0 = \mathbf{3.00 \times 10^{-4}}\) cm

    That is 3.00 µm — about a twentieth the width of a human hair.
