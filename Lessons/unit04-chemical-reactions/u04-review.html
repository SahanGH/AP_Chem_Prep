The five tools

Change classifierphysical \(=\) same substances, IMFs only; chemical \(=\) new substances, bonds made/broken. Dissolving a salt is physical; dissolving \(\ce{HCl(g)}\) is chemical (it ionizes)
Particulate rulesconserve atoms AND charge; soluble salts drawn as separated ions; precipitate drawn as a cluster; excess reactant stays in the picture
Net ionic recipesplit strong electrolytes only (soluble salts, strong acids, strong bases); keep solids, liquids, gases, weak electrolytes whole; cancel spectators
Reaction typesprecipitation (insoluble product), acid–base (proton transfer), redox (oxidation states change)
Solution stoichvolume \(\times\) molarity \(\to\) moles \(\to\) ratio \(\to\) moles \(\to\) mass or volume. Check limiting reactant using the coefficients, not raw moles

Solubility, the working set

  • Always soluble: \(\ce{NO3-}\), alkali metal cations, \(\ce{NH4+}\). Check these first — they settle most questions.
  • Usually soluble: \(\ce{Cl-}\)/\(\ce{Br-}\)/\(\ce{I-}\) except with \(\ce{Ag+}\), \(\ce{Pb^2+}\); \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) except with \(\ce{Ba^2+}\), \(\ce{Pb^2+}\), \(\ce{Ca^2+}\).
  • Usually insoluble: \(\ce{CO3^2-}\), \(\ce{PO4^3-}\), \(\ce{S^2-}\), \(\ce{OH-}\) — unless the cation is on the always-soluble list.

Redox in four lines

  • Assign states: element 0; O is \(-2\); H is \(+1\); sum to the charge.
  • OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.
  • Oxidized \(\to\) reducing agent. Reduced \(\to\) oxidizing agent. (The labels name what it does to the other species.)
  • Free elements on both sides is a reliable redox tell.

The traps, one line each

  • Splitting a weak acid, a weak base, or water in a net ionic equation.
  • Drawing soluble salts as bonded pairs, or the precipitate as free ions.
  • Losing atoms between “before” and “after” boxes, or deleting the excess reactant.
  • Naming the oxidizing agent when you mean the reducing agent.
  • Using a 1:1 titration ratio for a diprotic acid.
  • Dividing excess-reactant moles by one solution's volume instead of the total volume.
  • Treating a colour change alone as proof of reaction.
