Ladder 1 • Precipitation stoichiometry

ZUM §4.7

The spine gains one new entrance ramp: \[ \text{volume} \times M \to \text{moles} \xrightarrow{\text{mole ratio}} \text{moles} \to \text{grams} \] When two solution amounts are given, it is a limiting-reactant problem — same as Chapter 3.

I do: lead iodide, the whole problem

50.0 mL of 0.200 M \(\ce{Pb(NO3)2}\) is mixed with 75.0 mL of 0.100 M \(\ce{NaI}\). Find the mass of precipitate and the concentration of \(\ce{Pb^2+}\) left in solution.

Net ionic first (rule 2 exception): \(\ce{Pb^2+ + 2I- -> PbI2(s)}\), \(M_{\ce{PbI2}} = 461.0\,\mathrm{g/mol}\) Moles of each reactant: \[ \ce{Pb^2+}: 0.0500 \times 0.200 = 0.0100\,\mathrm{mol} \qquad \ce{I-}: 0.0750 \times 0.100 = 0.00750\,\mathrm{mol} \] Limiting? The lead present would need \(2 \times 0.0100 = 0.0200\,\mathrm{mol}\) of iodide; only 0.00750 is there, so \(\ce{I-}\) is limiting. Precipitate (from the limiting reactant only): \[ 0.00750 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.00375\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{PbI2} \;\Rightarrow\; 0.00375 \times 461.0 = \mathbf{1.73~g} \] Leftover \(\ce{Pb^2+}\) — and here is the solution-specific step: the total volume is now \(50.0 + 75.0 = 125.0\,\mathrm{mL}\): \[ n = 0.0100 - 0.00375 = 0.00625\,\mathrm{mol} \qquad [\ce{Pb^2+}] = \frac{0.00625}{0.1250} = \mathbf{0.0500~M} \] Dividing by the combined volume, not the original 50.0 mL, is the step exams test.

Ladder 2 • Neutralization stoichiometry

ZUM §4.8

Strong acid meets strong base and the net ionic equation is always the same: \(\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}\). The only bookkeeping trap is a formula that delivers more than one \(\ce{H+}\) or \(\ce{OH-}\).

I do: how much base kills the acid

What volume of 0.150 M \(\ce{NaOH}\) neutralizes 35.0 mL of 0.0900 M \(\ce{H2SO4}\)?

Moles of acid, then of \(\ce{H+}\) — sulfuric acid is diprotic: \[ 0.0350 \times 0.0900 = 0.00315\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{H2SO4} \;\Rightarrow\; 2 \times 0.00315 = 0.00630\,\mathrm{mol}~\ce{H+} \] \(\ce{OH-}\) required (1:1 with \(\ce{H+}\)): 0.00630 mol Volume of base: \[ V = \frac{0.00630}{0.150} = 0.0420\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{42.0~mL} \]

The factor of 2 is the whole exam question. Skip it and every downstream number is exactly half right — which is to say, wrong.

Ladder 3 • Titration

ZUM §4.8

A titration is a neutralization run backward: the known solution (the titrant) is added until the indicator changes, and the measured volume reveals the unknown. Vocabulary that gets tested: equivalence point (moles of \(\ce{H+}\) \(=\) moles of \(\ce{OH-}\), set by stoichiometry) versus endpoint (where the indicator changes — chosen to coincide).

I do: standardizing a sodium hydroxide solution
\(\ce{NaOH}\) solutions cannot be made to a precise concentration by weighing (the pellets absorb water), so they are standardized against a weighable acid, potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP, \(M = 204.22\,\mathrm{g/mol}\), one acidic H). A 0.6745 g KHP sample requires 27.85 mL of the \(\ce{NaOH}\) solution. Find the base's molarity. Moles of acid, from the balance: \[ \frac{0.6745}{204.22} = 3.303e-3\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{KHP} \] At the endpoint, moles of \(\ce{NaOH}\) delivered \(=\) moles of KHP (1:1, one acidic H): \[ M_{\ce{NaOH}} = \frac{3.303\times10^{-3}}{0.02785} = \mathbf{0.1186~M} \]

The mass was weighed to four figures and the volume read to four, so the answer honestly carries four.

(d) A late reading overstates the base volume, overstating the moles of acid — reported molarity is too high.

Ladder 4 • Oxidation states; spotting redox

ZUM §4.9

Oxidation states are electron bookkeeping. The rules, in priority order: element 0; monatomic ion \(=\) its charge; F \(-1\); O \(-2\) (except peroxides); H \(+1\) with nonmetals, \(-1\) with metals; everything must sum to the species' overall charge. Then:

OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons). The substance containing the oxidized atom is the reducing agent, and vice versa.
I do: assigning states, then judging a reaction
(a) Chromium in \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\): K is \(+1\) (\(\times 2 = +2\)), O is \(-2\) (\(\times 7 = -14\)); the compound is neutral, so \(2x + 2 - 14 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{+6}\). (b) Sulfur in \(\ce{SO3^2-}\): O contributes \(-6\); the ion carries \(2-\), so \(x - 6 = -2 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{+4}\). (c) Is \(\ce{Zn(s) + CuSO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + Cu(s)}\) redox? Zn: \(0 \to +2\) — lost electrons, oxidized, so zinc metal is the reducing agent. Cu: \(+2 \to 0\) — gained electrons, reduced, so \(\ce{CuSO4}\) (the \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) in it) is the oxidizing agent. S and O never change (\(+6\), \(-2\)): sulfate is along for the ride.

Ladder 5 • Balancing redox; a redox titration

ZUM §4.10–4.11

Balancing by oxidation states: find the per-atom electron loss and gain, choose coefficients so total loss \(=\) total gain, then finish the non-redox atoms by inspection.

I do: copper dissolving in dilute nitric acid
\[ \ce{Cu(s) + HNO3(aq) -> Cu(NO3)2(aq) + NO(g) + H2O(l)} \qquad\text{(unbalanced)} \] Find the changes: Cu \(0 \to +2\): loses 2. N \(+5 \to +2\) (in the NO only): gains 3. Equalize electrons: \(3 \times \text{Cu}\) loses 6; \(2 \times \text{N}\) gains 6: \[ \ce{3Cu + HNO3 -> 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO + H2O} \] Finish by inspection. The right side now holds \(6 + 2 = 8\) N, so 8 \(\ce{HNO3}\); 8 H makes 4 \(\ce{H2O}\): \[ \boxed{\;\ce{3Cu + 8HNO3 -> 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO + 4H2O}\;} \] Count everything: Cu \(3=3\); N \(8 = 6+2\); H \(8 = 8\); O \(24 = 18 + 2 + 4\). Note only 2 of the 8 nitrogens changed state — the other 6 became spectator nitrate.

    the first drop of permanganate that stays purple
