Ladder 1 • Water, and what dissolves in it

ZUM §4.1–4.2

Water is a polar molecule — a bent frame with the oxygen end partially negative and the hydrogen ends partially positive. Dissolving an ionic solid is an electrical event: each ion is pried away and surrounded by water molecules facing it charge-to-charge (hydration).

What a dissolved substance does to conductivity sorts everything into three bins:

BinWho is in itIn solution
strong electrolytesoluble salts; strong acids and basesions only — ionizes \(\approx 100\%\)
weak electrolyteweak acids and basesmostly intact molecules, few ions
nonelectrolytemolecular substances (sugars, alcohols)molecules only; no ions
I do: sorting three solutes

Classify \(\ce{NaNO3}\), \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) (acetic acid), and sucrose (\(\ce{C12H22O11}\)) — and say what is actually floating in each solution.

\(\ce{NaNO3}\) — a soluble salt, so a strong electrolyte. The solution contains \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\) ions and essentially no intact \(\ce{NaNO3}\) units at all. \(\ce{HC2H3O2}\) — an acid, but not one of the six strong ones, so a weak electrolyte. About 99% of the molecules stay intact; roughly 1% ionize to \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{C2H3O2-}\). It conducts, but dimly. Sucrose — molecular, neither acid nor base, so a nonelectrolyte. It dissolves beautifully (water hydrates its polar \(\ce{-OH}\) groups) yet the solution carries no current — dissolving and ionizing are different events.

    weak — mostly \(\ce{HF}\) molecules
  3. methanol, \(\ce{CH3OH}\)
    non — molecules only

(a) strong — \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{SO4^2-}\)   (b) weak — mostly \(\ce{HF}\) molecules   (c) non — molecules only   (d) weak — mostly \(\ce{NH3}\) molecules

(a) Soluble salt \(\to\) strong; two \(\ce{K+}\) and one \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) per formula unit, no intact \(\ce{K2SO4}\). (b) Acid but not one of the six strong ones \(\to\) weak; mostly intact \(\ce{HF}\), a little \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{F-}\). (c) An alcohol — molecular, not an acid or base \(\to\) nonelectrolyte; hydrated \(\ce{CH3OH}\) molecules only. (d) Weak base \(\to\) weak electrolyte; mostly \(\ce{NH3}\) molecules with a little \(\ce{NH4+}\) and \(\ce{OH-}\).

Ladder 2 • Molarity, and the ions it implies

ZUM §4.3 \[ M = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{litres of solution}} \]

For a strong electrolyte the label concentration is only the start — the ion concentrations follow from the subscripts.

I do: a calcium nitrate solution, down to the ions

8.20 g of \(\ce{Ca(NO3)2}\) (\(M = 164.10\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)) is dissolved in enough water to make 250.0 mL of solution. Find the molarity, then every ion concentration.

Moles first: \(\dfrac{8.20}{164.10} = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}\) Molarity: \(\dfrac{0.0500}{0.2500} = 0.200\,\mathrm{M}~\ce{Ca(NO3)2}\) Ions, by subscript: each formula unit releases one \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and two \(\ce{NO3-}\): \[ [\ce{Ca^2+}] = 0.200\,\mathrm{M} \qquad [\ce{NO3-}] = 2 \times 0.200 = 0.400\,\mathrm{M} \] There is no “\(\ce{Ca(NO3)2}\) concentration” in the beaker at all — a strong electrolyte exists in solution only as its ions.

Ladder 3 • Dilution

ZUM §4.3

Adding water changes the volume, not the amount of solute. “Moles before \(=\) moles after” is the entire theory: \[ M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2 \]

I do: bench acid from the stock bottle

How would you prepare 500.0 mL of 1.00 M acetic acid from the 17.4 M glacial stock?

Moles needed: \(0.5000 \times 1.00 = 0.500\,\mathrm{mol}\) Stock volume carrying that many moles: \[ V_1 = \frac{M_2 V_2}{M_1} = \frac{(1.00)(500.0)}{17.4} = 28.7\,\mathrm{mL} \] The procedure matters: measure 28.7 mL of stock, then add water to a total volume of 500.0 mL in a volumetric flask — not “add 500.0 mL of water,” which would overshoot the volume and undershoot the concentration.

  3. To what final volume must 10.0 mL of 2.50 M \(\ce{HNO3}\) be diluted to give 0.125 M?
  4. In every dilution, which quantity is unchanged, and why does that make \(M_1V_1 = M_2V_2\) true?
    moles of solute

(a) 6.25 mL   (b) 0.240 M   (c) 200. mL   (d) moles of solute

(a) \(V_1 = (0.150)(250.0)/6.00 = \mathbf{6.25}\) mL. (b) \(M_2 = (4.00)(15.0)/250.0 = \mathbf{0.240}\) M. (c) \(V_2 = (2.50)(10.0)/0.125 = \mathbf{200.}\) mL. (d) The moles of solute — water adds volume but no solute, and since \(n = MV\) on both sides of the event, \(M_1V_1 = M_2V_2\) is just “\(n\) before \(=\) \(n\) after.”

Ladder 4 • Predicting precipitates

ZUM §4.4–4.5

Mix two salt solutions and the possible new pairings are found by swapping partners; whether either pairing actually forms a solid is decided by the solubility rules:

  1. Group 1A (\(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{K+}\), …) and \(\ce{NH4+}\) salts: soluble. Nitrates: soluble. No exceptions worth memorizing.
  2. Chlorides, bromides, iodides: soluble except with \(\ce{Ag+}\), \(\ce{Pb^2+}\), \(\ce{Hg2^2+}\).
  3. Sulfates: soluble except with \(\ce{Ba^2+}\), \(\ce{Pb^2+}\), \(\ce{Ca^2+}\), \(\ce{Sr^2+}\).
  4. Hydroxides: insoluble except group 1A (and \(\ce{Ba^2+}\), marginally).
  5. Carbonates, phosphates, chromates, sulfides: insoluble except group 1A and \(\ce{NH4+}\).
I do: potassium carbonate meets copper(II) sulfate

Will a precipitate form when \(\ce{K2CO3(aq)}\) and \(\ce{CuSO4(aq)}\) are mixed?

Ions in the pot: \(\ce{K+}\), \(\ce{CO3^2-}\), \(\ce{Cu^2+}\), \(\ce{SO4^2-}\). Swap partners — the two candidates: \(\ce{K2SO4}\) and \(\ce{CuCO3}\). Consult the rules: \(\ce{K2SO4}\) — group 1A salt, soluble; stays dissolved. \(\ce{CuCO3}\) — a carbonate, and copper is not group 1A or ammonium: insoluble. A pale blue-green solid forms. \[ \ce{K2CO3(aq) + CuSO4(aq) -> CuCO3(s) + K2SO4(aq)} \]

The phases are the answer — writing \(\ce{(s)}\) on the right compound is the whole point of the exercise.

Precipitate or no reaction? Give the formula and phase of any solid.
  1. \(\ce{NaBr(aq) + Pb(NO3)2(aq)}\)
    \(\ce{PbBr2(s)}\)
  2. \(\ce{NH4Cl(aq) + K2SO4(aq)}\)
    no reaction
  3. \(\ce{Ni(NO3)2(aq) + NaOH(aq)}\)
    \(\ce{Ni(OH)2(s)}\)
  4. \(\ce{Na3PO4(aq) + CaCl2(aq)}\)
    \(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2(s)}\)

(a) \(\ce{PbBr2(s)}\)   (b) no reaction   (c) \(\ce{Ni(OH)2(s)}\)   (d) \(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2(s)}\)

(a) Bromide meets \(\ce{Pb^2+}\) — one of the three exceptions: \(\ce{PbBr2(s)}\). (b) Candidates \(\ce{NH4+}\)/ \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) and \(\ce{K+}\)/\(\ce{Cl-}\) are all soluble combinations: no reaction — all four ions stay in solution. (c) Hydroxide with a non-1A metal: \(\ce{Ni(OH)2(s)}\). (d) Phosphate with calcium: \(\ce{Ca3(PO4)2(s)}\) — note the charge-balanced formula, \(3(2+)\) against \(2(3-)\).

Ladder 5 • Molecular, complete ionic, net ionic

ZUM §4.6

One reaction, three languages: the molecular equation keeps formulas whole; the complete ionic shows every strong electrolyte as its ions; the net ionic deletes the spectator ions — the ones appearing identically on both sides — and keeps only the chemistry.

I do: the carbonate precipitation, three ways

For the mixture from Ladder 4:

Molecular: \[ \ce{K2CO3(aq) + CuSO4(aq) -> CuCO3(s) + K2SO4(aq)} \] Complete ionic — every \(\ce{(aq)}\) strong electrolyte splits; the solid does not: \[ \ce{2K+ + CO3^2- + Cu^2+ + SO4^2- -> CuCO3(s) + 2K+ + SO4^2-} \] Cross out what never changed (\(\ce{2K+}\), \(\ce{SO4^2-}\)): \[ \boxed{\;\ce{Cu^2+(aq) + CO3^2-(aq) -> CuCO3(s)}\;} \]

The net ionic is the reusable fact: any soluble copper(II) salt plus any soluble carbonate gives this same equation. Charge check: \(2+ + 2- = 0\) on both sides.

YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. Net ionic equation for \(\ce{NaBr(aq) + Pb(NO3)2(aq)}\):
    \(\ce{Pb^2+ + 2Br- -> PbBr2(s)}\)
  2. Net ionic equation for \(\ce{Ni(NO3)2(aq) + NaOH(aq)}\):
    \(\ce{Ni^2+ + 2OH- -> Ni(OH)2(s)}\)
  3. List the spectator ions in question (a):
    \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\)
  4. Why does \(\ce{K+}\) never appear in any net ionic equation in this chapter?
    every potassium salt is soluble, so \(\ce{K+}\) is always a spectator

(a) \(\ce{Pb^2+ + 2Br- -> PbBr2(s)}\)   (b) \(\ce{Ni^2+ + 2OH- -> Ni(OH)2(s)}\)   (c) \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\)   (d) every potassium salt is soluble, so \(\ce{K+}\) is always a spectator

(a) \(\ce{Pb^2+(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> PbBr2(s)}\) — the 2 is required for charge balance. (b) \(\ce{Ni^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq) -> Ni(OH)2(s)}\). (c) \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{NO3-}\) appear unchanged on both sides. (d) Group 1A salts are soluble without exception, so \(\ce{K+}\) always enters and leaves as a free aqueous ion — a permanent spectator.
