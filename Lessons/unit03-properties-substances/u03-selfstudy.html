AP Chemistry lessons

Unit 3 • Properties of Substances and Mixtures

Self-Study • CED 3.1–3.13, I do / You do

Fourteen ladders • four YOUR TURN questions each • the heaviest unit on the exam

How to use these notes — read this first

Each skill is a ladder: a worked example, then four YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four before checking anything.

This unit is worth 18 %–22 % of the AP exam, roughly one question in five, and it is assembled from seven different places in the textbook. That is why it is the longest self-study in the course. Do not try to do all fourteen ladders in one sitting. Two habits that decide this unit. Temperature in any gas calculation is always kelvin. And when you explain why one substance boils higher, melts higher or dissolves better than another, name the intermolecular force and say why it is stronger — never just assert that it is. One scope note. Phase diagrams and colligative properties (freezing-point depression, boiling-point elevation, osmotic pressure) are excluded by the CED, and molality and percent-by-mass calculations with them. Concentration here means molarity.

Ladder 1 • Identifying intermolecular forces

CED 3.1

Three forces, in increasing strength. London dispersion acts in every substance. Dipole–dipole adds for polar molecules. Hydrogen bonding requires H bonded directly to N, O or F. A fourth, ion–dipole, appears when an ion is dissolved.

I do: naming the strongest force present

Identify the strongest intermolecular force in \(\ce{CH4}\), \(\ce{CH2Cl2}\), \(\ce{NH3}\) and a solution of \(\ce{NaCl}\) in water.

\(\ce{CH4}\) — nonpolar, symmetric. Only London dispersion. \(\ce{CH2Cl2}\) — polar (the shape does not cancel the \(\ce{C-Cl}\) dipoles), but its hydrogens are on carbon. Dipole–dipole. \(\ce{NH3}\) — hydrogen bonded directly to nitrogen. Hydrogen bonding. \(\ce{NaCl}\)(aq) — ions surrounded by polar water molecules. Ion–dipole, the strongest of the set. The trap: calling any molecule containing hydrogen a hydrogen-bonder. The hydrogen must be attached to N, O or F — carbon does not count, however many hydrogens there are.
YOUR TURN 1 — four questions
  1. Strongest force in \(\ce{CO2}\):
    London dispersion
  2. Strongest force in \(\ce{CH3OH}\):
    hydrogen bonding
  3. Strongest force in \(\ce{HCl}\):
    dipole–dipole
  4. \(\ce{CH3F}\) contains fluorine but cannot hydrogen bond. Explain.
    its H atoms are on carbon, not on F

(a) London dispersion   (b) hydrogen bonding   (c) dipole–dipole   (d) its H atoms are on carbon, not on F

(a) London dispersion — \(\ce{CO2}\) is linear and nonpolar, so its bond dipoles cancel. (b) Hydrogen bonding — the \(\ce{-OH}\) group puts a hydrogen directly on oxygen. (c) Dipole–dipole — polar, but the hydrogen is on chlorine's partner, not on N, O or F. (d) Hydrogen bonding requires a hydrogen bonded directly to N, O or F. In \(\ce{CH3F}\) the fluorine is bonded to carbon, and all three hydrogens are on carbon too. There is no \(\ce{H-F}\) bond, so no hydrogen bond can form — the molecule manages only dipole–dipole attraction.

Ladder 2 • Ranking boiling points

CED 3.1 CED 3.3

Boiling means separating molecules completely, so the boiling point tracks the strength of the intermolecular forces. Compare force type first; only if the types match does size (electron count) decide.

I do: two comparisons, two different reasons

Rank \(\ce{Ar}\) (\(-186\)), \(\ce{HCl}\) (\(-85\)) and \(\ce{H2O}\) (\(+100\) °C), then explain why \(\ce{Br2}\) boils higher than \(\ce{Cl2}\).

The three-way ranking is by force type. \(\ce{Ar}\) has dispersion only; \(\ce{HCl}\) adds dipole–dipole; \(\ce{H2O}\) hydrogen bonds. Stronger forces need more energy to overcome, so the order follows directly. \(\ce{Br2}\) against \(\ce{Cl2}\) is a different comparison. Both are nonpolar with dispersion only, so type cannot decide. \(\ce{Br2}\) has far more electrons, so its electron cloud is more polarizable — it distorts more easily, giving larger instantaneous dipoles and stronger dispersion forces. Hence the higher boiling point. The method: ask is the force type the same? If not, type decides. If so, count electrons.
YOUR TURN 2 — four questions
  1. Which boils higher, \(\ce{CH4}\) or \(\ce{C4H10}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{C4H10}\) — more electrons
  2. Which boils higher, \(\ce{H2S}\) or \(\ce{H2O}\)? Why?
    \(\ce{H2O}\) — hydrogen bonding
  3. Which boils higher, \(\ce{CH3OCH3}\) or \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\)? Both are \(\ce{C2H6O}\).
    ethanol — its H is on O
  4. \(\ce{HF}\) has stronger hydrogen bonds than \(\ce{H2O}\), yet water boils higher. Explain.
    water forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule

(a) \(\ce{C4H10}\) — more electrons   (b) \(\ce{H2O}\) — hydrogen bonding   (c) ethanol — its H is on O   (d) water forms more hydrogen bonds per molecule

(a) \(\ce{C4H10}\). Both are nonpolar with dispersion only, so electron count decides; butane has far more electrons and a more polarizable cloud. (b) \(\ce{H2O}\). Sulfur is too large and too weakly electronegative for \(\ce{H2S}\) to hydrogen bond, so it manages only dipole–dipole; water hydrogen bonds. Force type beats the fact that \(\ce{H2S}\) has more electrons. (c) Ethanol. Same formula and the same number of electrons, so dispersion is comparable — but ethanol's hydrogen sits on the oxygen and can hydrogen bond, while both of the ether's hydrogens are on carbon. The whole difference is hydrogen bonding. (d) Because the number of hydrogen bonds matters more here than the strength of any one. Water has two hydrogens and two lone pairs, so it can form up to four hydrogen bonds per molecule; \(\ce{HF}\) has one hydrogen and three lone pairs, so it is limited to about two.

Ladder 3 • Classifying solids

CED 3.2

Four types, each with properties that follow from what is holding the particles together.

I do: classify and predict

Classify \(\ce{SiO2}\), \(\ce{NaCl}\), \(\ce{Cu}\) and solid \(\ce{CO2}\), and give one property of each that follows.

\(\ce{SiO2}\) — covalent network. A continuous lattice of strong covalent bonds. Melting means breaking covalent bonds, so the melting point is very high and it is hard and non-conducting. \(\ce{NaCl}\) — ionic. Strong electrostatic attractions between ions: high-melting and brittle, conducting only when molten or dissolved. \(\ce{Cu}\) — metallic. Cations in a delocalized electron sea: conducts as a solid, and is malleable. Solid \(\ce{CO2}\) — molecular. Only weak dispersion forces between whole \(\ce{CO2}\) molecules. Very low melting point — it sublimes at \(-78\) °C — and does not conduct. The distinction that matters most: in a molecular solid the covalent bonds within each molecule are strong, but melting only has to overcome the weak forces between molecules. That is why dry ice sublimes so easily despite containing strong \(\ce{C=O}\) bonds.
YOUR TURN 3 — four questions
  1. Classify diamond and give its expected melting point behaviour.
    covalent network, very high
  2. Classify solid \(\ce{I2}\):
    molecular
  3. A solid is hard, high-melting, and conducts only when molten. Which type?
    ionic
  4. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not, though both are carbon.
    graphite has delocalized electrons; diamond's are all in sigma bonds

(a) covalent network, very high   (b) molecular   (c) ionic   (d) graphite has delocalized electrons; diamond's are all in sigma bonds

(a) Covalent network. Melting requires breaking a continuous lattice of strong \(\ce{C-C}\) covalent bonds, so the melting point is extremely high (above 3500 °C). (b) Molecular — discrete \(\ce{I2}\) molecules held by dispersion forces only, so it is soft and low-melting. (c) Ionic. Hardness and a high melting point indicate strong attractions; conducting only when molten indicates charged particles that are fixed in the solid and mobile in the melt. (d) In diamond every carbon forms four single bonds, so all four valence electrons are localized in sigma bonds and none is free to move. In graphite each carbon bonds to only three others, leaving one electron per atom delocalized over the layer. Those mobile electrons carry current along the sheets.

Ladder 4 • Phase changes and heating curves

CED 3.3

On a heating curve, sloped sections are temperature rising (kinetic energy increasing) and flat sections are phase changes (potential energy increasing while temperature holds constant).

I do: reading the flat sections

A heating curve for water runs from ice below 0 °C to steam above 100 °C. Explain the two flat regions, and why the second is longer.

The flats are phase changes. At 0 °C the ice melts; at 100 °C the water boils. Throughout each, energy is being supplied but the temperature does not move. Why the temperature holds: the energy goes into overcoming intermolecular attractions — raising potential energy — not into speeding the molecules up. Temperature measures average kinetic energy, which is unchanged. Why the boiling flat is longer: melting only loosens the lattice — molecules leave fixed positions but stay in contact, and most hydrogen bonds survive. Vaporizing must separate them completely, overcoming essentially all the attractions. For water that is 2260 J/g against 334 J/g, nearly seven times as much.
YOUR TURN 4 — four questions
  1. On a heating curve, what is happening on a sloped section?
    temperature rising, kinetic energy increasing
  2. Why does temperature stay constant during melting?
    energy goes to potential, not kinetic
  3. Which requires more energy per gram for water, melting or vaporizing, and why?
    vaporizing — separates molecules completely
  4. Steam at 100 °C causes worse burns than water at 100 °C. Explain.
    it releases its heat of condensation as well

(a) temperature rising, kinetic energy increasing   (b) energy goes to potential, not kinetic   (c) vaporizing — separates molecules completely   (d) it releases its heat of condensation as well

(a) The substance is in a single phase and its temperature is rising: the energy supplied increases the average kinetic energy of the particles. (b) The energy supplied is used to overcome the intermolecular attractions holding the lattice, raising the potential energy. Since temperature reflects average kinetic energy, and that is not changing, the temperature holds steady. (c) Vaporizing, by a factor of nearly seven (2260  against 334 J/g). Melting only loosens the structure; vaporizing separates the molecules completely. (d) Steam must first condense on the skin, releasing its full heat of vaporization — about 2260 J/g — before it even begins to cool. Liquid water at the same temperature delivers only the energy from cooling. The condensation step is the extra damage.

Ladder 5 • The ideal gas law

CED 3.4 \(PV = nRT\), with \(R = 0.08206\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm/mol/K}\). That choice of \(R\) fixes every other unit: pressure in atm, volume in litres, temperature in kelvin.
I do: solving for volume

What volume does 0.250 mol of gas occupy at 2.00 atm and 300 K?

\[ V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{(0.250)(0.08206)(300)}{2.00} = \mathbf{3.08~L} \] Sense check: one mole at 1 atm and 273 K is 22.4 L. A quarter of a mole at double the pressure should be roughly \(22.4/4/2 \approx 2.8\) L, and a slightly higher temperature nudges it up. 3.08 is right. The error that costs whole questions: leaving the temperature in °C. At 27 °C you must use 300 K, and using 27 gives an answer wrong by a factor of eleven.
YOUR TURN 5 — four questions
  1. 1.50 mol in 5.00 L at 350 K. Pressure?
  2. 2.00 atm, 4.00 L, 0.300 mol. Temperature?
  3. 0.500 g of helium in 2.00 L at 298 K. Pressure?
  4. A rigid container is heated from 300 K to 600 K. What happens to the pressure, and why?
    doubles — \(P \propto T\) at fixed \(n\), \(V\)

(a) 8.62 atm   (b) 325 K   (c) 1.53 atm   (d) doubles — \(P \propto T\) at fixed \(n\), \(V\)

(a) \(P = nRT/V = (1.50)(0.08206)(350)/5.00 = \mathbf{8.62}\) atm. (b) \(T = PV/nR = (2.00)(4.00)/[(0.300)(0.08206)] = \mathbf{325}\) K. (c) \(n = 0.500/4.003 = 0.1249\) mol; \(P = (0.1249)(0.08206)(298)/2.00 = \mathbf{1.53}\) atm. (d) The pressure doubles. With \(n\) and \(V\) fixed, \(P \propto T\) in kelvin, and the temperature doubled. In kinetic terms the molecules move faster and strike the walls both more often and harder.

Ladder 6 • Gas stoichiometry

CED 3.4

Moles are the bridge. Convert to moles, use the balanced equation, then convert the moles of gas to a volume with \(PV = nRT\). Use 22.4 L/mol only at STP.

I do: solid to gas volume

5.00 g of \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) (\(M = 84.01\,\mathrm{g/mol}\)) decomposes, releasing \(\ce{CO2}\). What volume does the gas occupy at 1.00 atm and 298 K?

Moles of solid: \(5.00/84.01 = 0.0595\) mol. Through the equation: each \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) releases one \(\ce{CO2}\), so \(n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.0595\) mol. To a volume: \[ V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{(0.05952)(0.08206)(298)}{1.00} = \mathbf{1.46~L} \] Why not just use 22.4? Because 22.4 L/mol holds only at STP (273 K, 1 atm). At 298 K the molar volume is 24.5 L, and using 22.4 would give 1.33 L — about 9 % low.
YOUR TURN 6 — four questions
  1. What volume would that same \(\ce{CO2}\) occupy at STP?
  2. 0.200 mol of \(\ce{O2}\) at 2.00 atm and 273 K: volume?
  3. Why is 22.4 L/mol wrong at 298 K?
    molar volume depends on \(T\) and \(P\)
  4. Equal volumes of \(\ce{H2}\) and \(\ce{O2}\) at the same \(T\) and \(P\) contain equal what?
    equal moles (equal numbers of molecules)

(a) 1.33 L   (b) 2.24 L   (c) molar volume depends on \(T\) and \(P\)   (d) equal moles (equal numbers of molecules)

(a) At STP the molar volume is 22.4 L/mol: \(0.05952 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{1.33}\) L. (b) \(V = nRT/P = (0.200)(0.08206)(273)/2.00 = \mathbf{2.24}\) L. (c) Because molar volume is \(RT/P\), which changes with both. At 298 K and 1 atm it is 24.5 L/mol, not 22.4. (d) Equal numbers of moles, hence equal numbers of molecules — Avogadro's principle. Note they do not contain equal masses, since the molar masses differ.

Ladder 7 • Partial pressures

CED 3.4

Each gas in a mixture exerts the pressure it would exert alone. The total is their sum, and each one's share is set by its mole fraction — not by its molar mass.

I do: mole fractions to partial pressures

A vessel holds 0.400 mol \(\ce{N2}\), 0.200 mol \(\ce{O2}\) and 0.400 mol \(\ce{He}\) at a total pressure of 3.00 atm. Find each partial pressure.

Total moles: \(0.400 + 0.200 + 0.400 = 1.000\). \[ P_{\ce{N2}} = \frac{0.400}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{1.20~atm} \qquad P_{\ce{O2}} = \frac{0.200}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{0.60~atm} \] \[ P_{\ce{He}} = \frac{0.400}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{1.20~atm} \] Check: \(1.20 + 0.60 + 1.20 = 3.00\). ✓ Note what did not matter: helium is by far the lightest gas here and nitrogen is seven times heavier, yet they exert identical partial pressures because they are present in identical amounts. Pressure counts particles, not mass.
YOUR TURN 7 — four questions
  1. A mixture is 0.600 mol \(\ce{Ar}\) and 0.400 mol \(\ce{Ne}\) at 5.00 atm. Partial pressure of \(\ce{Ar}\)?
  2. Gas collected over water at 25 °C reads 760 torr total. If water's vapour pressure is 23.8 torr, what is the dry-gas pressure?
  3. Adding helium to a rigid vessel: what happens to the partial pressure of the gas already there?
    unchanged
  4. Why does a heavier gas not exert a larger partial pressure at equal moles?
    pressure depends on particle count, not mass

(a) 3.00 atm   (b) 736 torr   (c) unchanged   (d) pressure depends on particle count, not mass

(a) \(x(\ce{Ar}) = 0.600/1.000 = 0.600\); \(P = 0.600 \times 5.00 = \mathbf{3.00}\) atm. (b) \(760 - 23.8 = \mathbf{736}\) torr. Gas collected over water is saturated with water vapour, which contributes its own partial pressure and must be subtracted. (c) Unchanged. Its partial pressure depends only on how many moles of it are present and the volume and temperature — none of which changed. Only the total pressure rises. (d) Because pressure arises from the momentum delivered by collisions with the walls, and at a given temperature all gases have the same average kinetic energy. A heavier molecule moves more slowly in exactly the compensating proportion, so equal numbers of particles exert equal pressure.

Ladder 8 • Kinetic molecular theory and effusion

CED 3.5

At a given temperature all gases have the same average kinetic energy. They do not have the same average speed — because \(\text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\), the lighter molecule must move faster.

I do: energy, speed, and rate

Helium and argon are at the same temperature. Compare their kinetic energies and speeds, and find the ratio of their effusion rates.

Kinetic energy: equal. It depends only on absolute temperature. Speed: helium is faster. With \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\) the same for both, the smaller mass must carry the larger speed. Effusion rate goes as the inverse square root of molar mass: \[ \frac{\text{rate}_{\ce{He}}}{\text{rate}_{\ce{Ar}}} = \sqrt{\frac{39.95}{4.003}} = \sqrt{9.98} = \mathbf{3.16} \] Helium effuses about three times faster. Watch the direction of the ratio. The lighter gas is faster, so the heavier mass goes on top. Getting it upside down gives 0.32 — a classic sign-of-the-error answer.

  2. Ratio for \(\ce{CH4}\) (16.04) to \(\ce{SO2}\) (64.07):
  3. Two gases at the same temperature: which has the greater average kinetic energy?
    equal
  4. Describe how the speed distribution of \(\ce{He}\) differs from that of \(\ce{Ar}\) at the same temperature.
    helium's peak is at higher speed, broader and lower

(a) 3.98   (b) 2.00   (c) equal   (d) helium's peak is at higher speed, broader and lower

(a) \(\sqrt{32.00/2.016} = \mathbf{3.98}\). (b) \(\sqrt{64.07/16.04} = \sqrt{3.995} = \mathbf{2.00}\). (c) Equal — average kinetic energy depends only on absolute temperature, not on identity. Both enclose the same total area, since both represent the same number of molecules.

Ladder 9 • Deviation from ideal behaviour

CED 3.6

The ideal model assumes molecules have no volume and no attractions. Both assumptions fail — and they fail in opposite directions.

I do: two regimes, two causes

Explain why a real gas shows a lower pressure than predicted at moderately high pressure, but a higher pressure at very high pressure.

Moderately high pressure — attractions dominate. Molecules are close enough to attract one another. One heading for the wall is pulled back by those behind it and strikes with less force, so the measured pressure falls below the ideal prediction. Very high pressure — molecular volume dominates. Now the molecules are packed so tightly that the space they themselves occupy is no longer negligible. The free volume is less than \(V\), so the gas is more compressed than the law assumes and the pressure runs above the prediction. Where the model works best: high temperature (kinetic energy swamps the attractions) and low pressure (molecules far apart, own volume negligible).
YOUR TURN 9 — four questions
  1. State the two conditions for most nearly ideal behaviour.
    high \(T\), low \(P\)
  2. Which behaves more ideally at the same \(T\) and \(P\), \(\ce{He}\) or \(\ce{H2O}\) vapour? Why?
    \(\ce{He}\) — no hydrogen bonding
  3. Which assumption fails when a gas liquefies on cooling?
    the no-attractions assumption
  4. Why does raising the temperature reduce the deviation caused by attractions?
    kinetic energy overwhelms the attraction

(a) high \(T\), low \(P\)   (b) \(\ce{He}\) — no hydrogen bonding   (c) the no-attractions assumption   (d) kinetic energy overwhelms the attraction

(a) High temperature and low pressure. (b) Helium. It is a tiny, nonpolar atom with only very weak dispersion forces, so the no-attraction assumption nearly holds. Water vapour hydrogen bonds strongly, so it deviates far more. (c) The assumption of no intermolecular attractions. Liquefaction happens precisely because attractions exist and become able to hold molecules together once they slow down. (d) At higher temperature the molecules have far more kinetic energy relative to the strength of the intermolecular attractions. A passing attraction barely alters a fast molecule's path, so the gas behaves as though no forces acted.

Ladder 10 • Molarity and dilution

CED 3.7

Molarity is moles of solute per litre of solution, not of solvent. On dilution the moles of solute are unchanged — only the volume grows.

I do: a preparation and a dilution

(i) 0.0250 mol of solute is made up to 500.0 mL. Find the molarity. (ii) Then 50.0 mL of 2.00 M stock is diluted to 500.0 mL.

(i) \(M = n/V = 0.0250/0.500 = \mathbf{0.0500~M}\). (ii) Moles of solute do not change, so \(M_1V_1 = M_2V_2\): \[ M_2 = \frac{(2.00)(50.0)}{500.0} = \mathbf{0.200~M} \] Why “of solution” matters: to make the first solution you dissolve the solid in less than 500 mL and then make up to the mark. Adding solid to 500 mL of water gives a final volume above 500 mL and a concentration below target.
YOUR TURN 10 — four questions
  1. 11.7 g \(\ce{NaCl}\) (\(M = 58.44\)) made up to 250.0 mL. Molarity?
  2. Moles of solute in 40.0 mL of 0.500 M:
  3. Dilute 25.0 mL of 6.00 M to 0.500 M. Final volume?
  4. After dilution, has the number of moles of solute changed? Explain.
    no — only the volume changed

(a) 0.801 M   (b) 0.0200 mol   (c) 300 mL   (d) no — only the volume changed

(a) \(n = 11.7/58.44 = 0.2002\) mol; \(M = 0.2002/0.2500 = \mathbf{0.801}\) M. (b) \(n = MV = (0.500)(0.0400) = \mathbf{0.0200}\) mol. (c) \(V_2 = M_1V_1/M_2 = (6.00)(25.0)/0.500 = \mathbf{300}\) mL. (d) No. Adding solvent changes the volume, not the amount of solute, so \(n\) is constant and only the concentration falls. That conservation is exactly what makes \(M_1V_1 = M_2V_2\) valid.

Ladder 11 • Solubility and “like dissolves like”

CED 3.10

A solute dissolves when the solute–solvent attractions formed are comparable to the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions broken.

I do: two solutes, one solvent

Explain why \(\ce{NaCl}\) dissolves readily in water but not in hexane, and why \(\ce{I2}\) does the reverse.

\(\ce{NaCl}\) in water: water is polar and surrounds each ion with strong ion–dipole attractions — oxygen ends toward \(\ce{Na+}\), hydrogen ends toward \(\ce{Cl-}\). The energy released repays the cost of pulling the lattice apart. \(\ce{NaCl}\) in hexane: hexane is nonpolar and offers only weak dispersion forces, which cannot compensate for \(\ce{NaCl}\)'s very large lattice energy. It does not dissolve. \(\ce{I2}\) reversed: iodine is nonpolar. In hexane, dispersion forces between solute and solvent are comparable to those being replaced, so it dissolves. In water, dissolving would mean disrupting water's hydrogen-bond network with nothing comparable offered in return — so it barely dissolves.
YOUR TURN 11 — four questions
  1. Will \(\ce{CH3OH}\) dissolve in water? Why?
    yes — hydrogen bonds with water
  2. Will \(\ce{CCl4}\) dissolve in water? Why?
    no — nonpolar
  3. Which is more soluble in water, \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) or \(\ce{CH3(CH2)6CH3}\)?
    ethanol
  4. Describe how water molecules orient around a dissolved \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) ion.
    oxygen ends inward, toward the cation

(a) yes — hydrogen bonds with water   (b) no — nonpolar   (c) ethanol   (d) oxygen ends inward, toward the cation

(a) Yes. Its \(\ce{-OH}\) group hydrogen bonds with water, so the new solute–solvent attractions are comparable to the water–water ones they replace. (b) No. \(\ce{CCl4}\) is nonpolar and symmetric; it offers only dispersion forces, which cannot compensate for disrupting water's hydrogen-bond network. (c) Ethanol. Both have a hydrocarbon portion, but ethanol's is short and its \(\ce{-OH}\) dominates. Octane has no polar group at all. (d) The water molecules turn their oxygen ends inward, toward the cation. Oxygen carries the partial negative charge, so it is attracted to the positive \(\ce{Ca^2+}\); the attraction is ion–dipole, and it is especially strong here because the ion carries a \(2+\) charge.

Ladder 12 • Particulate pictures and separation

CED 3.8 CED 3.9

A particulate diagram of a solution must show what is actually in the beaker: dissociated ions for a soluble salt, intact molecules for a molecular solute, and only partial ionization for a weak acid.

I do: drawing three solutions correctly

Describe correct particulate drawings of aqueous \(\ce{K2SO4}\), \(\ce{C6H12O6}\) and \(\ce{HF}\).

\(\ce{K2SO4}\) — a soluble ionic compound, so it dissociates completely. Draw two \(\ce{K+}\) for every one \(\ce{SO4^2-}\), all separated and hydrated. The sulfate stays intact as one unit: the \(\ce{S-O}\) bonds are covalent and do not break. \(\ce{C6H12O6}\) — molecular and soluble. Draw whole, intact glucose molecules dispersed among the water. No ions at all. \(\ce{HF}\) — a weak acid. Draw mostly intact \(\ce{HF}\) molecules with only a few \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{F-}\) ions, reflecting partial ionization. Separation follows the same logic: filtration separates an undissolved solid from a solution; distillation separates liquids by volatility; chromatography separates by differing affinity for a stationary phase.

    three — one \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and two \(\ce{Cl-}\)
  2. Should a drawing of aqueous \(\ce{HCl}\) show intact \(\ce{HCl}\) molecules? Why?
    no — it is a strong acid, fully ionized
  3. Which technique separates sand from salt water?
    filtration
  4. Which technique separates ethanol from water, and on what property?
    distillation, by volatility

(a) three — one \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and two \(\ce{Cl-}\)   (b) no — it is a strong acid, fully ionized   (c) filtration   (d) distillation, by volatility

(a) Three: one \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and two \(\ce{Cl-}\). (b) No. \(\ce{HCl}\) is a strong acid and ionizes essentially completely, so the drawing should show separated \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) and essentially no intact \(\ce{HCl}\). (d) Distillation, exploiting the difference in volatility: ethanol boils at 78 °C and water at 100 °C, so the ethanol-rich vapour comes off first.

Ladder 13 • Photons and the spectrum

CED 3.11 CED 3.12 \(E = h\nu = hc/\lambda\). Energy is inversely proportional to wavelength: short wavelength means high energy.
I do: wavelength to energy per mole

Find the energy of one photon of 400 nm light, and the energy per mole.

\[ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^{8})}{400\times10^{-9}} = \mathbf{4.97\times10^{-19}~J} \] Per mole: \(4.97\times10^{-19} \times 6.022\times10^{23} = 2.99e5\) J \(= \mathbf{299~kJ/mol}\). The unit trap: the wavelength must be in metres. Leaving it in nanometres makes the answer wrong by a factor of \(10^{9}\). Sense check: 400 nm is violet, the high-energy end of the visible range. Red light at 700 nm should come out lower — and it does, at 2.84e-19 J.

Ladder 14 • The Beer–Lambert law

CED 3.13 \(A = \varepsilon b c\): absorbance is proportional to concentration at fixed path length. That proportionality is what makes it an analytical tool — measure \(A\), get \(c\).
I do: absorbance to concentration

A solution has \(A = 0.620\) in a 1.00 cm cell, with \(\varepsilon = 2.00e3\,\mathrm{}\) /M/cm. Find the concentration.

\[ c = \frac{A}{\varepsilon b} = \frac{0.620}{(2.00\times10^{3})(1.00)} = \mathbf{3.10\times10^{-4}~M} \] Why a calibration curve is used in practice: you prepare standards of known concentration, measure each one's absorbance, and plot \(A\) against \(c\). The result is a straight line through the origin, and an unknown's concentration is read off it. That avoids needing \(\varepsilon\) at all. Why absorbance and not transmittance: absorbance is the quantity that is linear in concentration. Transmittance falls exponentially, which is far harder to read.
YOUR TURN 14 — four questions
  1. Same solution diluted to half. New absorbance?
  2. \(c = 1.5e-4\,\mathrm{M}\), \(\varepsilon = 2.00e3\,\mathrm{}\), \(b = 1.00\,\mathrm{cm}\). Find \(A\).
  3. Why must the wavelength be chosen at the absorption maximum?
    greatest sensitivity
  4. A student's cell is scratched and scatters light. What happens to the measured absorbance and the calculated concentration?
    both come out too high

(a) 0.310   (b) 0.300   (c) greatest sensitivity   (d) both come out too high

(a) 0.310. \(A \propto c\) at fixed path length, so halving the concentration halves the absorbance. (b) \(A = (2.00\times10^{3})(1.00)(1.5\times10^{-4}) = \mathbf{0.300}\). (c) At the absorption maximum, \(\varepsilon\) is largest, so a given change in concentration produces the largest change in absorbance — the greatest sensitivity. It is also the flattest part of the spectrum, so a small error in wavelength setting matters least. (d) Scattered light does not reach the detector, so the instrument reads it as though it had been absorbed: the measured absorbance is too high, and the concentration calculated from it is correspondingly too high.
