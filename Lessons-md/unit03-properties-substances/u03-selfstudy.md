## Ladder 1 • Identifying intermolecular forces

`CED 3.1`

Three forces, in increasing strength. **London dispersion** acts in
every substance. **Dipole–dipole** adds for polar molecules.
**Hydrogen bonding** requires H bonded *directly* to N, O or F.
A fourth, **ion–dipole**, appears when an ion is dissolved.

> 📘 **I do: naming the strongest force present**
>
> Identify the strongest intermolecular force in CH₄, CH₂Cl₂,
> NH₃ and a solution of NaCl in water.
> 
> **CH₄** — nonpolar, symmetric. Only **London
> dispersion**.
> 
> **CH₂Cl₂** — polar (the shape does not cancel the C-Cl
> dipoles), but its hydrogens are on carbon. **Dipole–dipole**.
> 
> **NH₃** — hydrogen bonded directly to nitrogen.
> **Hydrogen bonding**.
> 
> **NaCl(aq)** — ions surrounded by polar water molecules.
> **Ion–dipole**, the strongest of the set.
> 
> **The trap:** calling any molecule containing hydrogen a
> hydrogen-bonder. The hydrogen must be attached to N, O or F — carbon does
> not count, however many hydrogens there are.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 1 — four questions**
>
> 1. Strongest force in CO₂: 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Strongest force in CH₃OH: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Strongest force in HCl: 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. CH₃F contains fluorine but cannot hydrogen bond. Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) London dispersion     (b) hydrogen bonding    
> (c) dipole–dipole     (d) its H atoms are on carbon, not on F

## Ladder 2 • Ranking boiling points

`CED 3.1` `CED 3.3`

Boiling means separating molecules completely, so the boiling point tracks
the strength of the intermolecular forces. Compare force *type*
first; only if the types match does size (electron count) decide.

> 📘 **I do: two comparisons, two different reasons**
>
> Rank Ar ($-186$), HCl ($-85$) and H₂O ($+100$
> °C), then explain why Br₂ boils higher than Cl₂.
> 
> **The three-way ranking is by force type.** Ar has dispersion
> only; HCl adds dipole–dipole; H₂O hydrogen bonds. Stronger
> forces need more energy to overcome, so the order follows directly.
> 
> **Br₂ against Cl₂ is a different comparison.** Both are
> nonpolar with dispersion only, so type cannot decide. Br₂ has far
> more electrons, so its electron cloud is more **polarizable** — it
> distorts more easily, giving larger instantaneous dipoles and stronger
> dispersion forces. Hence the higher boiling point.
> 
> **The method:** ask *is the force type the same?* If not, type
> decides. If so, count electrons.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 2 — four questions**
>
> 1. Which boils higher, CH₄ or C₄H₁₀? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Which boils higher, H₂S or H₂O? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which boils higher, CH₃OCH₃ or CH₃CH₂OH? Both are
>    C₂H₆O.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. HF has stronger hydrogen bonds than H₂O, yet water boils
>    higher. Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) C₄H₁₀ — more electrons     (b) H₂O —
> hydrogen bonding     (c) ethanol — its H is on O     (d) water forms
> more hydrogen bonds per molecule

## Ladder 3 • Classifying solids

`CED 3.2`

Four types, each with properties that follow from what is holding the
particles together.

> 📘 **I do: classify and predict**
>
> Classify SiO₂, NaCl, Cu and solid CO₂, and give one
> property of each that follows.
> 
> **SiO₂ — covalent network.** A continuous lattice of strong
> covalent bonds. Melting means breaking covalent bonds, so the melting
> point is very high and it is hard and non-conducting.
> 
> **NaCl — ionic.** Strong electrostatic attractions between
> ions: high-melting and brittle, conducting only when molten or dissolved.
> 
> **Cu — metallic.** Cations in a delocalized electron sea:
> conducts as a solid, and is malleable.
> 
> **Solid CO₂ — molecular.** Only weak dispersion forces between
> whole CO₂ molecules. Very low melting point — it sublimes at
> $-78$ °C — and does not conduct.
> 
> **The distinction that matters most:** in a molecular solid the
> covalent bonds *within* each molecule are strong, but melting only
> has to overcome the weak forces *between* molecules. That is why dry
> ice sublimes so easily despite containing strong C=O bonds.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>
> 1. Classify diamond and give its expected melting point behaviour.
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Classify solid I₂: 
>    *(working space)*

>    Which type?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not,
>    though both are carbon.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) covalent network, very high     (b) molecular    
> (c) ionic     (d) graphite has delocalized electrons; diamond's are all
> in sigma bonds

## Ladder 4 • Phase changes and heating curves

`CED 3.3`

On a heating curve, sloped sections are temperature rising (kinetic energy
increasing) and flat sections are phase changes (potential energy
increasing while temperature holds constant).

> 📘 **I do: reading the flat sections**
>
> A heating curve for water runs from ice below 0 °C to steam
> above 100 °C. Explain the two flat regions, and why the second
> is longer.
> 
> **The flats are phase changes.** At 0 °C the ice melts;
> at 100 °C the water boils. Throughout each, energy is being
> supplied but the temperature does not move.
> 
> **Why the temperature holds:** the energy goes into overcoming
> intermolecular attractions — raising *potential* energy — not into
> speeding the molecules up. Temperature measures average *kinetic*
> energy, which is unchanged.
> 
> **Why the boiling flat is longer:** melting only loosens the lattice
> — molecules leave fixed positions but stay in contact, and most hydrogen
> bonds survive. Vaporizing must separate them completely, overcoming
> essentially all the attractions. For water that is
> 2260 J/g against 334 J/g, nearly seven
> times as much.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 4 — four questions**
>
> 1. On a heating curve, what is happening on a *sloped* section?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Why does temperature stay constant during melting?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which requires more energy per gram for water, melting or
>    vaporizing, and why?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Steam at 100 °C causes worse burns than water at
>    100 °C. Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) temperature rising, kinetic energy increasing    
> (b) energy goes to potential, not kinetic     (c) vaporizing —
> separates molecules completely     (d) it releases its heat of
> condensation as well

## Ladder 5 • The ideal gas law

`CED 3.4`

$PV = nRT$, with $R = 0.08206\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm/mol/K}$. That
choice of $R$ fixes every other unit: pressure in atm, volume in litres,
temperature in **kelvin**.

> 📘 **I do: solving for volume**
>
> What volume does 0.250 mol of gas occupy at 2.00 atm and
> 300 K?
> 
> $$ V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{(0.250)(0.08206)(300)}{2.00}    = \mathbf{3.08~L} $$
> 
> **Sense check:** one mole at 1 atm and 273 K is
> 22.4 L. A quarter of a mole at double the pressure should be
> roughly $22.4/4/2 \approx 2.8$ L, and a slightly higher temperature nudges
> it up. 3.08 is right.
> 
> **The error that costs whole questions:** leaving the temperature in
> °C. At 27 °C you must use 300 K, and
> using 27 gives an answer wrong by a factor of eleven.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>
> 1. 1.50 mol in 5.00 L at 350 K.
>    Pressure?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. 2.00 atm, 4.00 L, 0.300 mol.
>    Temperature?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. 0.500 g of helium in 2.00 L at
>    298 K. Pressure?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. A rigid container is heated from 300 K to
>    600 K. What happens to the pressure, and why?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 8.62 atm     (b) 325 K    
> (c) 1.53 atm     (d) doubles — $P \propto T$ at fixed $n$, $V$

## Ladder 6 • Gas stoichiometry

`CED 3.4`

Moles are the bridge. Convert to moles, use the balanced equation, then
convert the moles of gas to a volume with $PV = nRT$. Use
22.4 L/mol *only* at STP.

> 📘 **I do: solid to gas volume**
>
> 5.00 g of NaHCO₃ ($M = 84.01\,\mathrm{g/mol}$)
> decomposes, releasing CO₂. What volume does the gas occupy at
> 1.00 atm and 298 K?
> 
> **Moles of solid:** $5.00/84.01 = 0.0595$ mol.
> 
> **Through the equation:** each NaHCO₃ releases one CO₂, so
> $n(\text{CO₂}) = 0.0595$ mol.
> 
> **To a volume:**
> 
> $$ V = \frac{nRT}{P} = \frac{(0.05952)(0.08206)(298)}{1.00}    = \mathbf{1.46~L} $$
> 
> **Why not just use 22.4?** Because 22.4 L/mol holds
> only at STP (273 K, 1 atm). At 298 K the
> molar volume is 24.5 L, and using 22.4 would give
> 1.33 L — about 9 % low.

>

> 3. Why is 22.4 L/mol wrong at 298 K?
>    *(working space)*

>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 7 • Partial pressures

`CED 3.4`

Each gas in a mixture exerts the pressure it would exert alone. The total
is their sum, and each one's share is set by its **mole fraction** —
not by its molar mass.

> 📘 **I do: mole fractions to partial pressures**
>
> A vessel holds 0.400 mol N₂, 0.200 mol O₂ and
> 0.400 mol He at a total pressure of 3.00 atm. Find
> each partial pressure.
> 
> **Total moles:** $0.400 + 0.200 + 0.400 = 1.000$.
> 
> $$ P_{\text{N₂}} = \frac{0.400}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{1.20~atm} \qquad    P_{\text{O₂}} = \frac{0.200}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{0.60~atm} $$
> 
> $$ P_{\text{He}} = \frac{0.400}{1.000}(3.00) = \mathbf{1.20~atm} $$
> 
> **Check:** $1.20 + 0.60 + 1.20 = 3.00$. ✓
> 
> **Note what did not matter:** helium is by far the lightest gas here
> and nitrogen is seven times heavier, yet they exert identical partial
> pressures because they are present in identical amounts. Pressure counts
> particles, not mass.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 7 — four questions**
>

>    23.8 torr, what is the dry-gas pressure?
>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 8 • Kinetic molecular theory and effusion

`CED 3.5`

At a given temperature all gases have the same average *kinetic
energy*. They do not have the same average *speed* — because
$\text{KE} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, the lighter molecule must move faster.

> 📘 **I do: energy, speed, and rate**
>
> Helium and argon are at the same temperature. Compare their kinetic
> energies and speeds, and find the ratio of their effusion rates.
> 
> **Kinetic energy: equal.** It depends only on absolute temperature.
> 
> **Speed: helium is faster.** With
> $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$ the same for both, the smaller mass must carry the
> larger speed.
> 
> **Effusion rate** goes as the inverse square root of molar mass:
> 
> $$ \frac{\text{rate}_{\text{He}}}{\text{rate}_{\text{Ar}}} =    \sqrt{\frac{39.95}{4.003}} = \sqrt{9.98} = \mathbf{3.16} $$
> 
> Helium effuses about three times faster.
> 
> **Watch the direction of the ratio.** The *lighter* gas is
> faster, so the heavier mass goes on top. Getting it upside down gives
> 0.32 — a classic sign-of-the-error answer.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. Ratio of effusion rates of H₂ (2.016) to O₂ (32.00):
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Ratio for CH₄ (16.04) to SO₂ (64.07):
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Two gases at the same temperature: which has the greater average
>    kinetic energy?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Describe how the speed distribution of He differs from that
>    of Ar at the same temperature.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 3.98     (b) 2.00     (c) equal    
> (d) helium's peak is at higher speed, broader and lower

## Ladder 9 • Deviation from ideal behaviour

`CED 3.6`

The ideal model assumes molecules have no volume and no attractions. Both
assumptions fail — and they fail in opposite directions.

> 📘 **I do: two regimes, two causes**
>
> Explain why a real gas shows a lower pressure than predicted at moderately
> high pressure, but a higher pressure at very high pressure.
> 
> **Moderately high pressure — attractions dominate.** Molecules are
> close enough to attract one another. One heading for the wall is pulled
> back by those behind it and strikes with less force, so the measured
> pressure falls *below* the ideal prediction.
> 
> **Very high pressure — molecular volume dominates.** Now the
> molecules are packed so tightly that the space they themselves occupy is
> no longer negligible. The free volume is less than $V$, so the gas is more
> compressed than the law assumes and the pressure runs *above* the
> prediction.
> 
> **Where the model works best:** **high temperature** (kinetic
> energy swamps the attractions) and **low pressure** (molecules far
> apart, own volume negligible).

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 9 — four questions**
>
> 1. State the two conditions for most nearly ideal behaviour.
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Which behaves more ideally at the same $T$ and $P$, He or
>    H₂O vapour? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which assumption fails when a gas liquefies on cooling?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why does raising the temperature reduce the deviation caused by
>    attractions?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) high $T$, low $P$     (b) He — no hydrogen bonding
>     (c) the no-attractions assumption     (d) kinetic energy
> overwhelms the attraction

## Ladder 10 • Molarity and dilution

`CED 3.7`

Molarity is moles of solute per litre of *solution*, not of solvent.
On dilution the moles of solute are unchanged — only the volume grows.

> 📘 **I do: a preparation and a dilution**
>
> (i) 0.0250 mol of solute is made up to 500.0 mL.
> Find the molarity. (ii) Then 50.0 mL of
> 2.00 M stock is diluted to 500.0 mL.
> 
> **(i)** $M = n/V = 0.0250/0.500 = \mathbf{0.0500~M}$.
> 
> **(ii)** Moles of solute do not change, so
> $M_1V_1 = M_2V_2$:
> 
> $$ M_2 = \frac{(2.00)(50.0)}{500.0} = \mathbf{0.200~M} $$
> 
> **Why “of solution” matters:** to make the first solution you
> dissolve the solid in *less* than 500 mL and then
> make up to the mark. Adding solid to 500 mL of water gives
> a final volume above 500 mL and a concentration below
> target.

## Ladder 11 • Solubility and “like dissolves like”

`CED 3.10`

A solute dissolves when the solute–solvent attractions formed are
comparable to the solute–solute and solvent–solvent attractions broken.

> 📘 **I do: two solutes, one solvent**
>
> Explain why NaCl dissolves readily in water but not in hexane, and
> why I₂ does the reverse.
> 
> **NaCl in water:** water is polar and surrounds each ion with
> strong **ion–dipole** attractions — oxygen ends toward Na+,
> hydrogen ends toward Cl-. The energy released repays the cost of
> pulling the lattice apart.
> 
> **NaCl in hexane:** hexane is nonpolar and offers only weak
> dispersion forces, which cannot compensate for NaCl's very large
> lattice energy. It does not dissolve.
> 
> **I₂ reversed:** iodine is nonpolar. In hexane, dispersion
> forces between solute and solvent are comparable to those being replaced,
> so it dissolves. In water, dissolving would mean disrupting water's
> hydrogen-bond network with nothing comparable offered in return — so it
> barely dissolves.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 11 — four questions**
>
> 1. Will CH₃OH dissolve in water? Why? 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Will CCl₄ dissolve in water? Why? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which is more soluble in water, CH₃CH₂OH or
>    CH₃(CH₂)₆CH₃?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Describe how water molecules orient around a dissolved Ca²⁺
>    ion.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) yes — hydrogen bonds with water     (b) no — nonpolar
>     (c) ethanol     (d) oxygen ends inward, toward the cation

## Ladder 13 • Photons and the spectrum

`CED 3.11` `CED 3.12`

$E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$. Energy is *inversely* proportional to
wavelength: short wavelength means high energy.

> 📘 **I do: wavelength to energy per mole**
>
> Find the energy of one photon of 400 nm light, and the
> energy per mole.
> 
> $$ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} =    \frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(3.00\times10^{8})}{400\times10^{-9}}    = \mathbf{4.97\times10^{-19}~J} $$
> 
> **Per mole:** $4.97\times10^{-19} \times 6.022\times10^{23} = 2.99e5$ J $= \mathbf{299~kJ/mol}$.
> 
> **The unit trap:** the wavelength must be in **metres**.
> Leaving it in nanometres makes the answer wrong by a factor of
> $10^{9}$.
> 
> **Sense check:** 400 nm is violet, the high-energy end
> of the visible range. Red light at 700 nm should come out
> lower — and it does, at 2.84e-19 J.

## Ladder 14 • The Beer–Lambert law

`CED 3.13`

$A = \varepsilon b c$: absorbance is proportional to concentration at
fixed path length. That proportionality is what makes it an analytical
tool — measure $A$, get $c$.

> 📘 **I do: absorbance to concentration**
>
> A solution has $A = 0.620$ in a 1.00 cm cell, with
> $\varepsilon = 2.00e3\,\mathrm{}$ /M/cm. Find the
> concentration.
> 
> $$ c = \frac{A}{\varepsilon b} = \frac{0.620}{(2.00\times10^{3})(1.00)}    = \mathbf{3.10\times10^{-4}~M} $$
> 
> **Why a calibration curve is used in practice:** you prepare
> standards of known concentration, measure each one's absorbance, and plot
> $A$ against $c$. The result is a straight line through the origin, and an
> unknown's concentration is read off it. That avoids needing
> $\varepsilon$ at all.
> 
> **Why absorbance and not transmittance:** absorbance is the quantity
> that is *linear* in concentration. Transmittance falls
> exponentially, which is far harder to read.

>    *(working space)*
