### When ionization is complete `ZUM §14.4, 14.6`

`SP 5`

For a *strong* acid, ionization is complete, so
$[\text{H+}]$ simply equals the **\_\_\_\_\_\_**. No
equilibrium calculation is needed.

There are only **six strong acids**, and they are worth memorizing
outright — every other acid you meet this year is weak:

$$ \text{HCl} \quad \text{HBr} \quad \text{HI} \quad \text{HNO₃} \quad    \text{H₂SO₄} \quad \text{HClO₄} $$

Note that HF is *not* on the list. The strong bases are the
hydroxides of Group 1 and the heavier Group 2 metals —
LiOH, NaOH, KOH, and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂,
Ba(OH)₂.

> 📘 **Worked example 1: strong acid and strong base**
>
> 0.010 M HCl: $[\text{H+}] = 0.010$, so
> $\mathrm{pH} = -\log(0.010) = \mathbf{2.00}$.
> 
> 0.010 M NaOH: $[\text{OH-}] = 0.010$, so
> $\mathrm{pOH} = 2.00$ and $\mathrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.00 = \mathbf{12.00}$.

> 📘 **Worked example 2: the factor everyone forgets**
>
> 0.010 M Ba(OH)₂ produces *two* hydroxide ions per
> formula unit:
> 
> $$ [\text{OH-}] = 2(0.010) = 0.020\,\mathrm{M} $$
> 
> $\mathrm{pOH} = -\log(0.020) = 1.70$, so $\mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{12.30}$ —
> not 12.00. Always check the formula for how many ions are released.

### The standard setup `ZUM §14.5`

`SP 5`

Nothing here is new machinery — it is a Chapter 13 ICE table with $K_a$ in
place of $K$.

|  | HA | H+ | A⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Initial** | $C_0$ | $\approx 0$ | 0 |
| **Change** | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** |
| **Equilibrium** | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** |

$K_a = \frac{x^2}{C_0 - x} \approx \frac{x^2}{C_0}   \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad   x = [\text{H+}] \approx$ **\_\_\_\_\_\_**

> 📘 **Worked example: acetic acid**
>
> Find the pH of 0.100 M acetic acid,
> $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$.
> 
> $$ \begin{align*}   \frac{x^2}{0.100 - x} &= 1.8\times10^{-5}\\   x^2 &\approx (1.8\times10^{-5})(0.100) = 1.8\times10^{-6}\\   x &= 1.34\times10^{-3} = [\text{H+}]\\   \mathrm{pH} &= -\log(1.34\times10^{-3}) = \mathbf{2.87} \end{align*} $$
> 
> **5% check:** $1.34\times10^{-3}/0.100 = 1.3\%$ — well under 5%, so
> the approximation holds.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> Compare that pH of 2.87 with 0.100 M HCl, which has
> pH 1.00. Same concentration, but the weak acid's $[\text{H+}]$ is roughly
> 75 times smaller. This is the difference between *strong* and
> *concentrated*, made numerical.

### $K_b$ and the parallel method `ZUM §14.6`

`SP 5`

$\text{B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ BH+(aq) + OH⁻(aq)}   \qquad   K_b =$ **\_\_\_\_\_\_**

The ICE method is identical to the weak acid case — but it produces
$[\text{OH-}]$, so you must convert through pOH at the end.

> 📘 **Worked example: ammonia**
>
> Find the pH of 0.100 M NH₃, $K_b = 1.8\times10^{-5}$.
> 
> $$ \begin{align*}   x^2/0.100 &\approx 1.8\times10^{-5}\\   x &= 1.34\times10^{-3} = [\text{OH-}]\\   \mathrm{pOH} &= 2.87 \quad\Rightarrow\quad   \mathrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.87 = \mathbf{11.13} \end{align*} $$
> 
> Note the symmetry with acetic acid: identical $K$ value, identical $x$ —
> but the answers sit on opposite sides of 7.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> The single most common error here is stopping at $x$ and calling it the pH.
> For a base, $x$ is $[\text{OH-}]$, giving pOH first. Forgetting the
> conversion turns pH 11.13 into 2.87 — a basic solution reported as
> strongly acidic.

#### The conjugate relationship

For any conjugate acid–base pair:
$K_a \times K_b =$ **\_\_\_\_\_\_** $= 1.0\times10^{-14}$

> 📘 **Worked example: acetate**
>
> Acetic acid has $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$. Its conjugate base, acetate:
> 
> $$ K_b = \frac{K_w}{K_a} = \frac{1.0\times10^{-14}}{1.8\times10^{-5}}       = 5.6\times10^{-10} $$
> 
> Small, but not zero — which is exactly why sodium acetate solutions are
> basic.

### One proton at a time `ZUM §14.7`

`SP 5`

A polyprotic acid ionizes in successive steps, each with its own constant:

$$ \text{H₃PO₄} \xrightarrow{K_{a1}} \text{H₂PO₄⁻}   \xrightarrow{K_{a2}} \text{HPO₄²⁻}   \xrightarrow{K_{a3}} \text{PO₄³⁻} $$

$$ K_{a1} = 7.5\times10^{-3} \gg K_{a2} = 6.2\times10^{-8}   \gg K_{a3} = 4.8\times10^{-13} $$

Each successive constant is roughly **\_\_\_\_\_\_** times
smaller. The reason is electrostatic: pulling a positive proton away from an
increasingly **\_\_\_\_\_\_** anion gets progressively harder.

> 📌 **Note**
>
> The practical consequence: for pH purposes, **only the first
> ionization matters**. The second contributes so little H+ that ignoring
> it changes the pH by far less than experimental error. Solve $K_{a1}$ alone
> and stop.
> 
> H₂SO₄ is the exception worth knowing — its first ionization is
> *strong*, and its second ($K_{a2} = 1.2\times10^{-2}$) is large enough
> to matter in concentrated solutions.

### Deciding from the parent acid and base `ZUM §14.8`

`SP 6`

Dissolve a salt and you get its ions. Whether the solution is acidic or
basic depends on whether either ion reacts with water.

| **Cation from** | **Anion from** | **Solution** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| strong base | strong acid | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | NaCl |
| strong base | weak acid | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | NaC₂H₃O₂ |
| weak base | strong acid | **\_\_\_\_\_\_** | NH₄Cl |
| weak base | weak acid | compare $K_a$ and $K_b$ | NH₄C₂H₃O₂ |

The logic is entirely conjugate-pair reasoning: an anion derived from a
*weak* acid is a meaningful base, while one from a *strong* acid
is not.

> 📘 **Worked example: Zumdahl's sodium fluoride**
>
> Find the pH of 0.30 M NaF, given
> $K_a(\text{HF}) = 7.2\times10^{-4}$.
> 
> Major species: Na+, F-, H₂O. Sodium is the cation of a strong
> base and does nothing. Fluoride is the conjugate base of a weak acid, so it
> hydrolyses:
> F⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ HF(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
> 
> $$ \begin{align*}   K_b &= \frac{K_w}{K_a} = \frac{1.0\times10^{-14}}{7.2\times10^{-4}}        = 1.4\times10^{-11}\\   x^2/0.30 &\approx 1.4\times10^{-11}\\   x &= 2.0\times10^{-6} = [\text{OH-}]\\   \mathrm{pOH} &= 5.69 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{8.31} \end{align*} $$
> 
> Basic, as predicted — and only mildly so, because $K_b$ is tiny.
