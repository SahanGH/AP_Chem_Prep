> 📌 **How to use this sheet**
>
> Naming is not a reasoning skill, it is a **recall** skill sitting on
> top of one decision. The decision is always the same:
> 
> | **Type** | **What it is** | **How you know** |
> |---|---|---|
> | I | ionic, fixed-charge metal | metal in group 1A, 2A, 3A, or Ag,
>   Zn, Cd — no Roman numeral |
> | II | ionic, variable-charge metal | transition metal (or Sn,
>   Pb) — Roman numeral *required* |
> | III | binary covalent | two nonmetals — prefixes, and only here |
> | A | acid | formula begins with H and is aqueous |

Almost every naming error is a **type** error, not a memory error —
prefixes used on an ionic compound, or a Roman numeral left off a
transition metal.

> 📌 **The oxyanion pattern — learn this instead of memorizing**
>
> Where a series of anions differ only in oxygen count, the names follow one
> pattern. Two members: fewer oxygens $\to$
> *-ite*, more $\to$
> *-ate*. More than two: the extremes take
> *hypo-* (fewest) and
> *per-* (most).
> 
> The chlorine series is the one to hold in memory, because every other
> halogen copies it exactly:
> 
> $$ \underbrace{\text{ClO-}}_{\text{hypochlorite}} \quad    \underbrace{\text{ClO₂-}}_{\text{chlorite}} \quad    \underbrace{\text{ClO₃-}}_{\text{chlorate}} \quad    \underbrace{\text{ClO₄-}}_{\text{perchlorate}} $$
> 
> Know ClO₃- is chlorate and SO₄²⁻ is sulfate, and the rest of
> each series follows without memorizing anything else.

## Part 2 • Acids

An acid is one or more H+ ions attached to an anion, so **the
acid name is decided entirely by the anion's name**:

| **Anion ending** | **Acid name** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|
| *-ide* (no oxygen) | **hydro**(root)**ic** acid | chloride $\to$ hydrochloric acid |
| *-ite* | (root)**ous** acid | sulfite $\to$ sulfurous acid |
| *-ate* | (root)**ic** acid | sulfate $\to$ sulfuric acid |

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **“I ate it, so it was ic.”** The *-ate* anion gives the
> *-ic* acid. The commonest slip on this topic is naming H₂SO₃
> sulfuric acid — it contains sulf*ite*, so it is sulfur*ous*
> acid.

## Part 3 • Worked examples

> 📘 **Worked example 1: Fe(ClO₃)₂, one step at a time**
>
> **1. Type?** Fe is a transition metal $\Rightarrow$ Type II, so a
> Roman numeral is required and no prefixes are used.
> 
> **2. Anion?** ClO₃- is chlorate, charge $1-$. There are two of
> them, so the negative charge totals $2-$.
> 
> **3. Cation charge, by balance?** Fe must be $2+$.
> 
> **4. Assemble.** **Iron(II) chlorate.**
> 
> *Note what supplied the Roman numeral* — not the formula's
> subscript on iron, but the total charge on the anions. The subscript 2
> belongs to ClO₃-, not to Fe.

> 📘 **Worked example 2: reverse — ammonium sulfate**
>
> **1. Ions.** Ammonium is NH₄+; sulfate is SO₄²⁻.
> 
> **2. Balance.** One $2-$ needs two $1+$, so two ammonium ions.
> 
> **3. Assemble.** A polyatomic ion taken more than once needs
> parentheses: (NH₄)₂SO₄.
> 
> Writing NH₄₂SO₄ would read as forty-two hydrogens. Parentheses are not
> decoration.

> 📘 **Worked example 3: three lookalikes**
>
> $$ \text{Na₂SO₃} \qquad \text{Na₂SO₄} \qquad \text{Na₂S₂O₃} $$
> 
> Sodium is fixed-charge in all three, so the whole answer sits in the anion:
> sulf*ite*, sulf*ate*, and *thio*sulfate respectively —
> **sodium sulfite**, **sodium sulfate**,
> **sodium thiosulfate**.
> 
> The prefix *thio-* means an oxygen has been replaced by a sulfur:
> compare SO₄²⁻ with S₂O₃²⁻.
