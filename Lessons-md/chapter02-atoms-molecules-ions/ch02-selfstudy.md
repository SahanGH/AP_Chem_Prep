## Ladder 1 • The laws that demanded atoms

`ZUM §2.2–2.3`

Three quantitative laws came first; Dalton's theory was invented to
explain them.

| **Law** | **What it states** |
|---|---|
| Conservation of mass | mass is neither created nor destroyed in a
  reaction |
| Definite proportion | a given compound always has the same proportions
  by mass |
| Multiple proportions | when two elements form more than one compound,
  the masses of one combining with a fixed mass of the other are in
  *small whole-number ratios* |

The third is the decisive one: small whole numbers are what you expect if
matter comes in indivisible units, and are hard to explain otherwise.

> 📘 **I do: testing the law of multiple proportions**
>
> Two oxides of sulfur are analysed. Oxide A is 50.05% S by mass; oxide B
> is 40.05% S. Show that these obey the law of multiple proportions, and
> identify the ratio.
> 
> **Fix the sulfur, compare the oxygen.** Take 100 g of
> each:
> 
> $$ \text{A}: 50.05~\text{g S} + 49.95~\text{g O} \qquad    \text{B}: 40.05~\text{g S} + 59.95~\text{g O} $$
> 
> **Oxygen per gram of sulfur:**
> 
> $$ \text{A}: \frac{49.95}{50.05} = 0.998 \qquad    \text{B}: \frac{59.95}{40.05} = 1.497 $$
> 
> **Ratio of those two figures:**
> 
> $$ \frac{1.497}{0.998} = 1.50 = \frac{3}{2} $$
> 
> A small whole-number ratio, exactly as the law requires. The compounds
> are SO₂ and SO₃: for a fixed amount of sulfur, B holds half
> again as much oxygen.
> 
> **The move to remember:** hold one element constant, then divide.
> The ratio only looks simple after that normalization.

## Ladder 2 • Three experiments, three
conclusions

`ZUM §2.4`

Every one of these is examinable as *experiment $\to$ observation
$\to$ conclusion*. Learn the chain, not the anecdote.

| **Experiment** | **Key observation** | **Conclusion** |
|---|---|---|
| Thomson, cathode ray | the beam bends toward the positive plate,
  identically for every cathode metal | a negative particle common to all atoms; gave $e/m$, not $e$ |
| Millikan, oil drop | droplet charges are all multiples of one value | the charge on the electron, so with Thomson's ratio, its mass |
| Rutherford, gold foil | a few alpha particles deflect sharply backward | a tiny, dense, positive nucleus in a mostly empty atom |

> 📘 **I do: reasoning from the observation**
>
> **Why did Rutherford's result overturn the plum-pudding model?**
> 
> **What the old model predicted.** If positive charge were spread
> thinly through the whole atom, an alpha particle would meet only weak,
> diffuse repulsion everywhere. *Every* alpha should pass nearly
> straight through, with slight deflection at most.
> 
> **What was observed.** The vast majority did pass straight through
> — but roughly 1 in 20 000 bounced back at more than
> 90 °.
> 
> **Why that is decisive.** Reversing a fast, massive alpha particle
> requires a concentrated positive charge that is also *massive*
> enough not to simply recoil away. Spread-out charge cannot do it at any
> strength. So the positive charge and nearly all the mass must sit in a
> minute volume — a nucleus — while the rest of the atom is
> essentially empty.
> 
> **Note the shape of the argument:** the rare event, not the common
> one, carries the information. Most alphas passing through told
> Rutherford the atom is mostly empty; the few that came back told him
> what sits at the centre.

## Ladder 3 • Reading isotope symbols

`ZUM §2.5`

$$ \text{AZX} \qquad    Z = \text{atomic number} = \text{protons} \qquad    A = \text{mass number} = \text{protons} + \text{neutrons} $$

Three counts, three rules:

- **Protons** $= Z$ — and $Z$ *is* the element's
   identity. Change it and you have a different element.
- **Neutrons** $= A - Z$. Change it and you have an
   isotope of the same element.
- **Electrons** $= Z - \text{charge}$. Change it and you have
   an ion.

> 📘 **I do: three species, one procedure**
>
> **(a) ³¹₁₅P:** protons $= 15$; neutrons $= 31 - 15 = \mathbf{16}$; neutral, so electrons $= \mathbf{15}$.
> 
> **(b) ⁸⁰₃₅Br⁻:** protons $= 35$; neutrons $= 80 - 35 = \mathbf{45}$; the $1-$ charge means one *extra* electron, so
> electrons $= 35 - (-1) = \mathbf{36}$.
> 
> **(c) ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺:** protons $= 13$; neutrons
> $= 27 - 13 = \mathbf{14}$; the $3+$ charge means three electrons
> *missing*, so electrons $= 13 - 3 = \mathbf{10}$.
> 
> **The sign trap, stated once:** a *negative* ion has
> *more* electrons, a *positive* ion has fewer. The formula
> electrons $= Z - \text{charge}$ handles both automatically, provided you
> carry the charge with its sign.
> 
> **Worth noticing:** the bromide ion in (b) has 36 electrons, the
> same as krypton, and the aluminium ion in (c) has 10, the same as neon.
> Ions form by reaching a noble-gas electron count.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>

>    *(working space)*
> 3. An ion has 20 protons, 20 neutrons, and 18 electrons. Identify it
>    and write its full symbol.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. ¹⁴C and ¹⁴N share a mass number. Are they
>    isotopes of each other? Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 26, 30, 26     (b) 16, 16, 18    
> (c) ⁴⁰₂₀Ca²⁺     (d) no — different $Z$, so different
> elements

## Ladder 4 • The periodic table as a map

`ZUM §2.7`

The table is arranged so that position predicts behaviour.

- **Groups** (columns) share chemical behaviour: 1A
   alkali metals, 2A alkaline earth metals, 7A
   halogens, 8A noble gases.
- **Periods** (rows) are filled left to right.
- **Metals** sit left of the staircase, **nonmetals**
   right, metalloids along it (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te).
- Metals *lose* electrons to form cations; nonmetals
   *gain* them to form anions.

> 📘 **I do: predicting from position alone**
>
> **Predict the ion formed by strontium, by selenium, and by argon
> — using only their positions.**
> 
> **Strontium** is in group 2A, so it has two outer electrons to lose
> and forms **Sr²⁺**. Every group 2A metal does the same, which
> is why the group has a shared name.
> 
> **Selenium** is in group 6A, two short of eight, so it gains two:
> **Se²⁻**. The pattern across the nonmetals is
> $8 - \text{group number}$ electrons gained.
> 
> **Argon** is in group 8A, already at eight outer electrons, so it
> forms **no ion** — there is nothing to gain or lose cheaply. That
> is exactly why the noble gases are unreactive, and why every other ion
> above lands on a noble-gas count.
> 
> **The single rule underneath all three:** elements form the ion that
> reaches the nearest noble-gas electron count.

## Ladder 5 • Molecules, ions, and formulas

`ZUM §2.6`

An **ionic** compound is a lattice of ions held by electrostatic
attraction — its formula is the *simplest whole-number ratio*, not
a discrete particle. A **molecular** compound is made of discrete
molecules, and its formula counts the atoms in one of them.

Charge balance writes every ionic formula: total positive $=$ total
negative, with no charges shown in the finished formula.

> 📘 **I do: building formulas from charges**
>
> **(a) Aluminium and oxygen.** Al³⁺ and O²⁻. The lowest
> common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6, so two Al³⁺ ($+6$) balance three
> O²⁻ ($-6$): **Al₂O₃**.
> 
> **(b) Calcium and phosphate.** Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻. Again
> LCM 6: three Ca²⁺ ($+6$) with two PO₄³⁻ ($-6$). The
> polyatomic ion is taken more than once, so it needs parentheses:
> **Ca₃(PO₄)₂**.
> 
> **(c) Ammonium and sulfate.** NH₄+ and SO₄²⁻: two
> ammonium per sulfate, **(NH₄)₂SO₄**.
> 
> **Why parentheses are not optional:** NH₄₂SO₄ would read as
> forty-two hydrogens. The parentheses say “two of the whole group”.
> 
> **And the conceptual point:** Al₂O₃ does not describe a
> molecule. There is no Al₂O₃ particle — only a vast lattice in
> which aluminium and oxide ions occur in a 2:3 ratio.

## Ladder 6 • Averaging isotopes

`ZUM §2.5`

The mass on the periodic table is a **weighted average** over the
naturally occurring isotopes:

$$ \bar m = \sum(\text{fractional abundance} \times \text{isotope mass}) $$

No single atom has that mass. The average always lies between the
extremes and leans toward the abundant isotope — two checks that cost
nothing and catch most errors.

> 📘 **I do: the atomic mass of chlorine**
>
> Chlorine is 75.78% ³⁵Cl (34.969 u) and 24.22% ³⁷Cl
> (36.966 u).
> 
> $$ \bar m = (0.7578)(34.969) + (0.2422)(36.966) $$
> 
> $$ \phantom{\bar m} = 26.500 + 8.953 = \mathbf{35.45~u} $$
> 
> **Both checks:** 35.45 lies between 34.969 and 36.966 — good; and
> it sits much closer to 34.969, which is right because ³⁵Cl is
> roughly three times as abundant.
> 
> **What the value means physically:** there is no chlorine atom
> weighing 35.45 u. Every one weighs about 35 or about 37; 35.45 is what a
> mole of natural chlorine averages to, which is exactly the number needed
> for weighing bulk samples.

## Ladder 7 • Choosing the naming rule

`ZUM §2.8`

Nomenclature is drilled at length in Worksheet 3. What matters *here*
is the decision that comes first — almost every naming error is a
mis-classification, not a forgotten name.

| **Type** | **How you recognize it** | **How it is named** |
|---|---|---|
| I | metal of fixed charge (1A, 2A, 3A, Ag, Zn, Cd) | metal $+$ anion; *no* Roman numeral |
| II | transition metal, or Sn / Pb | Roman numeral gives the charge, deduced from the anions |
| III | two nonmetals | prefixes on both elements (no *mono-* on the first) |
| A | formula starts with H, aqueous | *-ide* to hydro… ic; *-ite* to *-ous*;
  *-ate* to *-ic* |

> 📘 **I do: classify first, then name**
>
> Name TiO₂, SO₃, and HBrO₃(aq) — classifying each before
> writing anything.
> 
> **TiO₂.** Titanium is a transition metal $\Rightarrow$
> **Type II**, so a Roman numeral is required and no prefixes are
> used. Two O²⁻ give $-4$, so titanium is $+4$:
> **titanium(IV) oxide**. The numeral came from the *anions*,
> never from the subscript on titanium.
> 
> **SO₃.** Sulfur and oxygen are both nonmetals $\Rightarrow$
> **Type III**, so prefixes and no numeral: **sulfur trioxide**.
> Writing “sulfur(VI) oxide” applies the wrong rule set entirely.
> 
> **HBrO₃(aq).** Starts with H and is aqueous $\Rightarrow$
> **acid**. The anion BrO₃- is bromate, an *-ate*, which
> gives an *-ic* acid: **bromic acid**.
> 
> **The pattern:** each name followed from the classification, and
> each classification took one glance at what kind of elements were
> present.
