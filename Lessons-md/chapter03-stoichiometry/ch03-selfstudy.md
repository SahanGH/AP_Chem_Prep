## Ladder 1 • Formula from percent composition

`ZUM §3.7`

The four steps, always the same: assume 100 g so percents become
grams; convert each element to moles; divide everything by the smallest;
multiply to whole numbers if needed. The molecular formula is the
empirical formula times $M/M_{\text{empirical}}$.

> 📘 **I do: caffeine**
>
> A stimulant is 49.47% C, 5.19% H, 28.86% N, and 16.48% O, with molar
> mass 194.20 g/mol. Find both formulas.
> 
> **Assume 100 g, convert to moles:**
> 
> $$ \text{C}: \frac{49.47}{12.01} = 4.119 \qquad    \text{H}: \frac{5.19}{1.008} = 5.15 \qquad    \text{N}: \frac{28.86}{14.01} = 2.060 \qquad    \text{O}: \frac{16.48}{16.00} = 1.030 $$
> 
> **Divide by the smallest (1.030):**
> 
> $$ \text{C}: 4.00 \qquad \text{H}: 5.00 \qquad \text{N}: 2.00 \qquad    \text{O}: 1.00 $$
> 
> **Empirical** C₄H₅N₂O, with
> $M_{\text{emp}} = 4(12.01) + 5(1.008) + 2(14.01) + 16.00 = 97.10\,\mathrm{g/mol}$.
> 
> **Molecular:** $194.20 / 97.10 = 2.00$, so
> $\boxed{\text{C₈H₁₀N₄O₂}}$ — caffeine.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 1**
>
> A fertilizer compound is 20.00% C, 6.71% H, 46.65% N, and 26.64% O,
> with molar mass 60.06 g/mol. Find both formulas.
> 
> *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** empirical CH₄N₂O; multiplier $= 1$, so molecular also
> CH₄N₂O (urea)

## Ladder 2 • Combustion analysis, C and H
only

`ZUM §3.7`

In excess oxygen every C atom lands in a CO₂ and every H atom in an
H₂O — so count the atoms there. Two H per water is the step most
often dropped.

> 📘 **I do: an unknown fuel gas**
>
> Burning 0.5812 g of a compound of C and H gives
> 1.760 g CO₂ and 0.901 g H₂O; the molar mass
> is 58.12 g/mol.
> 
> **Carbon:**
> $\dfrac{1.760}{44.01} = 0.03999\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{CO₂} \Rightarrow 0.03999\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{C} \Rightarrow 0.4803\,\mathrm{g}$
> 
> **Hydrogen** (two per water):
> $\dfrac{0.901}{18.02} = 0.05000\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{H₂O} \Rightarrow 0.1000\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{H} \Rightarrow 0.1008\,\mathrm{g}$
> 
> **Mass check:** $0.4803 + 0.1008 = 0.5811\,\mathrm{g}$ — the whole
> sample. C and H really are all there is.
> 
> **Ratio:** $\text{H}/\text{C} = 0.1000/0.03999 = 2.50 = 5/2$, so the
> empirical formula is C₂H₅, $M_{\text{emp}} = 29.06\,\mathrm{g/mol}$.
> Then $58.12/29.06 = 2.00$: $\boxed{\text{C₄H₁₀}}$, butane. Note the 2.50 was
> an instruction to multiply by 2, never to round.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 2**
>
> Burning 0.3007 g of a C,H compound gives 0.880 g
> CO₂ and 0.541 g H₂O; the molar mass is
> 30.07 g/mol. Find both formulas. 
> 
> *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** $\text{H}/\text{C} = 3.00 \to$ empirical CH₃; $30.07/15.03 = 2 \to$ molecular C₂H₆ (ethane)

## Ladder 3 • Combustion analysis with oxygen
in the compound

`ZUM §3.7`

Oxygen in the sample cannot be measured directly — it ends up in the
same CO₂ and H₂O as the oxygen from the air. Find C and H as
before; **oxygen is whatever sample mass is left**.

> 📘 **I do: a compound of C, H, and O**
>
> Burning 1.000 g of a compound of C, H, and O gives
> 1.911 g CO₂ and 1.174 g H₂O; the molar mass
> is 46.07 g/mol.
> 
> **C and H first:**
> 
> $$ \text{C}: \frac{1.911}{44.01} = 0.04342\,\mathrm{mol} \to 0.5215\,\mathrm{g}    \qquad    \text{H}: 2 \times \frac{1.174}{18.02} = 0.1303\,\mathrm{mol}    \to 0.1313\,\mathrm{g} $$
> 
> **Oxygen by difference:**
> 
> $$ m_{\text{O}} = 1.000 - 0.5215 - 0.1313 = 0.347\,\mathrm{g}    \Rightarrow \frac{0.347}{16.00} = 0.0217\,\mathrm{mol} $$
> 
> **Divide by the smallest (0.0217):**
> $\text{C}: 2.00$, $\text{H}: 6.00$, $\text{O}: 1.00$ — empirical C₂H₆O,
> $M_{\text{emp}} = 46.07\,\mathrm{g/mol} = M$, so
> $\boxed{\text{C₂H₆O}}$, ethanol. Multiplier 1 again.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3**
>
> Burning 1.500 g of a compound of C, H, and O gives
> 2.199 g CO₂ and 0.900 g H₂O; the molar mass
> is 90.08 g/mol. Find both formulas. 
> 
> *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** empirical CH₂O; $90.08/30.03 = 3 \to$ molecular
> C₃H₆O₃ (lactic acid)

## Ladder 4 • Reading a chemical equation

`ZUM §3.8`

An equation states how *atoms rearrange*. Coefficients are
**ratios** — of molecules, or equally of moles — never an
inventory of what you have. Atoms and mass are conserved; molecules and
moles need not be. And balancing is done only with coefficients: changing
a subscript changes the substance.

> 📘 **I do: reading an equation aloud**
>
> 4NH₃(g) + 5O₂(g) → 4NO(g) + 6H₂O(g)
> Everything this equation asserts:
> 
> - Four ammonia molecules react with five oxygen molecules to give
>    four nitrogen monoxide molecules and six water molecules — all
>    as gases.
> - Equally: 4 mol NH₃ react with 5 mol O₂ per 4 mol NO
>    and 6 mol H₂O. Or 1 mol NH₃ per $\tfrac{5}{4}$ mol
>    O₂ — the ratio is the content, not the absolute numbers.
> - Atom check: N $4 = 4$; H $12 = 12$; O $10 = 4 + 6$. Conserved.
> - Nine molecules become ten. Fine — molecule count is not
>    conserved; atoms are.
> 
> What it does *not* assert: that 4 grams of ammonia react with 5 grams
> of oxygen. Coefficients never count grams.

## Ladder 5 • Balancing by inspection

`ZUM §3.9`

Strategy: start from the most complicated molecule; save lone elements
(O₂, metals) for last, because their coefficient can be set freely;
if an odd atom count forces a fraction, use it, then double everything.
Count every atom at the end.

> 📘 **I do: methanol combustion**
>
> $$ \text{CH₃OH(l) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(g)} \qquad\text{(unbalanced)} $$
> 
> **Methanol first** — 1 C gives $1\,\text{CO₂}$; 4 H give
> $2\,\text{H₂O}$:
> CH₃OH + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
> 
> **Oxygen last:** right side $2 + 2 = 4$ O; methanol already carries
> 1, so O₂ must supply 3 atoms $= \tfrac{3}{2}$ molecules:
> CH₃OH + tfrac{3}{2}O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
> 
> **Clear the fraction — double *every* coefficient:**
> 
> $$ \boxed{\;\text{2CH₃OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 4H₂O(g)}\;} $$
> 
> Count: C $2 = 2$; H $8 = 8$; O $2 + 6 = 4 + 4$. Balanced.

## Ladder 6 • Mass-to-mass stoichiometry

`ZUM §3.10`

> 
grams of A $\xrightarrow{\;\div M_A\;}$ moles of A
$\xrightarrow{\;\text{coefficients}\;}$ moles of B
$\xrightarrow{\;\times M_B\;}$ grams of B

Grams never talk to grams directly — the equation's coefficients only
convert *moles*.

> 📘 **I do: oxygen from potassium chlorate, both directions**
>
> Heating potassium chlorate releases oxygen:
> 2KClO₃(s) → 2KCl(s) + 3O₂(g), with
> $M_{\text{KClO₃}} = 122.55\,\mathrm{g/mol}$.
> 
> **(a) Mass of O₂ from 12.25 g KClO₃:**
> 
> $$ \frac{12.25}{122.55} = 0.09996\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{KClO₃}    \;\xrightarrow{\times 3/2}\; 0.1499\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{O₂}    \;\xrightarrow{\times 32.00}\; \mathbf{4.80~g} $$
> 
> **(b) Mass of KClO₃ needed for 1.00 g O₂** —
> same machine, run backward:
> 
> $$ \frac{1.00}{32.00} = 0.03125\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{O₂}    \;\xrightarrow{\times 2/3}\; 0.02083\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{KClO₃}    \;\xrightarrow{\times 122.55}\; \mathbf{2.55~g} $$
> 
> The ratio flipped from $\tfrac{3}{2}$ to $\tfrac{2}{3}$ when the direction
> flipped — read it off the coefficients each time; never guess a
> direction.

## Ladder 7 • Limiting reactant, yield, and
leftovers

`ZUM §3.11`

When amounts of *two* reactants are given: convert both to moles,
then use the mole ratio to ask whether what is present covers what is
required. The limiting reactant fixes everything — theoretical yield
comes from it alone, and the leftover is
$(\text{present}) - (\text{consumed})$. The reactant with fewer grams, or
even fewer moles, is *not* automatically the limiting one.

> 📘 **I do: burning magnesium, the whole problem**
>
> 4.50 g of Mg burns in 6.00 g of O₂:
> 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s). Limiting reactant, theoretical yield,
> leftover mass — and then the percent yield if 6.50 g of MgO is
> actually collected.
> 
> **Moles first:**
> 
> $$ \text{Mg}: \frac{4.50}{24.31} = 0.1851\,\mathrm{mol} \qquad    \text{O₂}: \frac{6.00}{32.00} = 0.1875\,\mathrm{mol} $$
> 
> **Limiting?** The Mg present needs
> $0.1851 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.0926\,\mathrm{mol}$ of O₂; 0.1875 mol
> is available — plenty. So **Mg is limiting** even though the two
> mole amounts are nearly equal: the $2{:}1$ ratio decides, not the raw
> numbers.
> 
> **Theoretical yield** (from Mg only):
> $0.1851 \times \tfrac{2}{2} = 0.1851\,\mathrm{mol}$ MgO
> $\times\, 40.31 = \mathbf{7.46~g}$.
> 
> **Leftover O₂:** $0.1875 - 0.0926 = 0.0949\,\mathrm{mol}$
> $\times\, 32.00 = \mathbf{3.04~g}$.
> 
> **Mass check:** in $4.50 + 6.00 = 10.50$ g; out
> $7.46 + 3.04 = 10.50$ g. Books balanced.
> 
> **Percent yield:**
> $\dfrac{6.50}{7.46} \times 100 = \mathbf{87.1\%}$ — actual over
> theoretical, always.
