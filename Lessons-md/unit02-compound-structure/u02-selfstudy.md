## Ladder 1 • Bond type from electronegativity

`CED 2.1`

Bonding is a continuum, not three boxes. The electronegativity difference
$\Delta$EN tells you where on it a bond sits: near zero, the pair is
shared equally; large, the electron is effectively transferred.

> 📘 **I do: placing three bonds on the continuum**
>
> Classify K-F, C-O and H-H using
> EN: H 2.1, C 2.5, O 3.5, F 4.0, K 0.8.
> 
> **K-F:** $\Delta$EN $= 4.0 - 0.8 = 3.2$. Very large — the
> electron is transferred, giving K+ and F-. **Ionic.**
> 
> **C-O:** $\Delta$EN $= 3.5 - 2.5 = 1.0$. Moderate — the pair
> is shared but pulled toward oxygen, leaving $\delta+$ on C and $\delta-$
> on O. **Polar covalent.**
> 
> **H-H:** $\Delta$EN $= 0$. Identical atoms share equally.
> **Nonpolar covalent.**
> 
> **Read the boundaries as guides, not laws.** Roughly: below
> 0.4 nonpolar, 0.4–1.7 polar, above 1.7 ionic. A
> bond at 1.6 is not categorically different from one at 1.8;
> the character shifts gradually.

>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 2 • Coulomb's law and lattice energy

`CED 2.2`

Lattice energy is the energy released when gaseous ions come together into
a crystal. It is Coulombic, so only two things matter: the
**product of the charges** and the **distance** between ion
centres.

$$ \text{attraction} \;\propto\; \frac{q_1 q_2}{d} $$

> 📘 **I do: one factor at a time**
>
> Rank LiF, NaF and KF by lattice energy, then compare
> NaF with MgO.
> 
> **Within the fluoride series,** every compound pairs a $1+$ with a
> $1-$, so the charge product is the same and cannot decide anything.
> *Distance* decides. Cation radii rise Li+ (76) $ \text{NaF} > \text{KF} \quad (1030 > 910 > 808~\text{kJ/mol}) $$
> 
> **NaF against MgO:** now the *charges* change.
> NaF is $(+1)(-1) = 1$; MgO is $(+2)(-2) = 4$ — four times
> the charge product. The ions are also smaller
> (212 pm against 235 pm), so $d$ is shorter
> too. Both factors point the same way, and the gap is enormous:
> 3795  against 910 kJ/mol.
> 
> **The method:** check charge first. If the charges match, distance
> decides. If they differ, charge almost always dominates.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 2 — four questions**
>
> 1. Larger lattice energy, NaCl or NaF? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Larger lattice energy, CaO or KF? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. MgO melts far higher than NaF. Connect this to lattice
>    energy.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Explain why comparing MgO with NaCl does *not*
>    isolate the effect of charge.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) NaF — F- smaller, shorter $d$    
> (b) CaO — charge product 4 vs 1     (c) stronger attractions need
> more energy to break     (d) charge *and* size both differ

## Ladder 3 • Bond order, length and energy

`CED 2.2`

Within the same pair of atoms, more shared pairs means a shorter, stronger
bond. Bond order runs with energy and against length.

> 📘 **I do: a carbon–carbon series**
>
> Rank C-C, C=C and C#C by bond length and by bond energy.
> 
> Bond energies: 347, 614, 839 kJ/mol.
> 
> **Energy rises with bond order:** C-C $ C#C. Each extra shared pair adds attraction between the two nuclei
> and the electron density between them.
> 
> **Length falls as order rises:** C#C $ More shared electron density pulls the nuclei closer together.
> 
> **Note the energies are not simple multiples** — 614 is not twice
> 347, and 839 is not three times it. The second and third pairs are pi
> bonds, whose side-on overlap is less effective than the first bond's
> end-on overlap.

> 4. In O₂ (495) and F₂ (154), the bond order falls from 2 to

## Ladder 4 • Properties of ionic solids

`CED 2.3`

Every property of an ionic solid traces back to the same fact: charged
particles locked in a rigid lattice, held by strong electrostatic
attractions.

> 📘 **I do: four properties, one cause**
>
> Explain why NaCl is hard, brittle, high-melting, and conducts only
> when molten or dissolved.
> 
> **Hard and high-melting:** each ion is attracted to all its
> oppositely charged neighbours throughout the lattice. Deforming or melting
> the solid means overcoming many strong Coulombic attractions at once.
> 
> **Brittle:** a blow displaces one plane of ions by a single lattice
> position. Ions of *like* charge are then brought face to face,
> attraction along that plane becomes strong repulsion, and the crystal
> splits cleanly.
> 
> **Conductivity:** conduction requires mobile charges. In the solid
> the ions are fixed — charges are present but cannot move. Melting or
> dissolving frees them, and the liquid or solution conducts.
> 
> **The contrast that makes the point:** a metal, struck the same way,
> bends. Its delocalized electrons flow with the displaced layer and keep
> binding it, so no like-charge repulsion ever arises.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 4 — four questions**
>
> 1. Why does solid KBr not conduct, while molten KBr does?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. MgO is harder than NaCl. Explain Coulombically.
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Why does an ionic crystal shatter rather than bend?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. A solid dissolves in water and the solution conducts. Does that
>    prove the solid was ionic?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) ions fixed vs mobile     (b) charge product 4 vs 1    
> (c) like charges brought adjacent     (d) no — a molecular acid also
> ionizes

## Ladder 5 • Metals and alloys

`CED 2.4`

A metal is a lattice of cations in a sea of delocalized electrons. Every
metallic property follows from those electrons being free to move.

> 📘 **I do: conductivity, malleability, alloys**
>
> Explain metallic conduction and malleability, and why adding a second
> element hardens the metal.
> 
> **Conduction:** the valence electrons belong to the whole lattice,
> not to individual atoms. Under a potential difference they drift through
> the solid, carrying charge.
> 
> **Malleability:** when layers of cations slide over one another, the
> electron sea flows with them and continues to bind the lattice. Nothing
> brings like charges together, so the metal deforms instead of fracturing.
> 
> **Alloying:** atoms of a different size disrupt the regular layers.
> Layers can no longer slide smoothly past one another, so the alloy resists
> deformation — it is harder and stronger, though usually less ductile.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>
> 1. Why do metals conduct heat as well as electricity?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Brass is copper with zinc. Why is it harder than pure copper?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Why is an interstitial alloy such as steel usually harder than a
>    substitutional one?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Both metals and molten ionic solids conduct. State how the
>    *carriers* differ.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) mobile electrons carry kinetic energy     (b) zinc atoms
> disrupt the layers     (c) small atoms wedge into gaps and pin the
> layers     (d) electrons vs ions

## Ladder 6 • Drawing Lewis structures

`CED 2.5`

Count the valence electrons first — adjusting for charge — then build a
skeleton, complete the outer atoms' octets, and convert lone pairs to
multiple bonds only if the central atom is short.

> 📘 **I do: a neutral molecule and an anion**
>
> Draw CO₂ and CO₃²⁻.
> 
> **CO₂.** Valence electrons: $4 + 2(6) = \mathbf{16}$.
> Skeleton O-C-O uses 4. Completing octets on both oxygens uses the
> other 12 — but carbon then has only 4. Convert one lone pair on each
> oxygen into a bond: O=C=O, with two lone pairs on each oxygen. Every
> atom now has an octet, and the total is still 16. ✓
> 
> **CO₃²⁻.** Valence electrons:
> $4 + 3(6) + \mathbf{2} = \mathbf{24}$ — the $2-$ charge adds two.
> Carbon central, three oxygens around it. One C=O and two C-O,
> with the whole structure in brackets carrying $2-$.
> 
> **The single most common error** is forgetting to adjust for the
> charge. An anion has *more* electrons than the neutral atoms
> supply; a cation has fewer.

## Ladder 7 • Formal charge

`CED 2.6`

$$ \text{formal charge} = \text{valence electrons} -    \text{lone-pair electrons} - \tfrac{1}{2}(\text{bonding electrons}) $$

Formal charges must sum to the overall charge on the species. The best
structure keeps them small, and puts any negative charge on the most
electronegative atom.

> 📘 **I do: nitrate**
>
> Assign formal charges in NO₃-, drawn with one N=O and two
> N-O.
> 
> **Nitrogen:** 5 valence, no lone pairs, 8 bonding electrons:
> $5 - 0 - 4 = \mathbf{+1}$.
> 
> **Doubly bonded O:** 6 valence, 4 lone-pair electrons, 4 bonding:
> $6 - 4 - 2 = \mathbf{0}$.
> 
> **Each singly bonded O:** 6 valence, 6 lone-pair electrons, 2
> bonding: $6 - 6 - 1 = \mathbf{-1}$.
> 
> **Check the sum:** $(+1) + 0 + (-1) + (-1) = -1$, matching the ion's
> charge. ✓ If your formal charges do not sum to the overall
> charge, the structure is drawn wrong — go back before doing anything
> else.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 7 — four questions**
>
> 1. Formal charge on N in NH₄+ (4 bonds, no lone pairs):
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Formal charge on each O in CO₂ (each doubly bonded, two lone
>    pairs):
>    *(working space)*
> 3. In SO₄²⁻ drawn with four single bonds, the formal charge on
>    S is $+2$ and on each O is $-1$. Verify the sum.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Two structures for OCN- put the $-1$ on O in one and on N in
>    the other. Which is preferred?
>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 8 • Resonance

`CED 2.6`

When a double bond can be placed in more than one equivalent position, no
single Lewis structure is correct. The real species is the
**average** — the electrons are delocalized. It does not flip
between structures.

> 📘 **I do: nitrate's three structures**
>
> Explain why NO₃- needs three resonance structures, and predict its
> bond lengths.
> 
> **Why three:** the double bond can go to any one of the three
> oxygens, and the three choices are equivalent.
> 
> **What is real:** not one structure, and not rapid switching between
> them. The extra bonding pair is **delocalized** over all three
> N-O positions simultaneously.
> 
> **Bond lengths:** all three are **identical**, and each is
> intermediate between a normal N-O single bond and a normal N=O
> double bond. Every position has the same bond order, about
> $1\tfrac{1}{3}$.
> 
> **The experimental point:** measurement finds three equal bond
> lengths. A structure with one short and two long bonds would predict
> otherwise, which is exactly why the single Lewis structure is known to be
> inadequate.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. How many equivalent resonance structures does CO₃²⁻ have?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Predict the relative C-O bond lengths in CO₃²⁻.
>    *(working space)*
> 3. O₃ has two resonance structures. What does this predict about
>    its two O-O bonds?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is “the ion rapidly switches between structures” wrong?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) three     (b) all equal, between single and double    
> (c) identical, bond order 1.5     (d) the electrons are delocalized, not
> alternating

## Ladder 9 • VSEPR: shapes and angles

`CED 2.7`

Count the electron **domains** on the central atom — each bond
(single, double or triple) and each lone pair is one domain. Domains repel
and spread as far apart as possible. The *shape* is then named from
the positions of the **atoms** only.

> 📘 **I do: same domain count, three different shapes**
>
> Give the shape and approximate bond angle for CH₄, NH₃ and
> H₂O.
> 
> All three have **four** domains, so all three are based on a
> tetrahedron. What differs is how many domains are lone pairs.
> 
> **CH₄** — 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs: **tetrahedral**,
> 109.5 °.
> 
> **NH₃** — 3 bonds, 1 lone pair: **trigonal pyramidal**,
> about 107 °.
> 
> **H₂O** — 2 bonds, 2 lone pairs: **bent**, about
> 104.5 °.
> 
> **Why the angle shrinks:** a lone pair is held by only one nucleus,
> so it spreads out closer to the central atom and takes up more angular
> space than a bonding pair. Each lone pair squeezes the remaining bonds
> together, so the angle drops with every one added.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 9 — four questions**
>
> 1. Shape and angle of BF₃:

> 2. Shape and angle of SO₂: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Shape of SF₆ (six domains, no lone pairs):
>    *(working space)*
> 4. CO₂ has two double bonds yet only two domains. Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) trigonal planar, 120 °     (b) bent, just
> under 120 °     (c) octahedral     (d) a multiple bond

## Ladder 10 • Hybridization and sigma/pi counting

`CED 2.7`

The central atom mixes exactly as many atomic orbitals as it has domains:
2 domains sp, 3 domains sp², 4 domains sp³. Every single
bond is one sigma; every double bond is one sigma plus one pi; every
triple bond is one sigma plus two pi.

> 📘 **I do: hybridization and bond count together**
>
> For HCN, give the hybridization of carbon and count the sigma and pi
> bonds.
> 
> **Domains on carbon:** one bond to H, one triple bond to N. The
> triple bond is a single domain, so carbon has **two** domains —
> hence sp hybridized, and the molecule is linear.
> 
> **Bond count:** H-C is one sigma. C#N is one sigma plus
> *two* pi. Total: **2 sigma, 2 pi**.
> 
> **The rule that never fails:** the *first* bond between any two
> atoms is sigma; every additional bond between that same pair is pi.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 10 — four questions**

## Ladder 11 • Molecular polarity

`CED 2.1` `CED 2.7`

A molecule is polar only if it has polar bonds *and* a shape that
prevents their dipoles cancelling. Shape decides — which is why this
ladder comes after VSEPR.

> 📘 **I do: same bonds, opposite verdicts**
>
> CO₂ and H₂O both contain polar bonds. Explain why only water is
> polar.
> 
> **CO₂ is linear.** The two C=O bond dipoles are equal and
> point in exactly opposite directions, so they cancel completely. Net
> dipole zero: **nonpolar**, despite strongly polar bonds.
> 
> **H₂O is bent**, because oxygen's two lone pairs force it so.
> The two O-H dipoles no longer oppose each other and add to a net
> dipole pointing toward the oxygen: **polar**.
> 
> **The method:** identify the polar bonds, then ask whether the shape
> is symmetric enough for them to cancel. Never answer from the bonds alone.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 11 — four questions**
>
> 1. Is CCl₄ polar? Justify. 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Is CH₃Cl polar? Justify. 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Is NH₃ polar? Justify. 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. BF₃ and NH₃ both have three bonds to the central atom,
>    but only one is polar. Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) no — tetrahedral, dipoles cancel     (b) yes —
> asymmetric     (c) yes — pyramidal     (d) BF₃ is planar and
> symmetric; NH₃ has a lone pair and is pyramidal
