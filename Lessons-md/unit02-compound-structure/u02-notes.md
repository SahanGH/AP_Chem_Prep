#### INSTRUCTION A • A spectrum of bonds, not three boxes 25 min

### Types of chemical bonds `CED 2.1`

`SP 4`

Bonding is a competition for electrons, scored by
electronegativity (EN):

| **Bond type** | **Typical partners** | **Electron behavior** |
|---|---|---|
| Nonpolar covalent | two identical nonmetals | shared
  equally |
| Polar covalent | different nonmetals | shared
  unequally — partial charges $\delta^+/\delta^-$ |
| Ionic | metal + nonmetal | transferred to the
  nonmetal |
| Metallic | metal + metal | delocalized “sea” over
  cations |

> 📌 **Note**
>
> The AP exam treats these as a *continuum*: greater $\Delta$EN $\to$
> more ionic character. HF is very polar covalent; NaCl is mostly
> ionic. Don't fight over borderline cases — argue with $\Delta$EN.

Rank the bond polarity, least to most: C-H, C-F, C-O:
C-H $bond length
   (74 pm for H₂); its depth is the
   bond energy (432 kJ/mol).
- At very short distance PE shoots up:
   nucleus–nucleus repulsion dominates.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> The minimum is a *balance* of attraction and repulsion — not “where
> attraction stops.” And breaking a bond *requires* energy (climb out of
> the well); forming one *releases* it. Bond breaking is never
> exothermic.

#### INSTRUCTION A • Ionic solids and lattice energy 25 min

### The lattice and its energy `CED 2.3`

`SP 6`

An ionic solid is a 3-D lattice: each cation surrounded by anions and
vice versa — no “NaCl molecule,” just a repeating ratio. The
lattice energy is the energy change when gaseous ions assemble into
the solid; its magnitude follows Coulomb's law:
$|E| \propto$ $\dfrac{|q_1 q_2|}{r}$

Decision order for ranking:

1. Compare charge products first — they dominate.
2. Tie? Compare ionic radii — smaller ions,
   stronger lattice.

> 📘 **Worked example: rank MgO, NaF, NaCl**
>
> Charge products: MgO $(2\times2)=4$; NaF and NaCl $(1\times1)=1$. So MgO is
> far strongest. Tiebreak NaF vs NaCl: F- is smaller than Cl-, so
> NaF $>$ NaCl. Final: $\text{MgO} \gg \text{NaF} > \text{NaCl}$.

Consequences you can be asked to explain: high melting points
(strong electrostatic attractions throughout the
lattice), brittleness (shifting layers aligns
like charges $\to$ repulsion $\to$ cleave), and
conductivity only when molten or dissolved (ions must
be mobile).

#### GUIDED PRACTICE • Lattice ranking 15 min

Rank by melting point, highest first, one reason each: KCl, CaO,
KBr.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Metals and alloys 20 min

### The electron sea `CED 2.4`

`SP 1`

Metallic bonding: cations in fixed positions, valence electrons
delocalized over the whole sample. This one model explains:

- conductivity: mobile electrons carry charge
- malleability: layers slide without
   aligning like charges (contrast: ionic solids
   shatter)
- luster, thermal conduction: same mobile sea.

#### Alloys

- Substitutional: similar-size atoms replace
   host atoms (brass: Zn for Cu).
- Interstitial: small atoms fill gaps
   between hosts (steel: C in Fe). Interstitials pin sliding layers
   $\to$ harder, less malleable.

#### APPLICATION • Model-based predictions 20 min

   conducts either way. Explain both with particle-level models.
   
2. Pure iron bends easily; steel (Fe + small % C) resists. Why, at the
   particle level? 

> 📌 **Exit ticket**
>
> Predict which has the higher melting point, LiF or CsBr, with the
> two-step Coulombic argument.

> 2. Valence electrons in P: 5
> 3. Valence electrons in SO₄²⁻ total:
>    $6+4(6)+2 = 32$
> 4. Steel is which alloy type? interstitial

#### INSTRUCTION A • The five-step draw 25 min

### Lewis diagrams `CED 2.5`

`SP 1`

1. Count *total* valence electrons
   (add for anions, subtract for cations).
2. Skeleton: least electronegative atom central
   (never H); connect with single bonds.
3. Complete outer atoms' octets (H gets 2).
4. Leftovers go on the central atom.
5. Central atom short? Convert lone pairs to
   multiple bonds.

> 📘 **Worked example: CO₂ in 20 seconds**
>
> $4 + 2(6) = 16$ e$^-$. Skeleton O–C–O (12 left). Octets on the O's uses
> all 16 — C has only 4. Convert one lone pair from each O:
> O=C=O with two lone pairs on each oxygen — every atom has an
> octet, 16 e$^-$ total. Always end with the two-count check: electrons AND
> octets.

#### The exceptions worth knowing

- Odd-electron species (NO, NO₂): someone keeps an
   unpaired electron.
- Incomplete octet: BF₃ — B holds 6
   happily.
- Expanded octet: period-3+ central atoms (SF₆,
   PCl₅) may hold more than 8 — possible because $n\ge3$.

> 2. Shortest C–O bond: CO, CO₂, or CH₃OH?
>    CO
> 3. Central atom in OCS (carbon compound):

#### INSTRUCTION A • Formal charge: the tiebreaker 25 min

### Formal charge `CED 2.6`

`SP 5`

$FC = (\text{valence e}^-) -$ $(\text{nonbonding e}^-) - \tfrac12(\text{bonding e}^-)$

Selection rules — the best structure has:

formal charges closest to zero;

any negative FC on the more electronegative atom;

FCs summing to the species' overall charge
        (always — this is a check, not a preference).

> 📘 **Worked example: OCN- skeleton choice**
>
> Candidate skeletons: O–C–N vs C–O–N. For O–C–N with a C$\equiv$N
> triple: FC(O)$=-1$, FC(C)$=0$, FC(N)$=0$ — sum $-1$ ✓, minimal
> charges, negative on electronegative O. The C–O–N skeletons force larger
> charges (e.g. $+1$ on O). Carbon takes the center; formal charge proves it.

#### GUIDED PRACTICE • Compute and choose 15 min

For CO₂ drawn two ways — (i) two double bonds; (ii) single + triple:

FCs for (i): 0, 0, 0

FCs for (ii): $-1$ (single-bond O), 0, $+1$

Which is better and why?
        (i) — all formal charges zero

#### INSTRUCTION B • Resonance 20 min

### When one drawing isn't enough `CED 2.6`

`SP 1`

If the electron-pushing step could go more than one equivalent way, draw
*all* of them connected by double-headed arrows.
The molecule is the average — a resonance hybrid — not a
movie flipping between forms.

> 📘 **Worked example: nitrate**
>
> NO₃- (24 e$^-$): the double bond can sit on any of three oxygens —
> three equivalent forms. Experiment agrees: all three N–O bonds are
> *identical*, length between single and double, bond order $4/3$.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> “The bond switches back and forth” scores zero. The measured structure is
> one static average: equal bond lengths, fractional bond order, charge spread
> over equivalent atoms. Cite the *evidence* (equal lengths) when asked
> what resonance means.

#### INSTRUCTION A • Domains first, shape second 25 min

### VSEPR `CED 2.7`

`SP 1`

Electron domains (bonding groups + lone pairs) around the central atom
repel and spread to maximum separation. A double bond counts as
ONE domain.

| **Domains** | **Domain geometry** | **Angles** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 180$^\circ$ | CO₂ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 120$^\circ$ | BF₃ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5$^\circ$ | CH₄ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90/120$^\circ$ | PCl₅ |
| 6 | octahedral | 90$^\circ$ | SF₆ |

#### Lone pairs bend the name

Molecular geometry names only the *atoms*:

| **Domains (LP)** | **Molecular geometry** | **Angle** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 (1 LP) | trigonal pyramidal | $\approx107^\circ$ | NH₃ |
| 4 (2 LP) | bent | $\approx104.5^\circ$ | H₂O |
| 3 (1 LP) | bent | $

PF₃: 4 domains (1 LP) — trigonal pyramidal, $\approx107^\circ$

CO₃²⁻: 3 domains — trigonal planar, 120$^\circ$

HCN: 2 domains — linear, 180$^\circ$

SF₆: 6 domains — octahedral, 90$^\circ$

#### INSTRUCTION B • Explaining angles like a grader 20 min

### The three-sentence angle answer `CED 2.7`

`SP 6`

Full-credit template, using H₂O vs CH₄:

Count: both have 4 domains (tetrahedral
        arrangement).

Distinguish: water has 2 lone pairs; methane
        none.

Conclude: lone pairs compress the H–O–H angle from 109.5$^\circ$
        to $\approx$104.5$^\circ$.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> Naming the shape is 1 point; the *angle comparison* earns the rest.
> “Bent, about 104.5$^\circ$, because two lone pairs repel more strongly than
> bonding pairs” — practice the whole sentence.

#### APPLICATION • Shapes in contrast 20 min

`SP 1`

| **Domains** | **Hybridization** | **Leftover p orbitals for $\pi$** |
|---|---|---|

#### $\sigma$ and $\pi$

Single bond = 1 $\sigma$. Double = $\sigma + \pi$. Triple =
$\sigma + 2\pi$. Count for H₂C=CH₂:
5 $\sigma$ + 1 $\pi$.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Molecular polarity: the vector test 20 min

### Polar molecule or not `CED 2.7`

`SP 6`

Two requirements, both necessary:

polar bonds exist ($\Delta$EN $\ne$ 0), AND

the geometry does NOT cancel the bond dipoles.

> 📘 **Worked example: CO₂ vs H₂O**
>
> CO₂: two strong C=O dipoles, but linear geometry points them exactly
> opposite — vector sum zero — **nonpolar**. H₂O: two O–H
> dipoles at 104.5$^\circ$ add to a net dipole toward O — **polar**.
> Same logic kills CCl₄ (tetrahedral, cancels) and saves CHCl₃
> (one different corner, doesn't cancel).
