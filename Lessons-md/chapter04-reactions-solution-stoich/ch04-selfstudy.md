## Ladder 1 • Water, and what dissolves in it

`ZUM §4.1–4.2`

Water is a **polar** molecule — a bent frame with the oxygen end
partially negative and the hydrogen ends partially positive. Dissolving
an ionic solid is an electrical event: each ion is pried away and
surrounded by water molecules facing it charge-to-charge
(hydration).

What a dissolved substance does to conductivity sorts everything into
three bins:

| **Bin** | **Who is in it** | **In solution** |
|---|---|---|
| strong electrolyte | soluble salts; strong acids and bases | ions only — ionizes $\approx 100\%$ |
| weak electrolyte | weak acids and bases | mostly intact molecules, few ions |
| nonelectrolyte | molecular substances (sugars, alcohols) | molecules only; no ions |

> 📘 **I do: sorting three solutes**
>
> Classify NaNO₃, HC₂H₃O₂ (acetic acid), and sucrose
> (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) — and say what is actually floating in each solution.
> 
> **NaNO₃** — a soluble salt, so a **strong electrolyte**.
> The solution contains Na+ and NO₃- ions and essentially no
> intact NaNO₃ units at all.
> 
> **HC₂H₃O₂** — an acid, but not one of the six strong ones, so
> a **weak electrolyte**. About 99% of the molecules stay intact;
> roughly 1% ionize to H+ and C₂H₃O₂-. It conducts, but dimly.
> 
> **Sucrose** — molecular, neither acid nor base, so a
> **nonelectrolyte**. It dissolves beautifully (water hydrates its
> polar -OH groups) yet the solution carries no current — dissolving
> and ionizing are different events.

## Ladder 2 • Molarity, and the ions it
implies

`ZUM §4.3`

$$ M = \frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{litres of solution}} $$

For a strong electrolyte the label concentration is only the start —
the *ion* concentrations follow from the subscripts.

> 📘 **I do: a calcium nitrate solution, down to the ions**
>
> 8.20 g of Ca(NO₃)₂ ($M = 164.10\,\mathrm{g/mol}$) is
> dissolved in enough water to make 250.0 mL of solution.
> Find the molarity, then every ion concentration.
> 
> **Moles first:**
> $\dfrac{8.20}{164.10} = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}$
> 
> **Molarity:**
> $\dfrac{0.0500}{0.2500} = 0.200\,\mathrm{M}~\text{Ca(NO₃)₂}$
> 
> **Ions, by subscript:** each formula unit releases one Ca²⁺
> and *two* NO₃-:
> 
> $$ [\text{Ca²⁺}] = 0.200\,\mathrm{M} \qquad    [\text{NO₃-}] = 2 \times 0.200 = 0.400\,\mathrm{M} $$
> 
> There is no “Ca(NO₃)₂ concentration” in the beaker at all — a
> strong electrolyte exists in solution only as its ions.

## Ladder 3 • Dilution

`ZUM §4.3`

Adding water changes the volume, not the amount of solute. “Moles before
$=$ moles after” is the entire theory:

$$ M_1 V_1 = M_2 V_2 $$

> 📘 **I do: bench acid from the stock bottle**
>
> How would you prepare 500.0 mL of 1.00 M
> acetic acid from the 17.4 M glacial stock?
> 
> **Moles needed:** $0.5000 \times 1.00 = 0.500\,\mathrm{mol}$
> 
> **Stock volume carrying that many moles:**
> 
> $$ V_1 = \frac{M_2 V_2}{M_1} = \frac{(1.00)(500.0)}{17.4}        = 28.7\,\mathrm{mL} $$
> 
> **The procedure matters:** measure 28.7 mL of stock,
> then add water *to a total volume* of 500.0 mL in a
> volumetric flask — not “add 500.0 mL of water,”
> which would overshoot the volume and undershoot the concentration.

## Ladder 4 • Predicting precipitates

`ZUM §4.4–4.5`

Mix two salt solutions and the possible new pairings are found by
**swapping partners**; whether either pairing actually forms a solid
is decided by the **solubility rules**:

1. Group 1A (Na+, K+, …) and NH₄+ salts:
   soluble. Nitrates: soluble. No exceptions worth memorizing.
2. Chlorides, bromides, iodides: soluble *except* with
   Ag+, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺.
3. Sulfates: soluble *except* with Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺,
   Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺.
4. Hydroxides: insoluble *except* group 1A (and Ba²⁺,
   marginally).
5. Carbonates, phosphates, chromates, sulfides: insoluble
   *except* group 1A and NH₄+.

> 📘 **I do: potassium carbonate meets copper(II) sulfate**
>
> Will a precipitate form when K₂CO₃(aq) and CuSO₄(aq) are mixed?
> 
> **Ions in the pot:** K+, CO₃²⁻, Cu²⁺,
> SO₄²⁻.
> 
> **Swap partners — the two candidates:** K₂SO₄ and
> CuCO₃.
> 
> **Consult the rules:** K₂SO₄ — group 1A salt, soluble; stays
> dissolved. CuCO₃ — a carbonate, and copper is not group 1A or
> ammonium: **insoluble**. A pale blue-green solid forms.
> 
> K₂CO₃(aq) + CuSO₄(aq) → CuCO₃(s) + K₂SO₄(aq)
> 
> The phases *are* the answer — writing (s) on the right
> compound is the whole point of the exercise.

>

## Ladder 5 • Molecular, complete ionic, net
ionic

`ZUM §4.6`

One reaction, three languages: the **molecular equation** keeps
formulas whole; the **complete ionic** shows every strong
electrolyte as its ions; the **net ionic** deletes the
spectator ions — the ones appearing identically on both sides
— and keeps only the chemistry.

> 📘 **I do: the carbonate precipitation, three ways**
>
> For the mixture from Ladder 4:
> 
> **Molecular:**
> K₂CO₃(aq) + CuSO₄(aq) → CuCO₃(s) + K₂SO₄(aq)
> 
> **Complete ionic** — every (aq) strong electrolyte splits;
> the solid does *not*:
> 2K+ + CO₃²⁻ + Cu²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → CuCO₃(s) + 2K+ + SO₄²⁻
> 
> **Cross out what never changed** (2K+, SO₄²⁻):
> 
> $$ \boxed{\;\text{Cu²⁺(aq) + CO₃²⁻(aq) → CuCO₃(s)}\;} $$
> 
> The net ionic is the reusable fact: *any* soluble copper(II) salt
> plus *any* soluble carbonate gives this same equation. Charge check:
> $2+ + 2- = 0$ on both sides.
