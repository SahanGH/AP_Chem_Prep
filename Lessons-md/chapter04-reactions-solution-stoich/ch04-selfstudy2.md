## Ladder 1 • Precipitation stoichiometry

`ZUM §4.7`

The spine gains one new entrance ramp:

$$ \text{volume} \times M \to \text{moles}    \xrightarrow{\text{mole ratio}} \text{moles} \to \text{grams} $$

When *two* solution amounts are given, it is a limiting-reactant
problem — same as Chapter 3.

> 📘 **I do: lead iodide, the whole problem**
>
> 50.0 mL of 0.200 M Pb(NO₃)₂ is mixed with
> 75.0 mL of 0.100 M NaI. Find the mass of
> precipitate and the concentration of Pb²⁺ left in solution.
> 
> **Net ionic first** (rule 2 exception):
> Pb²⁺ + 2I- → PbI₂(s), $M_{\text{PbI₂}} = 461.0\,\mathrm{g/mol}$
> 
> **Moles of each reactant:**
> 
> $$ \text{Pb²⁺}: 0.0500 \times 0.200 = 0.0100\,\mathrm{mol} \qquad    \text{I-}: 0.0750 \times 0.100 = 0.00750\,\mathrm{mol} $$
> 
> **Limiting?** The lead present would need $2 \times 0.0100 = 0.0200\,\mathrm{mol}$ of iodide; only 0.00750 is there, so **I-
> is limiting**.
> 
> **Precipitate** (from the limiting reactant only):
> 
> $$ 0.00750 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 0.00375\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{PbI₂}    \;\Rightarrow\; 0.00375 \times 461.0 = \mathbf{1.73~g} $$
> 
> **Leftover Pb²⁺** — and here is the solution-specific step:
> the total volume is now $50.0 + 75.0 = 125.0\,\mathrm{mL}$:
> 
> $$ n = 0.0100 - 0.00375 = 0.00625\,\mathrm{mol} \qquad    [\text{Pb²⁺}] = \frac{0.00625}{0.1250} = \mathbf{0.0500~M} $$
> 
> Dividing by the *combined* volume, not the original 50.0 mL, is the
> step exams test.

## Ladder 2 • Neutralization stoichiometry

`ZUM §4.8`

Strong acid meets strong base and the net ionic equation is always the
same: H+(aq) + OH-(aq) → H₂O(l). The only bookkeeping trap is a
formula that delivers more than one H+ or OH-.

> 📘 **I do: how much base kills the acid**
>
> What volume of 0.150 M NaOH neutralizes
> 35.0 mL of 0.0900 M H₂SO₄?
> 
> **Moles of acid, then of H+** — sulfuric acid is diprotic:
> 
> $$ 0.0350 \times 0.0900 = 0.00315\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{H₂SO₄}    \;\Rightarrow\; 2 \times 0.00315 = 0.00630\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{H+} $$
> 
> **OH- required** (1:1 with H+): 0.00630 mol
> 
> **Volume of base:**
> 
> $$ V = \frac{0.00630}{0.150} = 0.0420\,\mathrm{L} = \mathbf{42.0~mL} $$
> 
> The factor of 2 is the whole exam question. Skip it and every downstream
> number is exactly half right — which is to say, wrong.

>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 3 • Titration

`ZUM §4.8`

A titration is a neutralization run *backward*: the known solution
(the titrant) is added until the indicator changes, and the measured
volume reveals the unknown. Vocabulary that gets tested:
equivalence point (moles of H+ $=$ moles of OH-, set by
stoichiometry) versus endpoint (where the indicator changes —
chosen to coincide).

> 📘 **I do: standardizing a sodium hydroxide solution**
>
> NaOH solutions cannot be made to a precise concentration by weighing
> (the pellets absorb water), so they are *standardized* against a
> weighable acid, potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHP,
> $M = 204.22\,\mathrm{g/mol}$, one acidic H). A 0.6745 g
> KHP sample requires 27.85 mL of the NaOH solution.
> Find the base's molarity.
> 
> **Moles of acid, from the balance:**
> 
> $$ \frac{0.6745}{204.22} = 3.303e-3\,\mathrm{mol}~\text{KHP} $$
> 
> **At the endpoint, moles of NaOH delivered $=$ moles of KHP**
> (1:1, one acidic H):
> 
> $$ M_{\text{NaOH}} = \frac{3.303\times10^{-3}}{0.02785}    = \mathbf{0.1186~M} $$
> 
> The mass was weighed to four figures and the volume read to four, so the
> answer honestly carries four.

## Ladder 4 • Oxidation states; spotting
redox

`ZUM §4.9`

Oxidation states are electron bookkeeping. The rules, in priority order:
element 0; monatomic ion $=$ its charge; F $-1$; O $-2$ (except
peroxides); H $+1$ with nonmetals, $-1$ with metals; everything must sum
to the species' overall charge. Then:

> 
**OIL RIG** — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of
electrons). The substance *containing* the oxidized atom is the
**reducing agent**, and vice versa.

> 📘 **I do: assigning states, then judging a reaction**
>
> **(a) Chromium in K₂Cr₂O₇:** K is $+1$ ($\times 2 = +2$), O is
> $-2$ ($\times 7 = -14$); the compound is neutral, so
> $2x + 2 - 14 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{+6}$.
> 
> **(b) Sulfur in SO₃²⁻:** O contributes $-6$; the ion carries
> $2-$, so $x - 6 = -2 \Rightarrow x = \mathbf{+4}$.
> 
> **(c) Is Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s) redox?**
> Zn: $0 \to +2$ — lost electrons, **oxidized**, so zinc metal is
> the **reducing agent**. Cu: $+2 \to 0$ — gained electrons,
> **reduced**, so CuSO₄ (the Cu²⁺ in it) is the
> **oxidizing agent**. S and O never change ($+6$, $-2$): sulfate is
> along for the ride.

## Ladder 5 • Balancing redox; a redox
titration

`ZUM §4.10–4.11`

Balancing by **oxidation states**: find the per-atom electron loss
and gain, choose coefficients so total loss $=$ total gain, then finish
the non-redox atoms by inspection.

> 📘 **I do: copper dissolving in dilute nitric acid**
>
> $$ \text{Cu(s) + HNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + NO(g) + H₂O(l)}    \qquad\text{(unbalanced)} $$
> 
> **Find the changes:** Cu $0 \to +2$: loses 2. N $+5 \to +2$ (in the
> NO only): gains 3.
> 
> **Equalize electrons:** $3 \times \text{Cu}$ loses 6;
> $2 \times \text{N}$ gains 6:
> 3Cu + HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + H₂O
> 
> **Finish by inspection.** The right side now holds $6 + 2 = 8$ N,
> so 8 HNO₃; 8 H makes 4 H₂O:
> 
> $$ \boxed{\;\text{3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O}\;} $$
> 
> **Count everything:** Cu $3=3$; N $8 = 6+2$; H $8 = 8$; O
> $24 = 18 + 2 + 4$. Note only 2 of the 8 nitrogens changed state — the
> other 6 became spectator nitrate.
