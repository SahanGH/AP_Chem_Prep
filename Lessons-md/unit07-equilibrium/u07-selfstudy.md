> 📌 **How to use these notes — read this first**
>
> Each skill is a **ladder**: a worked example, then **four**
> YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four
> before checking anything.
> 
> **The distinction this whole unit rests on.** $K$ is a
> **constant** at a given temperature; $Q$ is the **same
> expression evaluated right now**, at whatever concentrations happen to
> exist. Comparing the two tells you which way the reaction will move. Every
> Le Ch\^atelier question is really a $Q$-versus-$K$ question underneath.
> 
> **The habit that decides this unit.** Only *temperature* changes
> the value of $K$. Adding reactant, removing product, changing the volume
> or adding a catalyst all leave $K$ exactly where it was — they move the
> system along a fixed $K$, they do not move $K$.
> 
> **One scope note.** Converting between $K_c$ and $K_p$ is
> **excluded** by the CED. The conceptual difference between them is
> examinable; the $K_p = K_c(RT)^{\Delta n}$ conversion is not, and does not
> appear here.

## Ladder 1 • What equilibrium is

`CED 7.1` `CED 7.2`

Equilibrium is *dynamic*: both reactions continue, at equal rates.
Concentrations stop changing; the molecules do not stop reacting.

> 📘 **I do: what stops and what does not**
>
> Describe what is happening at equilibrium, macroscopically and at the
> particle level.
> 
> **Macroscopically: nothing changes.** Concentrations, colour and
> pressure are all constant. On a concentration-versus-time graph, every
> curve has flattened.
> 
> **At the particle level: everything continues.** The forward reaction
> is still converting reactants to products, and the reverse is still
> converting them back — at exactly the *same rate*. Every molecule
> consumed one way is replaced by one made the other way.
> 
> **Why the graph cannot prove this.** If the reaction had simply
> stopped, the curves would also flatten. The evidence for dynamic behaviour
> comes from experiments such as isotopic labelling, where a labelled
> molecule placed on one side later appears on both.
> 
> **Also note: reactants are still present.** A reaction at equilibrium
> has not gone to completion, and never will — which is why the arrow is
> drawn both ways.

>

>    *(working space)*

>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 2 • Writing equilibrium expressions

`CED 7.3`

Products over reactants, each raised to its coefficient. Pure solids and
pure liquids are omitted.

> 📘 **I do: two expressions, one omission**
>
> Write $K$ for
> N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g) and for
> CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g).
> 
> **First:** all three are gases, so all appear, each raised to its
> coefficient.
> 
> $$ K = \frac{[\text{NH₃}]^2}{[\text{N₂}][\text{H₂}]^3} $$
> 
> **Second:** both CaCO₃ and CaO are **pure solids** and
> are omitted.
> 
> $$ K = [\text{CO₂}] $$
> 
> **Why solids drop out.** A pure solid has a fixed density and
> composition, so its “concentration” does not change no matter how much
> is present. Adding more solid cannot shift the equilibrium, so it is
> absorbed into the constant rather than written.
> 
> **The consequence worth noticing:** in the second reaction, the
> pressure of CO₂ above the solid at a given temperature is fixed by
> $K$ alone — however much or little CaCO₃ is in the flask.

## Ladder 3 • Calculating K from equilibrium concentrations

`CED 7.4`

If you are handed the equilibrium concentrations, substitute them
straight in. The only difficulty is remembering the exponents.

> 📘 **I do: substitution**
>
> At equilibrium, H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI has
> $[\text{HI}] = 0.50\,\mathrm{M}$ and
> $[\text{H₂}] = [\text{I₂}] = 0.10\,\mathrm{M}$. Find $K$.
> 
> $$ K = \frac{[\text{HI}]^2}{[\text{H₂}][\text{I₂}]}      = \frac{(0.50)^2}{(0.10)(0.10)}      = \frac{0.25}{0.010} = \mathbf{25} $$
> 
> **The exponent is the whole difficulty.** Forgetting to square
> HI gives 50 — exactly double, and a common wrong answer.
> 
> **Sense check:** $K = 25$ is comfortably greater than 1, so products
> should dominate at equilibrium. Indeed 0.50 M of HI
> against 0.10 M of each reactant. ✓
> 
> **$K$ has no units on this exam.** Report it as a bare number.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>
> 1. $[\text{NH₃}] = 0.40$, $[\text{N₂}] = 0.20$, $[\text{H₂}] = 0.20$ for
>    N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃. Find $K$.
>    *(working space)*
> 2. For 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄ with $[\text{N₂O₄}] = 0.60$ and

## Ladder 4 • What the magnitude of K tells you

`CED 7.5`

$K$ measures *extent* — how far the reaction proceeds — and
nothing else. It says nothing about rate.

> 📘 **I do: reading three values**
>
> Interpret $K = 1\times10^{10}$, $K = 1$ and
> $K = 1\times10^{-8}$.
> 
> **$K = 10^{10}$:** equilibrium lies far to the **right**. At
> equilibrium the mixture is essentially all products, with only traces of
> reactant. Such a reaction is often said to “go to completion”, though
> strictly a little reactant always remains.
> 
> **$K = 1$:** neither side dominates — comparable amounts of both are
> present.
> 
> **$K = 10^{-8}$:** far to the **left**. Almost nothing reacts;
> the mixture is essentially all reactant.
> 
> **What none of these tells you: the rate.** A reaction with
> $K = 10^{10}$ may be immeasurably slow if its activation energy is large
> — the conversion of diamond to graphite is exactly that. Extent and rate
> are independent properties.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 4 — four questions**
>
> 1. $K = 5\times10^{6}$. Which side is favoured?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. $K = 3\times10^{-5}$. Which side? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Does a large $K$ mean a fast reaction? 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Two reactions have $K = 100$ and $K = 0.01$. How are they
>    related?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) products     (b) reactants     (c) no     (d) they
> are the reverse of each other

## Ladder 5 • Q versus K

`CED 7.3` `CED 7.8`

$Q$ has exactly the same form as $K$ but is evaluated at the
*current* concentrations. Comparing them predicts the direction of
change.

> 📘 **I do: predicting the shift**
>
> For H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI with $K = 25$, a mixture holds
> $[\text{HI}] = 0.20$, $[\text{H₂}] = 0.30$,
> $[\text{I₂}] = 0.30$ M. Which way does it move?
> 
> $$ Q = \frac{(0.20)^2}{(0.30)(0.30)} = \frac{0.040}{0.090}    = \mathbf{0.44} $$
> 
> **Compare:** $Q = 0.44$ is much less than $K = 25$. There is too
> little product relative to equilibrium, so the reaction proceeds
> **forward**, to the right, making more HI until $Q$ climbs to
> 25.
> 
> **The three cases, once and for all:**
> $Q  K$ shift left (make reactants).
> 
> **Why this is the master tool:** every Le Ch\^atelier prediction is
> this comparison in disguise. Adding a reactant lowers $Q$; the system
> responds by moving right. Nothing else is going on.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>
> 1. $Q = 0.5$, $K = 2.0$. Which way? 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. $Q = 150$, $K = 25$. Which way? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. $Q = K$. What happens? 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Adding more reactant to a mixture at equilibrium: what happens to
>    $Q$, and then to the system?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) right     (b) left     (c) nothing — already at
> equilibrium     (d) $Q$ falls; the system shifts right

## Ladder 6 • ICE tables

`CED 7.7`

Initial, Change, Equilibrium. The change row carries the
**coefficients** as multipliers of $x$ — that is where most errors
enter.

> 📘 **I do: setting one up correctly**
>
> 0.500 M PCl₅ is placed in a flask and dissociates by
> PCl₅ ⇌ PCl₃ + Cl₂. Build the ICE table.
> 
> |  | PCl₅ | PCl₃ | Cl₂ |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Initial | 0.500 | 0 | 0 |
> | Change | $-x$ | $+x$ | $+x$ |
> | Equilibrium | $0.500-x$ | $x$ | $x$ |

**Here every coefficient is 1**, so every change is $\pm x$.

**But if the equation were H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI**, the change row
would read $-x$, $-x$, $+\mathbf{2}x$ — the coefficient 2 multiplies.
Writing $+x$ for HI is the single commonest ICE error and corrupts
every later line.

**Reading the table:** the equilibrium row is what goes into $K$.
Nothing else does.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 6 — four questions**
>
> 1. For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ starting from 1.0 M of each
>    reactant, write the change row.
>    *(working space)*
> 2. For 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄ starting from 0.80 M NO₂,
>    write the equilibrium row.
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which row is substituted into the $K$ expression?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why must coefficients appear in the change row?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) $-x$, $-3x$, $+2x$     (b) $0.80 - 2x$ and $x$    
> (c) the equilibrium row     (d) species react in fixed proportions

## Ladder 7 • The 5% approximation

`CED 7.7`

When $K$ is very small, $x$ is tiny compared with the initial
concentration and the subtraction can be dropped. **Always test the
assumption afterwards.**

> 📘 **I do: assume, solve, then check**
>
> For A ⇌ B + C with $K = 1.0\times10^{-5}$ and
> $[\text{A}]_0 = 0.100\,\mathrm{M}$, find $x$.
> 
> **Set up:**
> $\dfrac{x^2}{0.100 - x} = 1.0\times10^{-5}$.
> 
> **Assume $x \ll 0.100$**, so the denominator is about 0.100:
> 
> $$ x = \sqrt{(1.0\times10^{-5})(0.100)} = \mathbf{1.0\times10^{-3}} $$
> 
> **Now test it.** As a fraction of the initial concentration,
> $1.0\times10^{-3}/0.100 = 1.0\,\mathrm{\%}$ — comfortably below the
> 5 % threshold, so the assumption is **valid** and no
> quadratic is needed.
> 
> **What “valid” means:** solving the quadratic properly gives
> $x = 9.95\times10^{-4}$, differing by 0.5 % — far less than
> the precision of the data.
> 
> **When it fails,** as it does whenever $K$ is not small, you must use
> the quadratic. Testing takes ten seconds; skipping the test is what costs
> marks.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 7 — four questions**
>
> 1. What is the usual threshold for accepting the approximation?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. $x = 0.004$ from an initial 0.200 M. Is it valid?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. $x = 0.03$ from an initial 0.10 M. Is it valid?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. For which kind of $K$ is the approximation generally safe?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 5 %     (b) yes — 2 %    
> (c) no — 30 %     (d) very small $K$

## Ladder 8 • Le Ch\^atelier: concentration

`CED 7.9` `CED 7.10`

Add something and the system consumes it; remove something and the system
replaces it. $K$ does not change.

> 📘 **I do: four changes to one equilibrium**
>
> For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, state the effect of: adding N₂; removing
> NH₃; adding NH₃; adding argon at constant volume.
> 
> **Adding N₂:** shifts **right**. The system consumes some
> of the added reactant. ($Q$ falls below $K$.)
> 
> **Removing NH₃:** shifts **right**. The system replaces
> what was taken. ($Q$ falls.)
> 
> **Adding NH₃:** shifts **left**. ($Q$ rises above $K$.)
> 
> **Adding argon at constant volume:** **no shift**. Argon takes
> no part in the reaction and does not change the concentration of anything
> that does. The total pressure rises, but every partial pressure that
> matters is unchanged, so $Q$ is unchanged.
> 
> **Throughout, $K$ is constant** — only the position along it moved.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. For 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, effect of adding O₂?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Effect of removing SO₃? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Effect of adding helium at constant volume?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Does adding a reactant change $K$? 
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) right     (b) right     (c) none     (d) no

## Ladder 9 • Le Ch\^atelier: volume and pressure

`CED 7.9` `CED 7.10`

Compressing a gaseous equilibrium shifts it toward the side with
*fewer* moles of gas. Count the gas coefficients on each side —
solids and liquids do not count.

> 📘 **I do: counting moles of gas**
>
> Predict the effect of halving the volume for:
> (i) N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃;
> (ii) H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI.
> 
> **(i)** Left has $1 + 3 = 4$ moles of gas; right has 2. Compressing
> raises all pressures, and the system relieves that by moving to the side
> with fewer particles: **shifts right**.
> 
> **(ii)** Left has $1 + 1 = 2$; right has 2. **Equal**, so
> compression has **no effect** on the position — there is no side
> with fewer moles to move toward.
> 
> **The rule in one line:** equal moles of gas on both sides means
> volume changes do nothing.
> 
> **And note what a volume change never does:** it does not alter $K$.
> Only temperature does that.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 9 — four questions**
>
> 1. Effect of compressing 2NO₂ ⇌ N₂O₄?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Effect of expanding that same system? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Effect of compressing CO(g) + H₂O(g) ⇌ CO₂(g) + H₂(g)?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. In CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g), how many moles of gas are on
>    each side?

## Ladder 10 • Le Ch\^atelier: temperature and catalysts

`CED 7.9`

Temperature is the *only* change that alters $K$. A catalyst alters
neither $K$ nor the position.

> 📘 **I do: heat as a participant**
>
> For the exothermic 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, $\Delta H  effect of raising the temperature, and of adding a catalyst.
> 
> **Treat heat as a product** for an exothermic reaction:
> 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ + heat.
> 
> **Raising the temperature** adds heat, so the system shifts to
> consume it — to the **left**. Less SO₃ at equilibrium.
> 
> **And $K$ itself decreases.** This is the one case where the constant
> genuinely changes value, because $K$ is defined at a temperature.
> 
> **Adding a catalyst: nothing changes.** It lowers the activation
> energy of the forward and reverse reactions equally, so both rates rise in
> the same proportion. Equilibrium arrives sooner and lies in exactly the
> same place.
> 
> **The industrial tension this creates:** a lower temperature would
> give more SO₃, but too slowly to be useful. The Contact process
> compromises — moderate temperature plus a catalyst to make that
> temperature fast enough.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 10 — four questions**

>    *(working space)*

> 4. Why does a catalyst not shift the position?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) shifts right; $K$ increases     (b) temperature only

## Ladder 11 • Solubility equilibria and *K*\textsubscriptsp

`CED 7.11`

$K_{sp}$ is just an equilibrium constant for a dissolving solid. The solid
is omitted, and the stoichiometric coefficients become exponents — and
multipliers of $s$.

> 📘 **I do: two different stoichiometries**
>
> Find the molar solubility of AgCl
> ($K_{sp} = 1.8\times10^{-10}$) and of PbI₂
> ($K_{sp} = 7.1\times10^{-9}$).
> 
> **AgCl:** AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag+ + Cl⁻, a $1:1$ dissolution, so
> $[\text{Ag+}] = [\text{Cl-}] = s$.
> 
> $$ K_{sp} = s^2 \;\Rightarrow\; s = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-10}}    = \mathbf{1.3\times10^{-5}~M} $$
> 
> **PbI₂:** PbI₂(s) ⇌ Pb²⁺ + 2I⁻, so
> $[\text{Pb²⁺}] = s$ but $[\text{I-}] = \mathbf{2}s$.
> 
> $$ K_{sp} = (s)(2s)^2 = 4s^3 = 7.1\times10^{-9} $$
> 
> $$ s^3 = 1.775\times10^{-9} \;\Rightarrow\;    s = \mathbf{1.2\times10^{-3}~M} $$
> 
> **The factor of 2 is the entire difficulty.** Writing
> $K_{sp} = s^3$ instead of $4s^3$ gives 1.9e-3 — wrong, and a very
> common answer.
> 
> **A warning about comparing:** PbI₂ has the smaller $K_{sp}$ yet
> is nearly a hundred times *more* soluble than AgCl. $K_{sp}$
> values may only be compared directly between salts of the same
> stoichiometric type.

>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why can $K_{sp}$ values not always be compared directly?
>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 12 • The common-ion effect

`CED 7.12`

Adding an ion that already appears in the equilibrium pushes it backwards.
It is Le Ch\^atelier applied to dissolving.

> 📘 **I do: how much less soluble?**
>
> AgCl has $K_{sp} = 1.8\times10^{-10}$. Compare its solubility in
> pure water with that in 0.10 M NaCl.
> 
> **In pure water:**
> $s = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-10}} = 1.3e-5\,\mathrm{M}$.
> 
> **In 0.10 M NaCl:** chloride is already present at
> 0.10 M, overwhelmingly more than the dissolution could supply,
> so $[\text{Cl-}] \approx 0.10$.
> 
> $$ K_{sp} = [\text{Ag+}](0.10) \;\Rightarrow\;    [\text{Ag+}] = s = \frac{1.8\times10^{-10}}{0.10}    = \mathbf{1.8\times10^{-9}~M} $$
> 
> **That is about 7000 times less soluble.** The common ion suppresses
> dissolution dramatically.
> 
> **And $K_{sp}$ is unchanged throughout.** What changed is how the
> fixed product of concentrations is distributed between the two ions —
> much more chloride, so far less silver.
