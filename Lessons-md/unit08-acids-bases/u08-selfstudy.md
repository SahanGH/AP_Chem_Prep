> 📌 **How to use these notes — read this first**
>
> Each skill is a **ladder**: a worked example, then **four**
> YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four
> before checking anything.
> 
> **This unit is worth 11 %–15 % of the AP
> exam**, second only to Unit 3.
> 
> **The distinction that causes the most lost marks.**
> **Strength** is about the *extent* of ionization —
> a strong acid ionizes essentially completely. **Concentration** is
> about how much was dissolved. They are independent: a dilute strong acid
> and a concentrated weak acid are both entirely ordinary things.
> 
> **The habit that decides this unit.** Before calculating any pH, ask
> *strong or weak?* A strong acid gives $[\text{H+}]$ directly from the
> label. A weak one requires an equilibrium calculation with $K_a$. Treating
> a weak acid as strong is the single commonest error here.
> 
> $K_w = 1.0\times10^{-14}$ at 25 °C.
> 
> **One scope note.** The CED excludes deriving
> Henderson–Hasselbalch, computing the pH change on adding acid or base to
> a buffer, and species computations on polyprotic titration curves. None is
> asked here.

## Ladder 1 • Brønsted–Lowry pairs

`CED 8.1`

An acid donates a proton; a base accepts one. A conjugate pair differs by
**exactly one** H+.

> 📘 **I do: finding both pairs**
>
> Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in
> HCO₃⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ + OH⁻.
> 
> **Follow the protons.** HCO₃- *gains* one to become
> H₂CO₃: it acted as a **base**, and H₂CO₃ is its conjugate
> acid. That is pair one.
> 
> H₂O *loses* one to become OH-: it acted as an
> **acid**, and OH- is its conjugate base. That is pair two.
> 
> **Pairs: H₂CO₃/HCO₃- and H₂O/OH-.**
> 
> **The trap:** pairing HCO₃- with OH-. They are on opposite
> sides and look like a pair, but they differ by more than one proton and
> are not related as conjugates. Always check the one-proton rule.
> 
> **Amphiprotic species** such as HCO₃- can act either way —
> here it is a base, but with a stronger acid present it would donate
> instead.

>

>    *(working space)*

>    *(working space)*
> 4. Write an equation showing HCO₃- acting as an *acid*.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) HSO₄-     (b) NH₄+     (c)
> CH₃COOH/CH₃COO- and H₃O+/H₂O

## Ladder 2 • Strong versus weak

`CED 8.1`

Strong means essentially complete ionization — write a single arrow.
Weak means partial — write a double arrow, and expect an equilibrium
calculation.

> 📘 **I do: what is in the beaker**
>
> Compare 0.10 M HCl with 0.10 M
> CH₃COOH in terms of the particles present and the conductivity.
> 
> **HCl, strong:** essentially every molecule ionizes.
> HCl → H+ + Cl⁻. The solution contains 0.10 M
> H+, 0.10 M Cl- and almost no intact HCl. It
> conducts strongly.
> 
> **CH₃COOH, weak:** only about 1 % ionizes.
> CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H+. The solution is mostly *intact
> acetic acid molecules*, with roughly 0.001 M of each ion. It
> conducts weakly.
> 
> **Same concentration, hundred-fold difference in $[\text{H+}]$** —
> and about two pH units between them. Strength, not concentration, made
> that difference.
> 
> **On a particulate drawing:** the weak acid must show mostly whole
> molecules. Drawing it fully dissociated is the standard error.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 2 — four questions**
>
> 1. Which conducts better, 0.1 M HCl or
>    0.1 M HF? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Can a weak acid solution be more concentrated than a strong acid
>    solution?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which arrow does a weak acid take, and why?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Could a concentrated weak acid have a lower pH than a dilute
>    strong acid?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) HCl — more mobile ions     (b) yes    
> (c) double — partial ionization     (d) yes

## Ladder 3 • pH, pOH and *K*\textsubscriptw

`CED 8.2`

$\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H+}]$, $\text{pOH} = -\log[\text{OH-}]$, and at
25 °C they sum to 14.00.

> 📘 **I do: all four quantities from one**
>
> A solution has $[\text{H+}] = 2.5e-5\,\mathrm{M}$. Find pH, pOH and
> $[\text{OH-}]$.
> 
> $$ \text{pH} = -\log(2.5\times10^{-5}) = \mathbf{4.60} $$
> 
> $$ \text{pOH} = 14.00 - 4.60 = \mathbf{9.40} $$
> 
> $$ [\text{OH-}] = \frac{1.0\times10^{-14}}{2.5\times10^{-5}}    = \mathbf{4.0\times10^{-10}~M} $$
> 
> **Sense check:** pH below 7 means acidic, so $[\text{H+}]$ should
> exceed $[\text{OH-}]$. It does, by five orders of magnitude. ✓
> 
> **Significant figures in a logarithm:** only the digits *after*
> the decimal point count. $[\text{H+}]$ has two significant figures, so the
> pH is quoted to two decimal places — 4.60, not 4.6 or
> 4.602.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>
> 1. pH when $[\text{H+}] = 1.0e-3\,\mathrm{M}$:
>    *(working space)*
> 2. $[\text{OH-}]$ when pH $= 9.00$: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. pOH when $[\text{H+}] = 0.020\,\mathrm{M}$:
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Is a solution of pH 6.5 at 25 °C acidic, basic
>    or neutral?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 3.00     (b) 1.0e-5 M    
> (c) 12.30     (d) very slightly acidic

## Ladder 4 • pH of strong acids and bases

`CED 8.2`

For a strong acid or base, the concentration *is* the ion
concentration — but watch for a formula supplying more than one ion.

> 📘 **I do: watch the subscript**
>
> Find the pH of 0.0050 M Ba(OH)₂, assuming complete
> dissociation.
> 
> **Count the hydroxides.** Each formula unit gives **two**:
> 
> $$ [\text{OH-}] = 2(0.0050) = 0.010\,\mathrm{M} $$
> 
> $$ \text{pOH} = -\log(0.010) = 2.00 \qquad    \text{pH} = 14.00 - 2.00 = \mathbf{12.00} $$
> 
> **Forgetting the factor of 2** gives pOH 2.30 and pH
> 11.70 — close enough to look plausible, which is exactly what makes
> it dangerous.
> 
> **The sanity check that catches sign errors:** a group 2 hydroxide is
> a strong base, so the pH must be well above 7. Any answer below 7 is
> immediately wrong, and usually means pH and pOH were interchanged.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 4 — four questions**
>
> 1. pH of 0.010 M HCl: 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. pH of 0.010 M NaOH: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. pH of 0.0025 M Ca(OH)₂: 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is the concentration used directly for a strong acid but not
>    for a weak one?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 2.00     (b) 12.00     (c) 11.70
>     (d) the weak acid only partly ionizes

## Ladder 5 • pH of a weak acid

`CED 8.3`

Set up the $K_a$ equilibrium, assume $x$ is small, solve — then
**test** the assumption.

> 📘 **I do: the full routine**
>
> Find the pH of 0.200 M acetic acid,
> $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$.
> 
> **Equilibrium:**
> CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H+, with $x = [\text{H+}]$ formed.
> 
> $$ K_a = \frac{x^2}{0.200 - x} = 1.8\times10^{-5} $$
> 
> **Assume $x \ll 0.200$:**
> 
> $$ x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.200)} = 1.90e-3~\text{M} $$
> 
> **Test it:** $1.90\times10^{-3}/0.200 = 0.95\,\mathrm{\%}$, well
> under 5 %. **Valid** — no quadratic needed.
> 
> $$ \text{pH} = -\log(1.897\times10^{-3}) = \mathbf{2.72} $$
> 
> **Compare with a strong acid** at the same concentration: pH
> 0.70. The weak acid sits two units higher because so little of it
> ionized.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>
> 1. Find $[\text{H+}]$ for 0.100 M of an acid with
>    $K_a = 4.0\times10^{-6}$.
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Find the pH in (a). 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Check whether the approximation was valid in (a).
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is $[\text{H+}]$ far less than the acid's concentration?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 6.3e-4 M     (b) 3.20    
> (c) yes — 0.63 %     (d) only a small fraction ionizes

## Ladder 6 • Percent ionization

`CED 8.3`

$$ \%\text{ ionization} = \frac{[\text{H+}]_{\text{eq}}}    {[\text{acid}]_{\text{initial}}} \times 100 $$

It *rises* on dilution, even though the pH rises too.

> 📘 **I do: the counter-intuitive result**
>
> For the 0.200 M acetic acid above, find the percent ionization
> and predict what happens on dilution.
> 
> $$ \frac{1.90\times10^{-3}}{0.200}\times100 = \mathbf{0.95\%} $$
> 
> **On dilution the percent ionization increases.** $K_a$ is fixed, and
> $x \approx \sqrt{K_a C}$ — so $x$ falls only as the *square root*
> of the concentration, while the concentration itself falls linearly. The
> *ratio* therefore grows.
> 
> **Concretely:** at 0.0200 M the ionization is about
> 3.0 % — three times the fraction, even though $[\text{H+}]$
> itself has fallen and the pH has risen.
> 
> **Two things moving opposite ways.** Diluting makes the solution
> *less acidic* (higher pH) but the acid *more ionized* (higher
> percentage). Both are true, and confusing them is a common error.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 6 — four questions**
>
> 1. $[\text{H+}] = 2.0e-3\,\mathrm{M}$ from 0.50 M acid.
>    Percent ionization?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. On dilution, does percent ionization rise or fall?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. On dilution, does the pH rise or fall? 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Explain how both answers can be true at once.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 0.40 %     (b) rises     (c) rises    
> (d) $[\text{H+}]$ falls, but not as fast as the concentration

## Ladder 7 • p*K*\textsubscripta and acid strength

`CED 8.7`

$\mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a$. A *smaller* $\mathrm{p}K_a$ means a
*stronger* acid — the logarithm reverses the order.

> 📘 **I do: converting and comparing**
>
> Acid A has $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$; acid B has
> $\mathrm{p}K_a = 3.14$. Which is stronger?
> 
> **Put them on the same scale.**
> $\mathrm{p}K_a(\text{A}) = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = \mathbf{4.74}$.
> 
> **Compare:** B's 3.14 is *smaller*, so **B is the
> stronger acid**.
> 
> **Check by converting back:**
> $K_a(\text{B}) = 10^{-3.14} = 7.2\times10^{-4}$, which is indeed larger
> than $1.8\times10^{-5}$ — about forty times. ✓
> 
> **Why the reversal:** the minus sign in the definition. Every factor
> of ten *up* in $K_a$ is one unit *down* in $\mathrm{p}K_a$. It
> works exactly as pH does: lower pH means more acidic.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 7 — four questions**
>
> 1. $\mathrm{p}K_a$ for $K_a = 1.0\times10^{-7}$:
>    *(working space)*
> 2. $K_a$ for $\mathrm{p}K_a = 2.00$: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which is stronger, $\mathrm{p}K_a$ 4.2 or 9.1?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Two acids differ by 2 in $\mathrm{p}K_a$. How do their $K_a$
>    values compare?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 7.00     (b) 1.0e-2      (c) the
> 4.2 one     (d) a factor of 100

## Ladder 8 • Structure and acid strength

`CED 8.6`

Two separate trends, governed by two different factors — and one of them
runs opposite to what electronegativity alone would suggest.

> 📘 **I do: the two trends**
>
> Explain why acid strength increases along HF $ HBr $ $ 
> **Down the halogens: bond strength decides.** The halogen gets
> larger, so the H-X bond is longer and weaker and the proton is
> released more easily. Electronegativity *falls* down this group, so
> it would predict the opposite order — it is not the controlling factor
> here.
> 
> **Across the oxoacids: electron withdrawal decides.** Each added
> oxygen is highly electronegative and pulls electron density away from the
> O-H bond, weakening it. The extra oxygens also spread the negative
> charge of the resulting anion over more atoms, so the anion accommodates
> it better and the ionization proceeds further.
> 
> **The lesson:** identify which factor is varying before reaching for
> a rule. Electronegativity, bond strength and charge delocalization each
> dominate in different comparisons.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. Stronger acid, HBr or HCl? Why? 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Stronger acid, HClO₄ or HClO? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Stronger acid, HBrO₃ or HClO₃? 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why does electronegativity fail to explain the hydrohalic trend?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) HBr — weaker bond     (b) HClO₄ — more
> electron withdrawal     (c) HClO₃ — Cl more electronegative
>     (d) it predicts the reverse order

## Ladder 9 • Acid–base properties of salts

`CED 8.7`

Look at where each ion came from. An ion from a *weak* parent reacts
with water; an ion from a *strong* parent does not.

> 📘 **I do: three salts, three verdicts**
>
> Predict whether solutions of NaCl, NH₄Cl and
> NaCH₃COO are acidic, basic or neutral.
> 
> **NaCl: neutral.** Na+ comes from the strong base
> NaOH; Cl- from the strong acid HCl. Both are the conjugates
> of *strong* parents, so neither reacts appreciably with water.
> 
> **NH₄Cl: acidic.** NH₄+ is the conjugate acid of the weak
> base ammonia, so it donates a proton to water:
> NH₄+ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O+. Cl- is inert.
> 
> **NaCH₃COO: basic.** Acetate is the conjugate base of a weak
> acid, so it accepts a proton from water:
> CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻. Na+ is inert.
> 
> **The rule in one line:** the conjugate of a *weak* species is
> itself reactive; the conjugate of a *strong* species is not.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 9 — four questions**
>
> 1. Is KNO₃ acidic, basic or neutral? 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Is NaF acidic, basic or neutral? Why? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Is NH₄NO₃ acidic, basic or neutral? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is Cl- inert in water while F- is not?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) neutral     (b) basic — F- is from weak HF
>     (c) acidic — NH₄+ donates     (d) HCl is strong,
> HF is weak

## Ladder 10 • Buffers

`CED 8.4` `CED 8.8` `CED 8.9`

A buffer holds appreciable amounts of *both* members of a conjugate
pair, so it can neutralize either added acid or added base.

$$ \text{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a +    \log\frac{[\text{base}]}{[\text{acid}]} $$

> 📘 **I do: pH and mechanism**
>
> A buffer is 0.40 M in CH₃COOH and 0.25 M in
> CH₃COO- ($\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.74$). Find its pH, and explain how it
> resists change.
> 
> $$ \text{pH} = 4.74 + \log\frac{0.25}{0.40} = 4.74 + (-0.20)    = \mathbf{4.54} $$
> 
> Below the $\mathrm{p}K_a$, as it must be when the acid form dominates.
> 
> **Added H+** is consumed by the base component:
> CH₃COO⁻ + H+ → CH₃COOH. Strong acid becomes weak acid, so free
> $[\text{H₃O+}]$ barely rises.
> 
> **Added OH-** is consumed by the acid component:
> CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O.
> 
> **Why the pH barely moves:** it depends on the *ratio* of the
> pair, and a small addition changes that ratio only slightly. A logarithm
> of a slightly-changed ratio is a barely-changed number.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 10 — four questions**
>
> 1. pH of a buffer with equal concentrations of the pair
>    ($\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.74$):
>    *(working space)*
> 2. pH when $[\text{base}]/[\text{acid}] = 10$:
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Write the equation for the buffer consuming added OH-.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why does a solution of acetic acid *alone* not buffer?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 4.74     (b) 5.74    
> (c) CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O     (d) it has almost no
> conjugate base

## Ladder 11 • Buffer capacity

`CED 8.10`

Capacity is how much acid or base a buffer can absorb before its pH moves
sharply. It depends on the **absolute amounts** of the pair, not on
their ratio.

> 📘 **I do: same pH, different capacity**
>
> Two buffers both have pH 4.74. Buffer X is 1.0 M in each
> component; buffer Y is 0.010 M in each. Compare them.
> 
> **Same pH:** both have a $1\!:\!1$ ratio, and pH depends on the
> ratio, so both read 4.74.
> 
> **Very different capacity:** X holds a hundred times more of each
> component. Adding 0.005 mol of strong acid barely dents X's
> reservoir, but would consume half of Y's base component and move its pH
> substantially.
> 
> **The distinction to hold on to:** **ratio** sets the pH;
> **amount** sets the capacity. Two independent properties, controlled
> by two different things.
> 
> **Best capacity when $\text{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$,** because the ratio
> is then 1 and the buffer has equal reserves in both directions. Useful
> range is roughly $\mathrm{p}K_a \pm 1$.

## Ladder 12 • Titration curves

`CED 8.5`

Four landmarks on a weak-acid titration: the start, the
half-equivalence point (pH $= \mathrm{p}K_a$), the equivalence point
(above 7), and the excess-base region.

> 📘 **I do: reading the four regions**
>
> 25.00 mL of 0.100 M acetic acid
> ($\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.74$) is titrated with 0.100 M NaOH.
> Describe each landmark.
> 
> **Start (0 mL):** weak acid alone. pH 2.87 from the $K_a$
> calculation — not 1.00, because it is weak.
> 
> **Half-equivalence (12.50 mL):** half the acid has become acetate, so
> $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A-}]$ and
> $\text{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a = \mathbf{4.74}$. Reading a
> $\mathrm{p}K_a$ straight off a curve at this point is a standard exam
> task.
> 
> **Equivalence (25.00 mL):** all the acid is now acetate, a weak base.
> It hydrolyses water, so the pH is **above 7** — here 8.72.
> This is the landmark most often got wrong; equivalence does *not*
> mean pH 7 unless both acid and base were strong.
> 
> **Beyond equivalence:** excess strong base dominates and the pH
> approaches that of the titrant.
> 
> **Indicator choice follows:** phenolphthalein (8.3–10.0) brackets
> 8.72; methyl orange (3.1–4.4) would change far too early.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 12 — four questions**
>

>    *(working space)*
> 2. Is the equivalence point of a strong acid with a strong base above,
