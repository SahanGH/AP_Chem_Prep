> 📌 **The question to ask before every calculation**
>
> This unit contains four situations, and nearly every lost mark comes from
> running the wrong one. Before touching a calculator, name the major species
> in solution and decide which case you are in:
> 
> | **What is in solution** | **Case** | **Method** |
> |---|---|---|
> | strong acid or base alone | complete ionization | pH directly |
> | weak acid (or base) alone | one equilibrium | ICE with $K_a$ (or $K_b$) |
> | weak acid *and* its conjugate | buffer | Henderson–Hasselbalch |
> | strong reagent added | react to completion *first* | then one of the
>   above |

At **11–15%** this is the second-heaviest unit on the exam. It is
also the one where method selection, rather than arithmetic, decides the
grade.

## Brønsted–Lowry and Conjugate Pairs CED 8.1 • Zumdahl §14.1–14.2

> 📌 **By the end you can…**
>
> - Identify conjugate acid–base pairs.
> - Relate acid strength to $K_a$ and to conjugate base strength.

**Read:** Zumdahl §14.1–14.2 • PDF pp. 697–705

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. Net ionic equation for strong acid $+$ strong base:
>    H+ + OH⁻ → H₂O
> 2. Name two strong acids (there are six):
>    HCl, HNO₃
> 3. $K$ expression for HA ⇌ H+ + A⁻:
>    $[\text{H+}][\text{A⁻}]/[\text{HA}]$
> 4. $K \ll 1$ favors: reactants

#### INSTRUCTION A • Two models, one of which AP uses 25 min

### Proton donors and acceptors `CED 8.1`

`SP 1`

- **Arrhenius:** an acid produces H+ in
   water; a base produces OH-. Historically
   important, but it applies only to aqueous solutions.
- **Brønsted–Lowry:** an acid is a proton
   donor; a base is a proton
   acceptor. This is the model the CED uses.

Water acts as a base because its oxygen carries
unshared electron pairs, either of which can bond to a
proton. So H+ in solution is really hydronium, H₃O+;
writing H+ is accepted shorthand.

### Conjugate pairs `CED 8.1`

Every Brønsted–Lowry reaction contains *two* pairs differing by one
proton:
HC₂H₃O₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O+ + C₂H₃O₂⁻
HC₂H₃O₂/C₂H₃O₂⁻ is one pair; H₂O/H₃O+ is the other.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.** HCl is
> strong, so Cl- is such a poor base it is effectively inert in water.
> HCN is very weak, so CN- is a base strong enough to matter.
> 
> Quantitatively, $K_a \times K_b$ equals $K_w$ for a
> conjugate pair — adding the two reactions gives the autoionization of
> water.

## pH, pOH, and Strong Acids and Bases CED 8.2 • Zumdahl §14.3–14.4, 14.6

> 📌 **By the end you can…**
>
> - Convert among $[\text{H+}]$, $[\text{OH-}]$, pH and pOH.
> - Calculate the pH of strong acid and strong base solutions.

**Read:** Zumdahl §14.3–14.4, 14.6 • PDF pp. 705–724

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. $K_aK_b =$ $K_w$
> 2. Conjugate base of HF: F-
> 3. $K_w$ at 25 °C:
>    $1.0\times10^{-14}$

#### INSTRUCTION A • The logarithmic scale 25 min

### pH, pOH, and $K_w$ `CED 8.2`

`SP 5`

$$ \mathrm{pH} = -\log[\text{H+}] \qquad    \mathrm{pOH} = -\log[\text{OH-}] \qquad    \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14.00 $$

Each pH unit is a factor of 10 in $[\text{H+}]$, so
pH 3 is 1000 times more acidic than pH 6.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **Neutral means $[\text{H+}] = [\text{OH-}]$, not pH 7.** $K_w$ rises with
> temperature, so pure water at 50 °C has a pH below 7 and is
> still perfectly neutral. The number 7 applies only at 25 °C.

#### INSTRUCTION B • When ionization is complete 20 min

### Strong acids and bases `CED 8.2`

`SP 5`

For a strong acid, ionization is complete, so $[\text{H+}]$ simply equals the
acid concentration. No equilibrium calculation is
needed.

There are only **six strong acids**, worth memorizing outright:

$$ \text{HCl} \quad \text{HBr} \quad \text{HI} \quad \text{HNO₃} \quad    \text{H₂SO₄} \quad \text{HClO₄} $$

Note that HF is *not* among them. The strong bases are the Group 1
hydroxides and Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂.

> 📘 **Worked example 1: the factor everyone forgets**
>
> 0.010 M HCl: $[\text{H+}] = 0.010$, so
> $\mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{2.00}$.
> 
> 0.010 M Ba(OH)₂ produces *two* hydroxides per formula
> unit:
> 
> $$ [\text{OH-}] = 2(0.010) = 0.020 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad    \mathrm{pOH} = 1.70,\ \mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{12.30} $$
> 
> — not 12.00. Always check the formula for how many ions are released.

## Weak Acid Equilibria CED 8.3 • Zumdahl §14.5

> 📌 **By the end you can…**
>
> - Calculate the pH of a weak acid solution.
> - Compute and interpret percent ionization.

**Read:** Zumdahl §14.5 • PDF pp. 710–719

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. pH of 0.010 M HCl: 2.00
> 2. pH of 0.010 M Ba(OH)₂:
>    12.30
> 3. ICE stands for: Initial, Change, Equilibrium

#### INSTRUCTION A • A weak acid problem is a Unit 7 problem 25 min

### $K_a$ and the ICE table `CED 8.3`

`SP 5`

$$ \text{HA(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + A⁻(aq)} \qquad    K_a = \frac{[\text{H+}][\text{A⁻}]}{[\text{HA}]} $$

Because $K_a$ is small, $x$ is usually negligible against $C_0$:

$$ K_a \approx \frac{x^2}{C_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad    x = [\text{H+}] = \sqrt{K_aC_0} $$

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **The 5% check is part of the answer.** Compute $x/C_0$ as a
> percentage and compare with 5%. An FRQ that asks you to justify the
> approximation wants the number, not the assertion — exactly as in Unit 7.

> 📘 **Worked example 2: strong is not concentrated**
>
> 0.10 M acetic acid, $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$:
> 
> $$ x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.10)} = 1.34\times10^{-3}    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{pH} = \mathbf{2.87} $$
> 
> Check: $1.34\times10^{-3}/0.10 = 1.3\%$ ✓
> 
> **Compare with 0.10 M HCl, which has pH 1.00.** Same
> concentration; the difference is entirely *strength* — the fraction
> that ionizes. “Strong” and “concentrated” are different words describing
> different things.

#### APPLICATION • Weak acid calculations 20 min

1. pH of 0.250 M HF ($K_a = 7.2\times10^{-4}$),
   with the check.
   *(working space)*
2. Percent ionization of 0.100 M acetic acid:
   1.3%
3. Diluting a weak acid raises the percent ionization but also raises
   the pH. Explain how both can be true.
   

> 📌 **Exit ticket**
>
> Two solutions both have 0.10 M concentration: one HCl, one
> acetic acid. Which is more concentrated, and which is more acidic?

## Weak Bases and Polyprotic Acids CED 8.3 • Zumdahl §14.6–14.7

> 📌 **By the end you can…**
>
> - Calculate the pH of a weak base solution.
> - Explain why the first ionization dominates for a polyprotic acid.

**Read:** Zumdahl §14.6–14.7 • PDF pp. 719–730

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. $[\text{H+}]$ for a weak acid: $\sqrt{K_aC_0}$
> 2. The approximation threshold: 5%
> 3. pH of 0.10 M acetic acid: 2.87

#### INSTRUCTION A • The same table, one extra step 25 min

### Weak bases and $K_b$ `CED 8.3`

`SP 5`

$$ \text{B(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ BH+(aq) + OH⁻(aq)} \qquad    K_b = \frac{[\text{BH+}][\text{OH⁻}]}{[\text{B}]} $$

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **For a base, the ICE $x$ is $[\text{OH-}]$, not $[\text{H+}]$.** Taking
> $-\log x$ gives the pOH, and you must then subtract
> from 14.00.
> 
> This is the single most-lost point in the unit. Reporting $-\log x$ as the
> pH turns a strongly basic answer into a strongly acidic one — a swing of
> several units in the wrong direction, which any sanity check should catch.

> 📘 **Worked example 3: ammonia**
>
> 0.100 M NH₃, $K_b = 1.8\times10^{-5}$:
> 
> $$ x = \sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.100)} = 1.34\times10^{-3} = [\text{OH-}] $$
> 
> $$ \mathrm{pOH} = 2.87 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad    \mathrm{pH} = 14.00 - 2.87 = \mathbf{11.13} $$
> 
> Note the pOH is numerically the same as acetic acid's pH — the two have
> equal constants. The final answers are wildly different, and the conversion
> step is the only reason.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Polyprotic acids 20 min

### Why only the first ionization matters `CED 8.3`

`SP 5`

H₃PO₄ has $K_{a1} = 7.5\times10^{-3}$,
$K_{a2} = 6.2\times10^{-8}$, $K_{a3} = 4.8\times10^{-13}$.

Each successive constant is about $10^5$ times
smaller, because each proton must be pulled from an
increasingly negative anion. So the pH may be
calculated from $K_{a1}$ alone.

The exception is H₂SO₄: its first ionization is
strong and its second
($K_{a2} = 1.2\times10^{-2}$) still contributes measurably.

   *(working space)*

3. Justify calculating H₃PO₄'s pH from $K_{a1}$ alone.

> 1. For a weak base, ICE $x$ is: $[\text{OH-}]$
> 2. pH of 0.100 M NH₃: 11.13

#### INSTRUCTION A • p$K_a$ and structure 25 min

### p$K_a$ `CED 8.7`

`SP 5`

$$ \mathrm{p}K_a = -\log K_a $$

A *lower* p$K_a$ means a stronger acid. Acetic
acid's $K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$ gives p$K_a =$ 4.74.

### Structure and acid strength `CED 8.6`

`SP 6`

- **Binary acids down a group:** HF $ ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> HF is the *weakest* hydrohalic acid despite fluorine being the
> most electronegative element. Holding the proton tightly in a very strong
> H–F bond matters more than pulling electron density away from it. Do not
> reach for electronegativity when the trend is down a group.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Salt solutions 20 min

### Trace each ion to its parent `CED 8.7`

`SP 6`

| **Cation from** | **Anion from** | **Solution** | **Example** |
|---|---|---|---|
| strong base | strong acid | **neutral** | NaCl |
| strong base | weak acid | **basic** | NaC₂H₃O₂ |
| weak base | strong acid | **acidic** | NH₄Cl |

The logic is entirely conjugate-pair reasoning: an anion from a
*weak* acid is a meaningful base, while one from a *strong* acid
is not.

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**

> 1. p$K_a$ of acetic acid: 4.74
> 2. NH₄Cl solutions are: acidic
> 3. Adding a product shifts an equilibrium:

#### INSTRUCTION A • The common-ion effect 25 min

### Le Ch\^atelier applied to an acid `CED 8.4`

`SP 6`

HF(aq) ⇌ H+(aq) + F⁻(aq)
Dissolve NaF in this solution and you have added a
product, so the equilibrium shifts
left and the acid ionizes *less*.

The algebra collapses to a ratio:

$$ [\text{H+}] = K_a\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A⁻}]} $$

No square root, no quadratic — when both members of a conjugate pair are
present in comparable amounts, $x$ is negligible against both.

#### INSTRUCTION B • What makes a buffer 20 min

### Recognizing one `CED 8.4`

`SP 6`

A buffer contains significant amounts of **both members of a
conjugate pair**. Three ways it appears:

Stated outright — HC₂H₃O₂ plus NaC₂H₃O₂.

Made by partial neutralization of a weak acid
        with strong base.

Hiding at the halfway point of a titration.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> HCl with NaCl is **not** a buffer. Cl- has no affinity
> for protons, so nothing absorbs added acid. A buffer needs a *weak*
> conjugate pair — reversibility is the whole point.

## Henderson–Hasselbalch CED 8.8–8.9 • Zumdahl §15.2

> 📌 **By the end you can…**
>
> - Apply the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.
> - Explain why the ratio, not the concentration, sets the pH.

**Read:** Zumdahl §15.2 • PDF pp. 763–767

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. $[\text{H+}] = K_a \times$
>    $[\text{HA}]/[\text{A⁻}]$
> 2. A buffer contains: both members of a conjugate
>    pair
> 3. p$K_a$ of NH₄+: 9.25

#### INSTRUCTION A • Taking the logarithm 25 min

### The equation `CED 8.9`

`SP 5`

$$ \boxed{\;\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a    + \log\frac{[\text{base}]}{[\text{acid}]}\;} $$

- Equal amounts $\Rightarrow$ log term is
   0, so pH $=$
   p$K_a$.
- More base than acid $\Rightarrow$ pH
   above p$K_a$.
- More acid than base $\Rightarrow$ pH
   below p$K_a$.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **Base over acid, and the base is the anion.** Inverting the ratio
> flips the sign of the correction, landing the pH the same distance on the
> wrong side of p$K_a$. Before dividing, name the two species aloud:
> C₂H₃O₂⁻ is the base, HC₂H₃O₂ is the acid.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Why the ratio is what matters 20 min

### Dilution barely moves a buffer's pH `CED 8.8`

`SP 5`

Only the *ratio* appears, so:

- Diluting a buffer leaves the pH essentially
   unchanged — both concentrations fall by the
   same factor. Contrast a weak acid alone, where dilution
   *does* raise the pH.
- You may use moles instead of molarity, since
   the shared volume cancels. This saves a step on nearly every
   titration problem.

> 📘 **Worked example 4: two buffers, one pH**
>
> 0.25 M HC₂H₃O₂ with 0.15 M NaC₂H₃O₂:
> 
> $$ \mathrm{pH} = 4.74 + \log(0.15/0.25) = 4.74 - 0.22 = \mathbf{4.52} $$
> 
> 0.050 M with 0.030 M gives the same ratio $0.60$ and
> therefore the **same pH, 4.52** — at one fifth the concentration. The
> pH is identical; the *capacity* is not.

## Buffer Action and Capacity CED 8.10 • Zumdahl §15.2–15.3

> 📌 **By the end you can…**
>
> - Calculate a buffer's pH after strong acid or base is added.
> - Compare buffering capacity and design a buffer.

**Read:** Zumdahl §15.2–15.3 • PDF pp. 761–770

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH $=$
>    p$K_a + \log$(base/acid)
> 2. pH when the ratio is 1: p$K_a$
> 3. Diluting a buffer changes the pH how?
>    hardly at all

#### INSTRUCTION A • Stoichiometry first, then equilibrium 25 min

### The two-step method `CED 8.10`

`SP 5`

**Stoichiometry.** The strong reagent reacts
        completely. Added OH- converts HA to
        A-; added H+ does the reverse. Work in moles. No $K$
        appears.

**Equilibrium.** Put the new amounts into
        Henderson–Hasselbalch.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **Never put added strong base into an ICE table.** Strong base does not
> sit at equilibrium with a weak acid — it consumes it. Mixing the two steps
> is the single most reliable way to lose an entire buffer question.

> 📘 **Worked example 5: a buffer absorbing a base**
>
> 1.00 L of a buffer is 0.50 M in HC₂H₃O₂ and
> 0.50 M in NaC₂H₃O₂. Add 0.010 mol NaOH.
> 
> **Start:** ratio 1, so pH $= 4.74$.
> 
> **Step 1:** OH⁻ + HC₂H₃O₂ → C₂H₃O₂⁻ + H₂O leaves
> $0.49$ mol acid and $0.51$ mol base.
> 
> **Step 2:** pH $= 4.74 + \log(0.51/0.49) = \mathbf{4.76}$.
> 
> **For contrast,** the same 0.010 mol of NaOH in
> 1.00 L of pure water gives pH $\mathbf{12.00}$. The buffer moved
> **0.02**; water moved **5.00**.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Capacity and design 20 min

### Why equal amounts buffer best `CED 8.10`

`SP 6`

Buffering capacity depends on two things: how *much* of each
component is present, and how *balanced* the ratio is.

> 
Optimal buffering when $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A⁻}]$, i.e. pH $=$ p$K_a$.   

Useful range: p$K_a \pm 1$.

To **design** a buffer, choose a weak acid whose p$K_a$ is as close as
possible to the target pH, then set the ratio with
Henderson–Hasselbalch.

> 📌 **Retrieval warm-up**
>
> 1. Buffer method step 1: stoichiometry to
>    completion
> 2. Optimal buffering ratio: 1
> 3. Useful buffer range: p$K_a \pm 1$

#### INSTRUCTION A • Four regions, four methods 25 min

### Weak acid titrated with strong base `CED 8.5`

`SP 5`

| **Region** | **Major species** | **Method** |
|---|---|---|
| before any base | HA only | weak-acid ICE |
| before equivalence | HA $+$ A- | **buffer:** H–H |
| at equivalence | A- only | weak-*base* ICE, $K_b = K_w/K_a$ |
| after equivalence | excess OH- | strong base alone |

> 📌 **Two landmarks worth a point each**
>
> **Halfway point:** $[\text{HA}] = [\text{A⁻}]$, so pH $=$ p$K_a$. This is
> how $K_a$ is measured from a curve.
> 
> **Equivalence point:** moles of base $=$ moles of acid. That is a
> *stoichiometric* definition and says nothing about the pH being 7.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> **A weak-acid equivalence point is not pH 7.** All the acid has become
> its conjugate base, which hydrolyses. Weak acid with strong base gives an
> equivalence pH above 7; weak base with strong acid gives
> one below 7. Only strong-with-strong lands on 7.00.

> 📘 **Worked example 6: acetic acid titration**
>
> 50.0 mL of 0.10 M HC₂H₃O₂
> ($= 5.0\,\mathrm{mmol}$) with 0.10 M NaOH; equivalence
> at 50.0 mL.
> 
> | **mL** | **Region** | **pH** |
> |---|---|---|
> | 0 | weak acid | 2.87 |
> | 25.0 | **halfway** — pH $=$ p$K_a$ | **4.74** |
> | 50.0 | **equivalence** — A- only | **8.72** |
> | 60.0 | excess base | 11.96 |

At equivalence, $[\text{C₂H₃O₂⁻}] = 5.0/100.0 = 0.050$ M and
$K_b = 5.6\times10^{-10}$, giving $[\text{OH-}] = 5.3\times10^{-6}$,
pOH $= 5.28$, pH $= 8.72$ — basic, as it must be.

#### INSTRUCTION B • Indicators and pH effects 20 min

### Choosing an indicator `CED 8.5`

`SP 6`

An indicator is itself a weak acid whose two forms differ in colour. It
changes over roughly p$K_a \pm 1$, so choose one whose
range brackets the equivalence pH.

The equivalence point is fixed by stoichiometry; the
end point is where the indicator changes. A good choice makes them
nearly coincide.

### pH and solubility `CED 8.11`

`SP 6`

> 📌 **Qualitative only**
>
> CED topic 8.11 carries an **exclusion statement**: computations of
> solubility as a function of pH are never assessed. Predict the direction and
> justify with Le Ch\^atelier — do not calculate.

A salt is more soluble in acid when its anion is a
meaningful base — that is, when its conjugate acid is
weak. CaCO₃ dissolves in acid because CO₃²⁻ is consumed;
AgCl does not, because Cl- comes from a strong acid and does not
react.
