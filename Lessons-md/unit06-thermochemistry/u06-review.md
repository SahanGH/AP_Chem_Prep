## The five tools

| **Sign convention** | always from the *system's* view.
  Exothermic $q  0$, surroundings
  cool. The thermometer is in the surroundings |
|---|---|
| **$q = mc\Delta T$** | only while the temperature is changing, and
  only for one phase. Use the *water's* mass and specific heat in
  calorimetry ($c = 4.18$) |
| **Phase changes** | $q = n\Delta H_{\text{fus}}$ or
  $n\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$. Temperature is constant, so $q = mc\Delta T$
  gives zero and cannot be used |
| **Hess's law** | reactions add, and so do their $\Delta H$. Reverse
  $\Rightarrow$ flip the sign; multiply by $n$ $\Rightarrow$ multiply
  $\Delta H$ by $n$ |
| **Two $\Delta H$ formulas** | formation: products $-$ reactants.
  Bonds: *broken* $-$ *formed*. The bond one runs the other way |

## Numbers worth knowing cold

- $c$(water) $= 4.18\,\mathrm{J/g/{}^\circ C}$ — unusually
   large, because of hydrogen bonding.
- $\Delta H_{\text{fus}}$(water) $=         6.02\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$;
   $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$(water) $=         40.7\,\mathrm{kJ/mol}$ — nearly seven times larger.
- On a heating curve for water, boiling is roughly **three
   quarters** of the total energy.
- $\Delta H_f^\circ = 0$ for any element in its standard state.

## The four-step calorimetry chain

1. $\Delta T = T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}}$ for the
   *water*.
2. $q_{\text{water}} = mc\Delta T$.
3. $q_{\text{rxn}} = -q_{\text{water}}$    (the sign reversal is
   where marks are lost).
4. $\Delta H = q_{\text{rxn}} / n$, reported in
   kJ/mol.

## The traps, one line each

- Calling a reaction endothermic because the temperature went up.
- Using $q = mc\Delta T$ during melting or boiling.
- Forgetting the minus sign converting $q_{\text{water}}$ to
   $q_{\text{rxn}}$.
- Reporting kilojoules when the question wants
   kJ/mol.
- Reversing a reaction in Hess's law without flipping the sign.
- Using *formed minus broken* for bond enthalpies.
- Treating an element's $\Delta H_f^\circ$ as something other than
   zero.

> 📌 **How to study for this exam**
>
> Redo WS 4's FRQ cold — the exam's free-response questions are its twins
> in shape. Then run the four-step calorimetry chain until it is automatic,
> because it opens most thermochemistry FRQs and the sign reversal in step 3
> is the most commonly dropped point in the unit.
> 
> For every “explain” item, name what the *particles* are doing:
> colliding and transferring kinetic energy, or overcoming intermolecular
> forces. And before writing any final answer, ask once: does the sign match
> the direction of the temperature change I was given?
