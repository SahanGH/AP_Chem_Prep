## Ladder 1 • Endothermic and exothermic

`CED 6.1`

Define the *system* (the reaction) and the *surroundings*
(everything else, including the beaker and the thermometer). Then ask
which way energy flowed.

> 📘 **I do: reading a temperature change backwards**
>
> A beaker becomes cold when two solids are mixed. State the sign of
> $\Delta H$ and explain the flow.
> 
> **The surroundings got colder**, so the surroundings *lost*
> energy. That energy went into the system.
> 
> **The system absorbed energy**, so the process is
> **endothermic** and $\Delta H$ is **positive**.
> 
> **The reversal that trips people:** what you feel is the
> *surroundings*, but the sign refers to the *system*. A cold
> beaker means a positive $\Delta H$; a hot beaker means a negative one.
> 
> **What never to write:** “the reaction gives off cold”. Cold is not
> a substance. Energy flowed one way only — into the system.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 1 — four questions**
>
> 1. Combustion warms its surroundings. Sign of $\Delta H$?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. NH₄NO₃ dissolving makes the beaker cold. Sign?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. In an exothermic reaction, do the surroundings gain or lose
>    energy?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Melting ice is endothermic. Explain in terms of the system.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) negative     (b) positive     (c) gain     (d) the
> ice absorbs energy to overcome attractions

## Ladder 2 • Energy diagrams

`CED 6.2`

The vertical axis is energy. Where the products sit relative to the
reactants gives the sign of $\Delta H$; the hump between them is the
activation energy, which says nothing about $\Delta H$.

> 📘 **I do: reading both quantities off one picture**
>
> A diagram shows reactants at 60 kJ, a peak at
> 150 kJ and products at 25 kJ. Find $E_a$
> and $\Delta H$, and classify the reaction.
> 
> **$E_a$ (forward):** $150 - 60 = \mathbf{90~kJ}$.
> 
> **$\Delta H$:** products minus reactants, $25 - 60 = \mathbf{-35~kJ}$. Negative, so **exothermic**.
> 
> **The visual signature:** products *below* reactants means
> exothermic; above means endothermic. Read the two *ends*, never the
> peak, for $\Delta H$.
> 
> **Why the peak is irrelevant to $\Delta H$:** a catalyst lowers the
> peak without moving either end, so it changes $E_a$ and leaves $\Delta H$
> untouched. The two quantities are independent.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 2 — four questions**
>
> 1. Reactants 40 kJ, peak 130 kJ,
>    products 95 kJ. $E_a$?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. $\Delta H$ for that reaction, and its classification?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. On an exothermic diagram, where do the products sit?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Does a large $E_a$ mean a large $\Delta H$? Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 90 kJ     (b) $+55$ kJ, endothermic    
> (c) below the reactants     (d) no — they are independent

## Ladder 3 • Heat transfer and thermal equilibrium

`CED 6.3`

Energy flows from hotter to cooler until both reach the same temperature.
Same temperature does not mean same energy.

> 📘 **I do: what equalizes and what does not**
>
> Two blocks at different temperatures are placed in contact and isolated.
> Describe what happens at the particle level, and state what is equal at
> equilibrium.
> 
> **At the boundary:** particles of the hotter block have greater
> average kinetic energy. They collide with particles of the cooler block
> and transfer energy to them.
> 
> **Over time:** the hotter block's average kinetic energy falls, the
> cooler block's rises, until the two averages match. Collisions continue,
> but with no further *net* transfer — the flow is now equal in both
> directions.
> 
> **What is equal at equilibrium: the temperature**, meaning the
> average kinetic energy per particle.
> 
> **What is *not* equal: the total thermal energy.** That depends
> on mass and specific heat capacity too. A large block of water at
> 25 °C holds far more energy than a small block of copper at
> the same temperature.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>
> 1. Which direction does energy flow between objects at different
>    temperatures?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. At thermal equilibrium, what quantity is equal?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Do the two objects then hold equal thermal energy? Explain.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Do collisions stop at thermal equilibrium? 
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) hotter to cooler     (b) temperature     (c) no —
> mass and $c$ differ     (d) no — net transfer stops, not the
> collisions

## Ladder 4 • Specific heat

`CED 6.4`

$q = mc\,\Delta T$. Specific heat capacity is the energy needed to raise
one gram by one degree — a property of the substance, not of the sample.

> 📘 **I do: energy to warm water**
>
> How much energy warms 125 g of water from 20.0 °C
> to 45.0 °C?
> 
> $$ q = mc\,\Delta T = (125)(4.18)(25.0) = \mathbf{1.31\times10^{4}~J}    = 13.1\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **$\Delta T$ is final minus initial**, and its size in
> °C equals its size in kelvin — so either unit works here, as
> long as you use a *difference*.
> 
> **Why water's value is so large.** At
> 4.18 J/g/°C, water takes far more energy per
> degree than most substances (copper is 0.385). Its hydrogen-bond
> network absorbs energy without the molecules speeding up much — which is
> why water moderates climate and makes a good coolant.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 4 — four questions**
>
> 1. Energy to warm 50.0 g of water by
>    30.0 °C:
>    *(working space)*
> 2. 2500 J into 100.0 g of water. Temperature
>    rise?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. 1200 J raises a metal
>    ($c = 0.900\,\mathrm{J/g/{}^\circ C}$) by
>    15.0 °C. Mass?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Equal masses of water and copper absorb the same energy. Which
>    warms more, and why?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 6270 J     (b) 5.98 °C    
> (c) 88.9 g     (d) copper — much smaller $c$

## Ladder 5 • Calorimetry

`CED 6.4` `CED 6.6`

In a coffee-cup calorimeter the *solution* absorbs the heat, so
$q$ is computed for the solution — and the reaction's $\Delta H$ is the
opposite sign, per mole.

> 📘 **I do: a neutralization**
>
> 50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl and
> 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH, both at
> 21.0 °C, are mixed. The temperature peaks at
> 28.2 °C. Find $\Delta H$ per mole of water formed.
> 
> **Mass of solution:** $50.0 + 50.0 = 100.0\,\mathrm{g}$ (assuming
> the density of water). *Total* volume — not one of them.
> 
> **Heat absorbed by the solution:**
> 
> $$ q = (100.0)(4.18)(7.2) = 3.0e3\,\mathrm{J} =    3.01\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **Moles of water formed:** $(0.0500)(1.00) = 0.0500$ mol —
> both reactants supply exactly this, so neither is in excess.
> 
> **Per mole, with the sign flipped:**
> 
> $$ \Delta H = -\frac{3.01}{0.0500} = \mathbf{-60.2~kJ/mol} $$
> 
> Negative because the solution *gained* what the reaction
> *released*.
> 
> **Two traps:** using 50.0 g instead of 100.0 g,
> and forgetting to flip the sign.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>
> 1. Why is the total mass of solution used, not one reactant's?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. If the same experiment used 100 mL of each, would
>    $\Delta T$ change? Would $\Delta H$ per mole?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Why is $\Delta H$ negative when the temperature rose?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Give one reason the measured magnitude is usually too small.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) the whole solution absorbs the heat     (b) $\Delta T$
> about the same; $\Delta H$ per mole unchanged     (c) the system
> released it     (d) heat lost to the surroundings

## Ladder 6 • Energy of phase changes

`CED 6.5`

A phase change uses $q = m\,\Delta H_{\text{phase}}$ — mass only, no
$\Delta T$, because the temperature does not change.

> 📘 **I do: ice to steam in three steps**
>
> Find the energy to convert 40.0 g of ice at 0 °C
> into steam at 100 °C.
> 
> **Step 1 — melt** (no $\Delta T$):
> $q = (40.0)(334) = 13.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
> 
> **Step 2 — warm the liquid** (this one has a $\Delta T$):
> $q = (40.0)(4.18)(100) = 16.7\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
> 
> **Step 3 — vaporize** (no $\Delta T$):
> $q = (40.0)(2260) = 90.4\,\mathrm{kJ}$.
> 
> $$ \text{Total} = 13.4 + 16.7 + 90.4 = \mathbf{120~kJ} $$
> 
> **Notice which step dominates.** Vaporizing takes more than the other
> two combined, because melting only loosens the lattice while vaporizing
> must separate the molecules completely — overcoming essentially all the
> hydrogen bonding.
> 
> **The structural error to avoid:** putting a $\Delta T$ into a
> phase-change step. During melting or boiling the temperature is constant,
> so there is no $\Delta T$ to use.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 6 — four questions**
>
> 1. Energy to melt 60.0 g of ice at 0 °C:
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Energy to vaporize 10.0 g of water at
>    100 °C:
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Why does a phase-change calculation use no $\Delta T$?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is $\Delta H_{\text{vap}}$ so much larger than
>    $\Delta H_{\text{fus}}$?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 20.0 kJ     (b) 22.6 kJ
>     (c) temperature is constant during the change    
> (d) vaporizing separates molecules completely

## Ladder 7 • Hess's law

`CED 6.9`

Enthalpy changes add. Reverse a reaction and the sign flips; multiply a
reaction and $\Delta H$ multiplies too.

> 📘 **I do: manipulating to reach a target**
>
> Given
> C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g), $\Delta H = -393.5$ kJ, and
> CO(g) + 1/2O₂(g) → CO₂(g), $\Delta H = -283.0$ kJ,
> find $\Delta H$ for C(s) + 1/2O₂(g) → CO(g).
> 
> **Target check:** CO must end on the *right*, but in the
> second equation it is on the left. So reverse the second and flip its
> sign:
> 
> $$ \text{CO₂(g) → CO(g) + 1/2O₂(g)} \qquad \Delta H = +283.0~\text{kJ} $$
> 
> **Add to the first:**
> C + O₂ + CO₂ → CO₂ + CO + 1/2O₂. The CO₂ cancels and the
> oxygens net to 1/2 O₂ on the left.
> 
> $$ \Delta H = (-393.5) + (+283.0) = \mathbf{-110.5~kJ} $$
> 
> **Why it works:** enthalpy depends only on the initial and final
> states, not the route. Any sequence of steps connecting the same start and
> finish must sum to the same total — which is what lets us obtain a value
> for a reaction too difficult to measure directly, as this one is.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 7 — four questions**
>
> 1. If A → B has $\Delta H = -50$ kJ, what is it for
>    B → A?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. If A → B has $\Delta H = -50$ kJ, what is it for
>    2A → 2B?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Why can enthalpy changes be added at all? 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is Hess's law useful in practice? 
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) $+50$ kJ     (b) $-100$ kJ     (c) enthalpy depends
> only on the states     (d) it gives values that cannot be measured
> directly

## Ladder 8 • Bond enthalpies

`CED 6.7`

Breaking bonds costs energy; forming them releases it.

$$ \Delta H \approx \sum(\text{bonds broken}) -    \sum(\text{bonds formed}) $$

> 📘 **I do: a simple gas-phase reaction**
>
> Estimate $\Delta H$ for H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g) from
> H-H 436, Cl-Cl 243, H-Cl 431
> kJ/mol.
> 
> **Broken** (energy in): $436 + 243 = 679$ kJ.
> 
> **Formed** (energy out): $2 \times 431 = 862$ kJ.
> 
> $$ \Delta H = 679 - 862 = \mathbf{-183~kJ} $$
> 
> Negative, so exothermic — more energy is released making the new bonds
> than was needed to break the old ones. The accepted value is
> -185 kJ, so the estimate is good to about
> 1 %.
> 
> **Why only an estimate:** tabulated bond enthalpies are
> *averages* across many different compounds. A particular H-Cl
> bond in a particular molecule differs slightly from the average. The
> method also assumes every species is a gas.
> 
> **Direction of the subtraction:** broken *minus* formed.
> Reversing it gives the right magnitude with the wrong sign, which is a
> whole-question error.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. Is breaking a bond endothermic or exothermic?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. If bonds broken total 800 kJ and formed
>    950 kJ, find $\Delta H$.
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Why is a bond-enthalpy result only approximate?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why does the method require all species to be gases?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) endothermic     (b) $-150$ kJ     (c) tabulated values
> are averages     (d) otherwise intermolecular forces contribute too

## Ladder 9 • Enthalpy of formation

`CED 6.8`

$$ \Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products})    - \sum \Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants}) $$

An element in its standard state has $\Delta H_f^\circ = 0$.

> 📘 **I do: a combustion from formation data**
>
> Find $\Delta H^\circ$ for
> C₂H₄(g) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) using
> $\Delta H_f^\circ$: C₂H₄ $+52.3$, CO₂ $-393.5$,
> H₂O(l) $-285.8$ kJ/mol.
> 
> **Products**, with coefficients applied:
> $2(-393.5) + 2(-285.8) = -787.0 - 571.6 = -1358.6$.
> 
> **Reactants:** $(+52.3) + 3(0) = 52.3$. Oxygen is an element in
> its standard state, so its value is **zero** — it is not omitted, it
> is genuinely zero.
> 
> $$ \Delta H^\circ = -1358.6 - 52.3 = \mathbf{-1411~kJ} $$
> 
> **Two things to get right:** apply the coefficients (a missed factor
> of 2 here changes the answer by hundreds of kilojoules), and subtract in
> the right direction — products minus reactants.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 9 — four questions**
>
> 1. What is $\Delta H_f^\circ$ for N₂(g)? Why?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. What is $\Delta H_f^\circ$ for O₃(g), and why is it not
>    zero?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Products total $-500$, reactants $-120$ kJ. Find
>    $\Delta H^\circ$.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why must the coefficients be applied to the tabulated values?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) zero — element in its standard state     (b) non-zero;
> O₂ is the standard state, not O₃     (c) $-380$ kJ    
> (d) the table is per mole

## Ladder 10 • Choosing the right method

`CED 6.6` `CED 6.7` `CED 6.8` `CED 6.9`

Three routes give $\Delta H$. Knowing which one the data are asking for is
half the skill.

> 📘 **I do: matching data to method**
>
> State which method to use for each set of data.
> 
> **Given a temperature change in a calorimeter:** use
> $q = mc\,\Delta T$, then divide by moles and flip the sign. This is the
> only *experimental* route.
> 
> **Given a table of $\Delta H_f^\circ$ values:** use products minus
> reactants. Most accurate of the three, and the usual exam default.
> 
> **Given a table of bond enthalpies:** use broken minus formed. Only
> an estimate, and only for gases.
> 
> **Given other reactions with their $\Delta H$ values:** use Hess's
> law — reverse and scale until they add to the target.
> 
> **The tell:** the data given *is* the instruction. Temperatures
> mean calorimetry; a $\Delta H_f^\circ$ table means the formation formula;
> bonds mean the estimate; whole reactions mean Hess.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 10 — four questions**
>
> 1. You are given the mass, specific heat and temperature change.
>    Which method?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. You are given two reactions with known $\Delta H$. Which method?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which method gives only an estimate, and why?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Which method is the only truly experimental one?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) calorimetry     (b) Hess's law     (c) bond
> enthalpies — averaged values     (d) calorimetry
