## Ladder 1 • Moles, mass and particles

`CED 1.1`

The mole is a counting unit: one mole is
$6.022\times10^{23}$ particles. The molar mass in
g/mol is the bridge between the mass you can weigh and the
number of particles you cannot.

> 📘 **I do: mass to moles to atoms**
>
> How many moles, and how many atoms, are in 12.5 g of calcium?
> 
> **Mass to moles** — divide by the molar mass:
> 
> $$ n = \frac{12.5}{40.08} = \mathbf{0.312~mol} $$
> 
> **Moles to atoms** — multiply by Avogadro's number:
> 
> $$ 0.3119 \times 6.022\times10^{23} = \mathbf{1.88\times10^{23}}    \text{ atoms} $$
> 
> **Sense check:** 12.5 g is well under one molar mass
> (40.08 g), so the answer must be under one mole. It is.

## Ladder 2 • Molar mass of a compound

`CED 1.1`

A subscript outside a bracket multiplies *everything* inside it.
That single rule is where most molar-mass errors come from.

> 📘 **I do: a compound with brackets**
>
> Find the molar mass of Ca(NO₃)₂.
> 
> **Read the formula first.** There is one calcium, and *two*
> nitrate groups. Each nitrate is one N and three O, so the compound holds
> 2 N and 6 O — not 1 N and 3 O.
> 
> $$ 40.08 + 2\,[\,14.01 + 3(16.00)\,] = 40.08 + 2(62.01)    = \mathbf{164.1~g/mol} $$
> 
> **The trap:** forgetting to distribute the 2 gives 102.1, and
> every later answer built on it is wrong.

## Ladder 3 • Mass spectra and average atomic mass

`CED 1.2`

A mass spectrum plots mass on the horizontal axis and abundance on the
vertical. Peak *position* gives isotope mass; peak *height*
gives how common that isotope is. The tabulated atomic mass is the
abundance-weighted average of the two.

> 📘 **I do: two isotopes to one atomic mass**
>
> Copper has two isotopes: ⁶³Cu,
> 62.930 u, 69.17 %; and ⁶⁵Cu,
> 64.928 u, 30.83 %. Find the average
> atomic mass.
> 
> **Weight each mass by its fractional abundance and add:**
> 
> $$ (62.930)(0.6917) + (64.928)(0.3083) = 43.53 + 20.02 =    \mathbf{63.55~u} $$
> 
> **Sense check:** the answer must land between the two isotope masses,
> and closer to the *more abundant* one. 63.55 sits nearer
> 62.930, as it must with 69 % of atoms being the lighter
> isotope.
> 
> **The trap:** averaging 62.930 and 64.928 without
> weighting gives 63.93 — wrong, because it assumes equal
> abundances.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>
> 1. Lithium: 6.015 u at 7.59 %,
>    7.016 u at 92.41 %. Average?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Chlorine: 34.969 u at 75.78 %,
>    36.966 u at 24.22 %. Average?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. An element has isotopes at 20 u and 22 u and an average atomic
>    mass of 20.2. Which peak is taller, and roughly by how much?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Explain why no single atom of chlorine has a mass of
>    35.45 u.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 6.94     (b) 35.45     (c) the 20 u peak,
> by roughly 9:1     (d) it is an average over isotopes, not a real
> particle mass

## Ladder 4 • Percent composition

`CED 1.3`

Percent composition asks what fraction of a compound's mass each element
contributes. Work from *one mole* of the compound.

> 📘 **I do: percent composition of a nitrate**
>
> Find the percent by mass of each element in Ca(NO₃)₂
> ($M = 164.1\,\mathrm{g/mol}$).
> 
> **Mass of each element in one mole:**
> Ca $= 40.08$;     N $= 2(14.01) = 28.02$;    
> O $= 6(16.00) = 96.00$.
> 
> $$ \%\text{Ca} = \frac{40.08}{164.1}\times100 = \mathbf{24.4\%} \qquad    \%\text{N} = \frac{28.02}{164.1}\times100 = \mathbf{17.1\%} $$
> 
> $$ \%\text{O} = \frac{96.00}{164.1}\times100 = \mathbf{58.5\%} $$
> 
> **Sense check:** the three must sum to 100 %.
> $24.4 + 17.1 + 58.5 = 100.0$. ✓

## Ladder 5 • Empirical and molecular formulas

`CED 1.3`

The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number *ratio* of atoms.
The molecular formula is the actual count. Percent composition gives you
the first; only the molar mass gives you the second.

> 📘 **I do: percentages to a molecular formula**
>
> A compound is 40.00 % C, 6.71 % H and
> 53.29 % O by mass, with
> $M = 180.2\,\mathrm{g/mol}$.
> 
> **Step 1 — assume 100.0 g,** so the percentages become
> masses in grams.
> 
> **Step 2 — convert each to moles:**
> 
> $$ \text{C: } \frac{40.00}{12.011} = 3.331 \qquad    \text{H: } \frac{6.71}{1.008} = 6.657 \qquad    \text{O: } \frac{53.29}{16.00} = 3.331 $$
> 
> **Step 3 — divide by the smallest** (3.331):
> C 1.00, H 2.00, O 1.00, so the empirical formula is **CH₂O**.
> 
> **Step 4 — scale to the molar mass.** Empirical mass
> $= 12.01 + 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 30.03$, and
> $180.2/30.03 = 2$… in fact $\mathbf{6.00}$.
> 
> $$ \text{CH₂O} \times 6 = \mathbf{\text{C₆H₁₂O₆}} $$
> 
> **The trap:** stopping at CH₂O. That ratio is shared by
> formaldehyde, acetic acid and glucose — the molar mass is what separates
> them.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>

>    $M = 78.11\,\mathrm{g/mol}$. Empirical and molecular
>    formulas?

> 2. 5.93 % H, 94.07 % O,
>    $M = 34.01\,\mathrm{g/mol}$. Empirical and molecular?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. 69.94 % Fe, 30.06 % O. Empirical
>    formula?
>    *(working space)*

>    never a molecular formula *and* the same structure?

> (c) Fe₂O₃     (d) the empirical formula fixes only the ratio

## Ladder 6 • Composition of mixtures

`CED 1.4`

A mixture has no formula. Its composition is whatever was put in, so it is
described by percent by mass — and its components keep their own
properties, which is how they can be separated.

> 📘 **I do: separating and weighing**
>
> A 8.00 g mixture of KCl and sand is stirred with water,
> filtered, and the filtrate evaporated to dryness, leaving
> 4.60 g of KCl. Find the percent by mass of KCl, and
> explain why the method works.
> 
> $$ \frac{4.60}{8.00}\times100 = \mathbf{57.5\%~KCl} $$
> 
> **Why it works:** KCl is ionic and dissolves as hydrated
> K+ and Cl- ions; sand is a covalent network solid whose bonds
> water cannot break. The difference in **solubility** is the property
> being exploited, and filtration separates the two.

## Ladder 7 • Electron configurations

`CED 1.5`

Fill lowest-energy orbitals first: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s,
3p, 4s, 3d, 4p. Capacities are $s$ 2, $p$ 6, $d$ 10.
The superscripts must sum to the electron count.

> 📘 **I do: an atom and an ion**
>
> Write the configuration of phosphorus ($Z = 15$) and of the sulfide ion,
> S²⁻.
> 
> **Phosphorus, 15 electrons:**
> 
> $$ \text{1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³} \qquad    (2+2+6+2+3 = 15 \text{ \checkmark}) $$
> 
> **Sulfide.** Neutral sulfur has 16 electrons; the $2-$ charge means
> it has *gained* two, giving 18:
> 
> $$ \text{1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶} \qquad (=18 \text{ \checkmark}) $$
> 
> **The trap:** writing the ion's configuration as though it were the
> atom. Always adjust the electron count for the charge first — negative
> means more electrons, positive means fewer.

## Ladder 8 • Reading a photoelectron spectrum

`CED 1.6`

A PES spectrum has one peak per occupied *subshell*. Peak
**height** gives the number of electrons in that subshell; peak
**position** gives their binding energy. Summing the heights gives
the atomic number — which identifies the element.

> 📘 **I do: peaks to an element**
>
> A spectrum shows five peaks of relative height $2$, $2$, $6$, $2$ and $3$,
> running from highest binding energy to lowest. Identify the element and
> explain which peak is highest in binding energy.
> 
> **Sum the heights:** $2+2+6+2+3 = 15$ electrons, so $Z = 15$ —
> **phosphorus**, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³.
> 
> **Highest binding energy: the first 1s peak.** Those electrons
> lie closest to the nucleus and are shielded by no others, so they feel the
> largest effective nuclear charge over the shortest distance. The Coulombic
> attraction is strongest, so the most energy is needed to remove one.
> 
> **Lowest binding energy: the 3p peak of height 3.** Outermost
> and most shielded, so held least tightly — these are the electrons
> phosphorus loses or shares in chemistry.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. Peaks of height $2, 2, 6, 1$. Identify the element.
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Peaks of height $2, 2, 6, 2, 4$. Identify the element.
>    *(working space)*
> 3. An element's spectrum has four peaks, the last of height 2.
>    Identify it and give its configuration.
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Two elements both show a 1s peak. Explain why the peak sits
>    at a *higher* binding energy for the element of larger $Z$.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) sodium     (b) sulfur     (c) magnesium,
> 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²     (d) more protons pull the 1s
> electrons harder

## Ladder 9 • Periodic trends, explained Coulombically

`CED 1.7`

Across a period, protons are added to the *same* shell: nuclear
charge rises, shielding barely changes, so the atom contracts and holds
its electrons more tightly. Down a group, a new shell is added: distance
and shielding both rise, so the atom expands and holds its outer electrons
more loosely.

> 📘 **I do: ranking and explaining**
>
> Rank Na, Mg and K by first ionization energy, and justify.
> 
> **Order: K $ kJ/mol).
> 
> **Na against Mg** — same shell, but magnesium has one
> more proton. The extra nuclear charge holds the valence electrons more
> tightly, so more energy is needed: Mg is higher.
> 
> **Na against K** — potassium's valence electron is in the
> next shell out, further from the nucleus and shielded by an extra filled
> shell. The attraction is weaker, so it is easier to remove: K is
> lower.
> 
> **What earns zero:** “Mg wants to lose two electrons” or
> “K is more reactive”. Name charge, distance, shielding — nothing
> else counts.

> 4. Why is the *second* ionization energy of sodium enormously

## Ladder 10 • Valence electrons and ionic compounds

`CED 1.8`

A main-group element's group number gives its valence-electron count, and
that fixes the ion it forms. Combine ions so the charges cancel; the
lattice energy that results is Coulombic.

> 📘 **I do: from groups to a formula and a comparison**
>
> Magnesium reacts with nitrogen. Give the ions, the formula, and predict
> whether the compound's lattice energy exceeds that of NaF.
> 
> **Ions:** Mg is group 2, two valence electrons, forms Mg²⁺.
> N is group 15, five valence electrons, forms N³⁻.
> 
> **Formula:** the charges must cancel. Three Mg²⁺ ($+6$) with
> two N³⁻ ($-6$) gives **Mg₃N₂**.
> 
> **Lattice energy against NaF:** compare Coulombically. The
> attraction is proportional to the product of the ionic charges. For
> Mg₃N₂ that product is $(2)(3) = 6$; for NaF it is
> $(1)(1) = 1$. Mg₃N₂ therefore has much the larger lattice energy.

>    *(working space)*

>    *(working space)*

>    993 °C. Explain how this follows from your answer to
>    (c).
>    *(working space)*
>
