## Ladder 1 • System, surroundings, and the
first law

`ZUM §6.1`

Draw a boundary. Inside is the system (the reaction); everything
outside is the surroundings. Energy crosses that boundary two
ways:

$$ \boxed{\;\Delta E = q + w\;} \qquad w = -P\,\Delta V $$

Signs, from the **system's** point of view — always:

| **Quantity** | **Positive when** | **Negative when** |
|---|---|---|
| $q$ | heat flows *in* (endothermic) | heat flows *out*
  (exothermic) |
| $w$ | surroundings compress the system | the system expands and
  *does* work |

The minus sign in $w = -P\Delta V$ exists so that an expanding gas
($\Delta V > 0$) reports negative work: it spent energy pushing the
atmosphere back.

> 📘 **I do: a gas that absorbs heat and expands**
>
> A gas absorbs 575 J of heat and expands from
> 1.20 L to 3.40 L against a constant external
> pressure of 1.00 atm. Find $w$ and $\Delta E$.
> 
> **Work** — expansion, so expect a negative value:
> 
> $$ w = -P\,\Delta V = -(1.00)(3.40 - 1.20) = -2.20\,\mathrm{L\cdot atm} $$
> 
> Convert, because L·atm is not a joule:
> 
> $$ -2.20 \times 101.3 = -223\,\mathrm{J} $$
> 
> **First law:**
> 
> $$ \Delta E = q + w = 575 + (-223) = \mathbf{+352~J} $$
> 
> **Read the answer back:** the gas took in 575 J, spent 223 J of it
> pushing the atmosphere aside, and kept the remaining 352 J as internal
> energy. The bookkeeping is the physics.

>    *(working space)*

## Ladder 2 • Enthalpy, and why chemists use
it

`ZUM §6.2`

At constant pressure — a beaker open to the room, which is nearly every
reaction you will run — the heat is given its own name:

$$ \boxed{\;\Delta H = q_P\;} $$

That is the whole reason $H$ exists: it is the energy change you can
measure with a thermometer, without tracking the work of expansion
separately.

|  | **$\Delta H  0$** |
|---|---|---|
| Name | exothermic | endothermic |
| Heat | released to surroundings | absorbed from surroundings |
| Surroundings feel | warmer | colder |
| Products' enthalpy | lower than reactants | higher than reactants |

$\Delta H$ is an **extensive** quantity: double the amounts and you
double the enthalpy change. So a thermochemical equation's $\Delta H$
belongs to *exactly* the coefficients written.

> 📘 **I do: scaling and reversing a thermochemical equation**
>
> Given
> 
> $$ \text{2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g)} \qquad    \Delta H = -198\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **(a) Heat released when 1.00 mol of SO₂ reacts.** The
> $-198$ kJ belongs to *two* moles of SO₂:
> 
> $$ \frac{-198}{2} = \mathbf{-99.0~kJ} $$
> 
> **(b) $\Delta H$ for the reverse reaction.** Reversing flips the
> sign:
> 
> $$ \text{2SO₃(g) → 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g)} \qquad    \Delta H = \mathbf{+198~kJ} $$
> 
> **(c) $\Delta H$ when 32.0 g of SO₂
> ($M = 64.07\,\mathrm{g/mol}$) reacts.**
> 
> $$ n = \frac{32.0}{64.07} = 0.4995\,\mathrm{mol}    \;\Rightarrow\; 0.4995 \times (-99.0) = \mathbf{-49.4~kJ} $$
> 
> Carry the unrounded moles into the multiplication: rounding to 0.500
> first would give $-49.5$, and that last digit is the one being asked
> for.
> 
> Every one of these is the same idea: $\Delta H$ scales with amount and
> flips with direction.

## Ladder 3 • Specific heat

`ZUM §6.2`

$$ \boxed{\;q = m\,c\,\Delta T\;} \qquad    \Delta T = T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}} $$

$c$ is the specific heat capacity — the energy needed to raise
one gram by one degree. Water's is unusually large,
4.18 J/g/°C, which is why lakes moderate
climate and why water is the coolant of choice.

Because $\Delta T$ is a *difference*, a change of 1 °C
equals a change of 1 K — so specific-heat problems are the
one place in this course where you may leave temperatures in
°C.

> 📘 **I do: heating a metal, then finding its identity**
>
> **(a)** How much heat raises 125 g of copper
> ($c = 0.385\,\mathrm{J/g/{}^\circ C}$) from 22.0 °C
> to 88.5 °C?
> 
> $$ q = (125)(0.385)(88.5 - 22.0) = (125)(0.385)(66.5)      = \mathbf{3200~J} = 3.20\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **(b)** A 45.0 g sample of an unknown metal absorbs
> 326 J while warming from 25.0 °C to
> 58.0 °C. Find $c$, and identify the metal.
> 
> $$ c = \frac{q}{m\,\Delta T} = \frac{326}{(45.0)(33.0)}      = \mathbf{0.220~J/g\cdot^\circ C} $$
> 
> Compare: Ag 0.235, Cu 0.385, Fe 0.449. The value points to
> **silver** — 0.220 against 0.235 is ordinary experimental error,
> while the alternatives are far off.

## Ladder 4 • Calorimetry

`ZUM §6.2`

A calorimeter measures the *surroundings* and infers the system:

$$ q_{\text{reaction}} = -q_{\text{solution}}    = -m\,c\,\Delta T $$

That minus sign is the entire technique. The thermometer sits in the
water, so a temperature *rise* means the water gained heat, which
means the reaction *lost* it.

> ⚠️ **AP trap**
>
> Two habits that protect the answer. Use the mass of the
> **solution**, not of the solute — in a coffee-cup calorimeter the
> water is what warms. And convert to **kJ per mole** at the end:
> $q$ is the heat for the sample you used, while $\Delta H$ is quoted per
> mole.

> 📘 **I do: a coffee-cup neutralization**
>
> 50.0 mL of 1.00 M HCl is mixed with
> 50.0 mL of 1.00 M NaOH, both at
> 21.0 °C. The temperature rises to 27.7 °C. Take
> the solution's density as 1.00 g/mL and
> $c = 4.18$. Find $\Delta H$ per mole of water formed.
> 
> **Heat gained by the solution** — total mass 100.0 g:
> 
> $$ q_{\text{soln}} = (100.0)(4.18)(6.7) = 2801\,\mathrm{J} $$
> 
> **Flip the sign for the reaction:**
> $q_{\text{rxn}} = -2801\,\mathrm{J} = -2.80\,\mathrm{kJ}$
> 
> **Moles of water formed:**
> $(0.0500)(1.00) = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}$ — 1:1, and neither reagent is in
> excess.
> 
> **Per mole:**
> 
> $$ \Delta H = \frac{-2.80}{0.0500} = \mathbf{-56.0~kJ/mol} $$
> 
> The accepted value for strong acid $+$ strong base is about
> $-57.3$ kJ/mol, so the experiment is good to about 2%.

## Ladder 5 • Hess's law

`ZUM §6.3`

Enthalpy is a state function: it depends only on the initial and
final states, never on the route. So known reactions can be added like
algebra to reach an unknown one.

1. **Reverse** a step $\Rightarrow$ flip the sign of
   $\Delta H$.
2. **Multiply** a step $\Rightarrow$ multiply $\Delta H$ by the
   same factor.
3. Add the manipulated steps; species on opposite sides cancel.

Work *backward* from the target: put each target species on the side
where it belongs, and the manipulations reveal themselves.

> 📘 **I do: forming nitrogen dioxide in two steps**
>
> Target: N₂(g) + 2O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g), $\Delta H = ?$
> 
> Given:
> 
> $$ \begin{align*}   \text{(i)}\quad & \text{N₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO(g)}     & \Delta H_1 &= +180\,\mathrm{kJ}\\   \text{(ii)}\quad & \text{2NO₂(g) → 2NO(g) + O₂(g)}     & \Delta H_2 &= +112\,\mathrm{kJ} \end{align*} $$
> 
> **Step (i):** N₂ is already a reactant in the target —
> *use as written*, $+180$ kJ.
> 
> **Step (ii):** the target needs 2NO₂ as a *product*, but
> (ii) has it as a reactant — **reverse it**:
> 
> $$ \text{2NO(g) + O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g)} \qquad \Delta H = -112\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **Add, and watch 2NO cancel** (product of the first, reactant
> of the second):
> 
> $$ \text{N₂ + O₂ + O₂ → 2NO₂} \quad\text{i.e.}\quad    \text{N₂ + 2O₂ → 2NO₂} $$
> 
> $$ \Delta H = 180 + (-112) = \mathbf{+68~kJ} $$
> 
> The 2NO cancelling is the check that the manipulations were right:
> if an intermediate survives, something is wrong.

## Ladder 6 • Standard enthalpies of
formation

`ZUM §6.4`

$\Delta H_f^\circ$ is the enthalpy change forming **one mole** of a
compound from its elements in their standard states. Two consequences do
all the work:

- An **element in its standard state has
   $\Delta H_f^\circ = 0$** — O₂(g), C(graphite),
   Br₂(l), Na(s). Nothing needs to happen to make it.
- $$ \boxed{\;\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}}         = \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{products})         - \sum n\Delta H_f^\circ(\text{reactants})\;} $$

Products minus reactants — in that order. Reversing it flips the sign
of the answer, which is the commonest error on this calculation.

> 📘 **I do: burning ethanol**
>
> C₂H₅OH(l) + 3O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g) + 3H₂O(l)
> $\Delta H_f^\circ$ (kJ/mol): C₂H₅OH(l) $-277.7$,
> CO₂(g) $-393.5$, H₂O(l) $-285.8$, O₂(g) $0$.
> 
> **Products:**
> 
> $$ 2(-393.5) + 3(-285.8) = -787.0 + (-857.4) = -1644.4\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **Reactants** — and note the oxygen contributes nothing:
> 
> $$ (-277.7) + 3(0) = -277.7\,\mathrm{kJ} $$
> 
> **Subtract:**
> 
> $$ \Delta H^\circ = -1644.4 - (-277.7) = \mathbf{-1366.7~kJ} $$
> 
> **Sense check:** a combustion must be strongly exothermic, and it
> is. A positive answer here would mean the arithmetic ran
> reactants-minus-products.

## Ladder 7 • Energy sources and fuel values

`ZUM §6.5–6.6`

Sections 6.5–6.6 are descriptive, and the CED does not assess them —
read for context. The one quantitative idea worth carrying is
**fuel value**: energy released per *gram*, which is what
matters when a fuel must be carried.

> 📘 **I do: comparing two fuels by mass**
>
> Methane releases 890. kJ/mol
> ($M = 16.04\,\mathrm{g/mol}$); octane releases
> 5470 kJ/mol ($M = 114.2\,\mathrm{g/mol}$).
> Which is the better fuel per gram?
> 
> $$ \text{CH₄}: \frac{890.}{16.04} = \mathbf{55.5~kJ/g} \qquad    \text{C₈H₁₈}: \frac{5470}{114.2} = \mathbf{47.9~kJ/g} $$
> 
> **Methane wins per gram** — it is the most hydrogen-rich
> hydrocarbon, and hydrogen carries more energy per gram than carbon. Yet
> cars burn octane, because methane is a gas: per *litre of tank*,
> the liquid wins easily. Which figure of merit you use depends on what is
> scarce — mass or volume.
