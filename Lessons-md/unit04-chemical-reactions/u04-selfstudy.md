> 📌 **How to use these notes — read this first**
>
> Each skill is a **ladder**: a worked example, then **four**
> YOUR TURN questions — same skill, new numbers, no help. Work all four
> before checking anything.
> 
> **The habit that decides this unit.** Every quantitative question
> here runs the same three-step road: **to moles**, **through the
> balanced equation**, **back out**. Grams never talk to grams directly.
> If you find yourself comparing two masses, or two volumes, you have
> skipped the middle step.
> 
> **One scope note.** The CED excludes the terms “oxidizing agent”
> and “reducing agent”, and excludes memorizing solubility rules beyond a
> short list. You will be asked which species is *oxidized* and which
> is *reduced* — never to label an “agent”. Lewis acid–base theory
> is also excluded; Brønsted–Lowry is the model used throughout.

## Ladder 1 • Balancing equations

`CED 4.1`

Change **coefficients** only. Changing a subscript changes the
substance, so it is never allowed.

> 📘 **I do: a combustion**
>
> Balance C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O.
> 
> **Carbon first** (it appears in one place on each side): 3 carbons
> left, so 3 CO₂.
> 
> **Hydrogen next:** 8 hydrogens left, so 4 H₂O.
> 
> **Oxygen last** — it is in two products, so leave it until the rest
> is fixed. Right side now has $3(2) + 4(1) = 10$ oxygens, so 5 O₂.
> 
> C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
> 
> **Check every element:** C 3=3, H 8=8, O 10=10. ✓
> 
> **Why oxygen last:** balancing the element that appears in the most
> places first forces you to redo it every time something else changes.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 1 — four questions**
>
> 1. Balance Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃: 
>    *(working space)*

>    *(working space)*
> 3. Balance Al + HCl → AlCl₃ + H₂: 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. A student balances H₂ + O₂ → H₂O by writing H₂O₂.
>    Explain the error.

## Ladder 2 • Net ionic equations

`CED 4.2`

Write what actually changes. Strong electrolytes in solution are already
separated into ions; anything that appears unchanged on both sides is a
spectator and cancels.

> 📘 **I do: molecular to net ionic**
>
> AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq). Write the net ionic
> equation.
> 
> **Split the strong electrolytes.** AgNO₃, NaCl and
> NaNO₃ are all soluble and ionic, so all are separated. AgCl is
> a *solid* and stays written as one unit.
> Ag+ + NO₃⁻ + Na+ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) + Na+ + NO₃⁻
> 
> **Cancel the spectators:** Na+ and NO₃- appear unchanged on
> both sides.
> Ag+(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
> 
> **The rule for what stays together:** solids, liquids, gases and weak
> electrolytes are written as whole units. Only dissolved strong
> electrolytes get split.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 2 — four questions**
>
> 1. Net ionic for BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq):
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Net ionic for HNO₃(aq) + KOH(aq) → KNO₃(aq) + H₂O(l):
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which species stay written as whole units in a net ionic equation?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is the net ionic equation the same for *any* strong acid
>    with *any* strong hydroxide base?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄(s)    
> (b) H+ + OH⁻ → H₂O     (c) solids, liquids, gases, weak
> electrolytes     (d) only H+ and OH- actually react

## Ladder 3 • Physical versus chemical change

`CED 4.4`

A chemical change makes a new substance — new bonds, new identity. A
physical change rearranges what is already there.

> 📘 **I do: the hard case**
>
> Classify: (i) ice melting, (ii) iron rusting, (iii) NaCl dissolving.
> 
> **(i) Physical.** The H₂O molecules are unchanged; only the
> arrangement and the intermolecular forces between them change.
> 
> **(ii) Chemical.** Iron and oxygen form iron(III) oxide — a new
> substance with new bonds.
> 
> **(iii) Physical**, and this is the one that causes arguments. The
> ionic lattice is pulled apart and each ion is surrounded by water, so
> strong new ion–dipole interactions do form. But no new *substance*
> is made: the ions in solution are the same Na+ and Cl- that were
> in the crystal, and evaporating the water returns the original solid
> unchanged. Recoverability is the deciding test.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 3 — four questions**
>
> 1. Classify: sugar dissolving in tea. 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Classify: a candle burning. 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Classify: dry ice subliming. 
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Give one observation that suggests a chemical change has occurred,
>    and one reason it is not conclusive.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) physical     (b) chemical     (c) physical    
> (d) e.g. colour change; but dissolving a dye also changes colour

## Ladder 4 • Mole-to-mole stoichiometry

`CED 4.5`

The coefficients of a balanced equation are a *mole* ratio, never a
mass ratio. That ratio is the only bridge between one substance and
another.

> 📘 **I do: the three-step road**
>
> For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, how many grams of NH₃ form from
> 28.0 g of N₂, with hydrogen in excess?
> 
> **To moles:** $28.0/28.02 = 0.999$ mol N₂.
> 
> **Through the equation:** the ratio N₂:NH₃ is $1:2$, so
> $0.999 \times 2 = 2.00$ mol NH₃.
> 
> **Back out:** $2.00 \times 17.03 = \mathbf{34.0~g}$.
> 
> **The check that catches the commonest error:** mass is conserved
> overall, but it is *not* transferred one-for-one between species.
> 28.0 g of N₂ does not give 28.0 g of NH₃;
> the extra mass comes from the hydrogen.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 4 — four questions**
>
> 1. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, moles of H₂O from
>    0.500 mol O₂:
>    *(working space)*
> 2. For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, moles of H₂ needed for
>    0.400 mol N₂:
>    *(working space)*
> 3. For 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃, grams of Fe₂O₃ from
>    11.2 g Fe:
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why can the coefficients not be read as a mass ratio?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 1.00 mol     (b) 1.20 mol    
> (c) 16.0 g     (d) different substances have different molar
> masses

## Ladder 5 • Limiting reactant

`CED 4.5`

Convert both reactants to moles, then test them *against the
equation's ratio*. Comparing raw moles, or masses, is the standard way to
get this wrong.

> 📘 **I do: testing against the ratio**
>
> 28.0 g N₂ and 4.00 g H₂ react by
> N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Which is limiting, and what mass of NH₃
> forms?
> 
> **To moles:** N₂ $= 28.0/28.02 = 0.999$;
> H₂ $= 4.00/2.016 = 1.98$.
> 
> **Test against the ratio.** Consuming all the N₂ would require
> $3 \times 0.999 = 3.00$ mol of H₂ — but only 1.98 is
> available. So **hydrogen is limiting**.
> 
> Note that hydrogen is limiting despite there being *twice as many
> moles* of it. The ratio, not the raw count, decides.
> 
> **Yield from the limiting reactant only:**
> $n(\text{NH₃}) = \tfrac{2}{3}(1.984) = 1.32$ mol,
> so $m = 1.32 \times 17.03 = \mathbf{22.5~g}$.
> 
> **Excess left over:** N₂ used $= 1.984/3 = 0.661$ mol, so
> $0.999 - 0.661 = 0.338$ mol remains
> $= 9.47\,\mathrm{g}$.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 5 — four questions**
>
> 1. 0.300 mol H₂ with 0.200 mol O₂ by
>    2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Limiting?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. How many moles of water form in (a)? 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. How much of the excess reactant is left in (a)?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is it wrong to say “the reactant present in the smaller
>    amount is limiting”?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) H₂     (b) 0.300 mol     (c)
> 0.050 mol O₂     (d) the stoichiometric ratio decides

## Ladder 6 • Percent yield

`CED 4.5`

$$ \%\text{ yield} = \frac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}} \times 100 $$

The theoretical yield always comes from the **limiting** reactant.

> 📘 **I do: yield from the previous ladder**
>
> The ammonia synthesis above has a theoretical yield of
> 22.5 g. A run produces 18.0 g. Find the percent yield
> and give two reasons it is below 100 %.
> 
> $$ \frac{18.0}{22.5}\times100 = \mathbf{80.0\%} $$
> 
> **Reasons:** the reaction may not have gone to completion — ammonia
> synthesis is an equilibrium and does not consume all its reactants; product
> is lost during separation and purification; side reactions may consume
> reactants; or the product may not be fully dry when weighed.
> 
> **What a yield above 100 % means:** not a better
> reaction — an error. Almost always the product was still wet, or
> contained impurities.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 6 — four questions**
>
> 1. Theoretical 5.00 g, actual 4.10 g. Percent
>    yield?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. A 85.0 % yield on a theoretical 12.0 g.
>    Actual mass?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Which reactant's amount sets the theoretical yield?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. A student reports 105 %. What has most likely
>    happened?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 82.0 %     (b) 10.2 g    
> (c) the limiting reactant     (d) the product was wet or impure

## Ladder 7 • Solution stoichiometry

`CED 4.5`

Volume and concentration give moles: $n = MV$. From there it is the same
three-step road as always.

> 📘 **I do: two solutions, a precipitate**
>
> 30.0 mL of 0.200 M AgNO₃ is mixed with
> 25.0 mL of 0.150 M NaCl. What mass of
> AgCl forms?
> 
> **To moles:** $n(\text{Ag+}) = (0.0300)(0.200) = 0.00600$;
> $n(\text{Cl-}) = (0.0250)(0.150) = 0.00375$.
> 
> **Test the ratio:** the net ionic equation
> Ag+ + Cl⁻ → AgCl is $1:1$, so the smaller amount limits —
> **chloride**.
> 
> **Back out:** $n(\text{AgCl}) = 0.00375$ mol;
> $m = 0.00375 \times 143.32 = \mathbf{0.537~g}$.
> 
> **If asked for a leftover concentration:** excess Ag+ is
> $0.00600 - 0.00375 = 0.00225$ mol in the *combined*
> 55.0 mL, giving 0.0409 M. Always use the total
> volume.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 7 — four questions**
>
> 1. Moles in 45.0 mL of 0.250 M:
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Volume of 0.500 M containing 0.0300 mol:
>    *(working space)*
> 3. In the worked example, what is $[\text{NO₃-}]$ after mixing?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why must the *combined* volume be used for a leftover
>    concentration?
>    *(working space)*
>

## Ladder 8 • Titration

`CED 4.6`

At the equivalence point the moles of acid and base have reacted in
exactly the ratio the equation demands. That single sentence solves every
titration question.

> 📘 **I do: finding an unknown concentration**
>
> 25.00 mL of HCl requires 22.40 mL of
> 0.150 M NaOH to reach the endpoint. Find $[\text{HCl}]$.
> 
> **Moles of base:** $(0.02240)(0.150) = 0.00336$ mol.
> 
> **Through the ratio:** HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O is $1:1$, so
> $n(\text{HCl}) = 0.00336$ mol.
> 
> **Back out:** $[\text{HCl}] = 0.00336/0.02500 = \mathbf{0.134~M}$.
> 
> **Watch for a ratio that is not $1:1$.** With H₂SO₄, one mole of
> acid supplies two of H+, so it takes *twice* as much base —
> and forgetting that halves or doubles your answer.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 8 — four questions**
>
> 1. 20.00 mL of NaOH needs
>    18.60 mL of 0.200 M HCl.
>    $[\text{NaOH}]$?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. How many moles of NaOH neutralize 0.0100 mol of
>    H₂SO₄?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. What is true of the moles of H+ and OH- at the
>    equivalence point?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. A student overshoots the endpoint. Is the calculated concentration
>    too high or too low?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 0.186 M     (b) 0.0200 mol

## Ladder 9 • Recognizing reaction types

`CED 4.7`

Three families cover almost everything on this exam: precipitation
(two solutions, an insoluble solid), acid–base (proton transfer), and
redox (electron transfer, shown by changing oxidation numbers).

> 📘 **I do: classify and justify**
>
> Classify each: (i) Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃;
> (ii) HNO₃ + NaOH → NaNO₃ + H₂O;
> (iii) Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.
> 
> **(i) Precipitation.** Two aqueous solutions meet and an insoluble
> solid, PbI₂, forms.
> 
> **(ii) Acid–base.** A proton moves from the acid to the hydroxide;
> the net ionic equation is H+ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
> 
> **(iii) Redox.** Zinc goes from 0 to $+2$ (oxidized, loses
> electrons); copper goes from $+2$ to 0 (reduced, gains them). Changing
> oxidation numbers is the signature.
> 
> **The quick test for redox:** if any element's oxidation number
> changes, it is redox. If none changes, it is not.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 9 — four questions**
>
> 1. Classify AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃: 
>    *(working space)*
> 2. Classify 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Classify CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O:
>    *(working space)*
> 4. What single test distinguishes a redox reaction from the other
>    two?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) precipitation     (b) redox     (c) acid–base    
> (d) does any oxidation number change?

## Ladder 10 • Acid–base reactions

`CED 4.8`

Brønsted–Lowry: an acid donates a proton, a base accepts one. A
conjugate pair differs by exactly one H+.

> 📘 **I do: pairs and strength**
>
> Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in
> NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄+ + OH⁻, and explain why NH₃ is a weak base.
> 
> **Pairs.** NH₃ accepts a proton to become NH₄+: that is one
> pair, base and its conjugate acid. H₂O donates a proton to become
> OH-: that is the other, acid and its conjugate base.
> 
> **Weak, not strong.** At equilibrium most of the ammonia is still
> NH₃. Only a small fraction has accepted a proton, so the solution
> contains far fewer OH- ions than a strong base of the same
> concentration would give — which is why the equation is written with a
> double arrow.
> 
> **Strength is about extent, not concentration.** A concentrated
> solution of a weak base is entirely possible.

>

>    *(working space)*
> 2. Conjugate acid of HCO₃-: 
>    *(working space)*
> 3. Identify both pairs in HF + H₂O ⇌ F⁻ + H₃O+:
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Distinguish a *strong* acid from a *concentrated* acid.
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) NO₃-     (b) H₂CO₃     (c) HF/F-
> and H₃O+/H₂O     (d) extent of ionization vs amount per

## Ladder 11 • Oxidation numbers

`CED 4.9`

Rules in order of priority: free elements 0; monatomic ions equal their
charge; oxygen $-2$ (peroxides $-1$); hydrogen $+1$ (metal hydrides
$-1$); the sum equals the overall charge.

> 📘 **I do: three species**
>
> Find the oxidation number of the central atom in MnO₄-,
> Cr₂O₇²⁻ and H₂SO₄.
> 
> **MnO₄-:** four oxygens at $-2$ give $-8$; the total must be
> $-1$.
> 
> $$ x + (-8) = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+7} $$
> 
> **Cr₂O₇²⁻:** seven oxygens give $-14$; the total is $-2$; and
> there are **two** chromiums.
> 
> $$ 2x + (-14) = -2 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x = +12 \;\Rightarrow\;    x = \mathbf{+6} $$
> 
> **H₂SO₄:** two hydrogens give $+2$, four oxygens $-8$, total 0.
> 
> $$ 2 + x - 8 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \mathbf{+6} $$
> 
> **The slip to avoid:** forgetting to divide by the number of central
> atoms. In dichromate the answer is $+6$, not $+12$.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 11 — four questions**
>

>    *(working space)*

> 4. Oxidation number of O in H₂O₂, and why it is not $-2$:

## Ladder 12 • Balancing redox in acid

`CED 4.9`

Balance electrons first — the number lost must equal the number gained —
then use H+ and H₂O to finish. Check the *charge* as well
as the atoms.

> 📘 **I do: permanganate and iron**
>
> Balance
> MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ + H+ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ + H₂O.
> 
> **Electron bookkeeping.** Mn goes $+7 \to +2$: a gain of **5**.
> Fe goes $+2 \to +3$: a loss of **1**. To make these equal, multiply
> the iron by 5.
> 
> **Then balance O with water and H with H+.** Four oxygens on the
> left need 4 H₂O on the right, which needs 8 H+ on the left:
> MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H+ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O
> 
> **Charge check — do this every time.**
> Left: $(-1) + 5(+2) + 8(+1) = +17$. Right: $(+2) + 5(+3) = +17$.
> ✓
> 
> If the charges do not match, the equation is wrong even if every atom
> balances.

> ✏️ **YOUR TURN 12 — four questions**
>
> 1. How many electrons does each Cr gain going from Cr₂O₇²⁻ to
>    Cr³⁺?
>    *(working space)*
> 2. How many Fe²⁺ are needed per Cr₂O₇²⁻?
>    *(working space)*
> 3. In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, which species is oxidized?
>    *(working space)*
> 4. Why is a charge check necessary even when all atoms balance?
>    *(working space)*
> 
> > **check:** (a) 3 each, 6 total     (b) six     (c) Zn    
> (d) electrons must balance too
