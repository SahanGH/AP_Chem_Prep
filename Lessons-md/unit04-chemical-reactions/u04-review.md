## The five tools

| **Change classifier** | physical $=$ same substances, IMFs only;
  chemical $=$ new substances, bonds made/broken. Dissolving a salt is
  physical; dissolving HCl(g) is chemical (it ionizes) |
|---|---|
| **Particulate rules** | conserve atoms AND charge; soluble salts drawn
  as separated ions; precipitate drawn as a cluster; excess reactant stays
  in the picture |
| **Net ionic recipe** | split strong electrolytes only (soluble salts,
  strong acids, strong bases); keep solids, liquids, gases, weak
  electrolytes whole; cancel spectators |
| **Reaction types** | precipitation (insoluble product), acid–base
  (proton transfer), redox (oxidation states change) |
| **Solution stoich** | volume $\times$ molarity $\to$ moles $\to$
  ratio $\to$ moles $\to$ mass or volume. Check limiting reactant using the
  coefficients, not raw moles |

## Solubility, the working set

- **Always soluble:** NO₃-, alkali metal cations,
   NH₄+. Check these first — they settle most questions.
- **Usually soluble:** Cl-/Br-/I- except with
   Ag+, Pb²⁺; SO₄²⁻ except with Ba²⁺,
   Pb²⁺, Ca²⁺.
- **Usually insoluble:** CO₃²⁻, PO₄³⁻, S²⁻,
   OH- — unless the cation is on the always-soluble list.

## Redox in four lines

- Assign states: element 0; O is $-2$; H is $+1$; sum to the charge.
- **OIL RIG** — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.
- Oxidized $\to$ *reducing* agent. Reduced $\to$
   *oxidizing* agent. (The labels name what it does to the other
   species.)
- Free elements on both sides is a reliable redox tell.

## The traps, one line each

- Splitting a weak acid, a weak base, or water in a net ionic
   equation.
- Drawing soluble salts as bonded pairs, or the precipitate as free
   ions.
- Losing atoms between “before” and “after” boxes, or deleting the
   excess reactant.
- Naming the oxidizing agent when you mean the reducing agent.
- Using a 1:1 titration ratio for a diprotic acid.
- Dividing excess-reactant moles by one solution's volume instead of
   the *total* volume.
- Treating a colour change alone as proof of reaction.
